# Free T3 (Free Triiodothyronine)

## Metadata
- category: blood
- unit: pg/mL (×1.536 → pmol/L; US labs report pg/mL, most European labs report pmol/L)
- source: lab
- confidence: established
- review_cadence: per-lab-panel
- last_verified: 2026-06-19

## Target Range
- adult (immunoassay): ~2.3–4.2 pg/mL (~3.5–6.5 pmol/L); assay- and method-dependent — NOT freely transferable across platforms; picomolar concentration makes fT3 the least precise of the routine thyroid panel; total T3 sometimes preferred for hyperthyroid workup
- note: every lab establishes its own interval from its own reference population; intervals differ materially across manufacturers (Abbott ARCHITECT, Roche Cobas, DiaSorin LIAISON all produce divergent ranges from the same population)
- diagnostic role: primarily **hyperthyroidism confirmation and T3-toxicosis identification** (elevated fT3, normal fT4, suppressed TSH); T3-toxicosis accounts for ~5% of thyrotoxicosis cases and may precede fT4 rise in early Graves' disease or toxic nodule
- NOT for hypothyroidism diagnosis or monitoring: fT3 is preserved until late-stage disease due to compensatory D2 upregulation; ATA 2014 guidelines do not recommend fT3 for hypothyroid diagnosis or LT4 dose adjustment
- low fT3 in illness: common (60–70% of critically ill patients); reflects non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS / low-T3 syndrome), not primary thyroid failure — TSH remains normal or only mildly altered; low fT3 predicts mortality in cardiac cohorts but is a severity marker, not a treatment target
- source of target: [[library/biomarkers/free-t3/research-report]] + ATA 2016 hyperthyroidism guidelines (Ross et al.) + ATA 2014 hypothyroidism guidelines (Jonklaas et al.)

## Affected By
- RAISES fT3 (physiological/pathological): hyperthyroidism (Graves' disease, toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma); exogenous T3 / liothyronine / desiccated thyroid extract; T3-toxicosis (early or relapsing Graves', autonomous toxic nodule); recovery phase of non-thyroidal illness (rebound deiodination); hCG stimulation (gestational thyrotoxicosis, hyperemesis gravidarum)
- RAISES fT3 (assay artifact): high-dose biotin ≥10 mg/day on streptavidin-based platforms (Roche, Beckman) — falsely elevated fT3; stop biotin ≥48 hours before testing; anti-T3 autoantibodies on one-step competitive immunoassays (falsely elevated; two-step assays give opposite direction); heterophile / anti-streptavidin / anti-ruthenium antibodies; furosemide (>80 mg/day), salicylates, phenytoin, heparin (in vitro artifact) — all displace T3 from binding proteins
- LOWERS fT3 (physiological/pathological): late / severe primary hypothyroidism; non-thyroidal illness / NTIS (any acute or chronic illness via D1 downregulation and D3 upregulation); caloric restriction and fasting (D1 inhibition — adaptive metabolic response; 53% T3 drop with total fasting, normalized with ≥50 g/day carbohydrate)
- LOWERS fT3 (drug — D1/D2 inhibition): propylthiouracil (PTU); amiodarone (strong D1 inhibitor, also raises rT3); glucocorticoids (suppress TRH/TSH + D1); propranolol (inhibits peripheral T3 production beyond beta-blockade); iodinated contrast agents (iopanoic acid, sodium ipodate)
- note: picomolar assay imprecision + NTI confounding limit fT3 interpretability in sick or hospitalized patients; always pair with TSH and fT4 for context

## Why It Matters
Free T3 is the biologically active thyroid hormone — the form that occupies nuclear receptors TRα and TRβ and drives nearly all classical thyroid effects. About 83% of circulating T3 is generated by peripheral deiodination of T4 (not direct thyroidal secretion), making serum fT3 sensitive to deiodinase status as well as thyroid output. Its main clinical value is in confirming and grading hyperthyroidism and, critically, catching T3-toxicosis — the pattern of elevated fT3 with normal fT4 and suppressed TSH that marks early Graves' disease or autonomous toxic nodules. Low fT3 in acute or chronic illness (non-thyroidal illness syndrome) is common and strongly predicts mortality in cardiac patients, but current evidence does not support treating it — it is a severity marker, not a therapeutic target. In hypothyroidism, fT3 adds little: peripheral D2 compensates until late disease, making TSH and fT4 the relevant markers. The ongoing LT4+LT3 combination-therapy debate centers on whether patients with persistently low-normal fT3 on levothyroxine monotherapy (particularly DIO2 Thr92Ala carriers) might benefit from added liothyronine, though RCT evidence has been largely neutral.
