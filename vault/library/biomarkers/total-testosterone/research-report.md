# Total Testosterone: Canonical Research Report

## Summary

Total testosterone is a serum measurement that captures the aggregate of all three molecular forms in which testosterone circulates: tightly SHBG-bound (~44%, biologically inert), loosely albumin-bound (~50%, bioavailable), and free (~2%, immediately active). Because the assay sums all three pools indiscriminately, a single number can be an accurate reflection of androgen status or a misleading artifact — depending on the SHBG concentration, the time of day the specimen was drawn, the laboratory method used, and the patient population being assessed.

The harmonized adult male reference range — derived from 9,054 community-dwelling non-obese men aged 19–39 across four cohort studies calibrated against the CDC LC-MS/MS reference measurement procedure — is **264–916 ng/dL (9.2–31.8 nmol/L)** (2.5th–97.5th percentile) [9, cohort]. The median is 531 ng/dL (18.4 nmol/L); the 5th percentile is 303 ng/dL (10.5 nmol/L). The conversion factor between reporting units is exact: **1 ng/dL × 0.0347 = nmol/L**. In practice, US clinical labs report in ng/dL; international literature uses nmol/L.

The widely cited 300 ng/dL (10.4 nmol/L) hypogonadism landmark is a reasonable clinical anchor but is explicitly not a bright diagnostic threshold. The 2018 Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline requires two AM fasting specimens on separate days, both below the lower limit of normal, combined with symptoms — because diurnal variation alone causes a 30–35% drop from morning peak to afternoon nadir, biological variability adds a within-person day-to-day coefficient of variation of 10–15%, and assay imprecision compounds both [10, regulatory].

Female total testosterone is substantially lower — approximately 15–70 ng/dL (0.5–2.4 nmol/L) — and immunoassay measurement is generally unreliable at these concentrations; LC-MS/MS is the required method [11, regulatory].

AM sampling (08:00–10:00) is mandatory because testosterone peaks between 07:00–07:30 and falls ≥43% by early evening in healthy men [5, mechanism_review]. LC-MS/MS is the preferred measurement method and the anchor for all published reference ranges; immunoassays introduce systematic bias at low concentrations, and the CDC Hormone Standardization (HoSt) Program is the ongoing certification mechanism for laboratory performance [18, regulatory]. When SHBG is abnormal — a common situation in obesity, metabolic disease, aging, thyroid dysfunction, and many medications — total testosterone must be interpreted alongside free or calculated free testosterone to avoid misclassification in either direction.

## Physiology & What Total Testosterone Measures

### Testosterone Biosynthesis and the HPG Axis

Testosterone is the primary circulating androgen in men and is synthesized predominantly (approximately 95%) in the **Leydig cells** of the testicular interstitium. The signal cascade begins in the hypothalamus, where specialized neurons release **gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH)** in discrete pulses — typically every 90–120 minutes in adult men. GnRH travels via the hypothalamic-pituitary portal blood to the anterior pituitary, where it binds to gonadotroph receptors and triggers the pulsatile release of **luteinizing hormone (LH)** and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) [1, mechanism_review].

LH is the primary driver of Leydig cell steroidogenesis. Upon binding to LH receptors on Leydig cells, cyclic AMP (cAMP) is generated as a second messenger, rapidly upregulating the steroidogenic acute regulatory (StAR) protein. StAR facilitates translocation of cholesterol from the outer to the inner mitochondrial membrane — the rate-limiting step in steroid hormone synthesis [2, mechanism_review]. Cholesterol is then converted to pregnenolone by the CYP11A1 (P450scc) enzyme at the inner mitochondrial membrane. Subsequent enzymatic steps involving HSD3B2, CYP17A1, and HSD17B convert pregnenolone through a series of intermediates to yield **testosterone**, which is secreted into the testicular venous blood and lymphatics [2, mechanism_review].

The HPG axis operates under a classic **long-loop negative feedback** mechanism. Rising serum testosterone suppresses both hypothalamic GnRH pulse frequency and pituitary sensitivity to GnRH, dampening further LH release. Estradiol — produced by peripheral aromatization of testosterone — provides additional and potent negative feedback, acting at both the hypothalamus and the anterior pituitary to reduce gonadotropin secretion [1, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review]. FSH acts primarily on Sertoli cells to support spermatogenesis, while inhibin B, secreted by Sertoli cells, selectively suppresses FSH in a short feedback loop. This architecture creates a homeostatic set-point that keeps serum testosterone within the adult male reference range under normal conditions.

### What "Total Testosterone" Measures: The Three Circulating Pools

A serum "total testosterone" assay measures the **sum of all three molecular pools** in which testosterone circulates, and understanding these pools is essential for interpreting any clinical result [4, mechanism_review]:

1. **SHBG-bound (~44%, tightly bound, NOT bioavailable):** The majority of circulating testosterone is bound with high affinity to sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), a hepatically synthesized glycoprotein. This fraction is biologically inert for most practical purposes — SHBG-bound testosterone cannot diffuse freely into target cells, and its tight association constant means it remains essentially sequestered during capillary transit [4, mechanism_review].

2. **Albumin-bound (~50%, loosely bound, bioavailable):** Roughly half of circulating testosterone associates weakly with albumin. Due to albumin's low binding affinity and the relatively short capillary transit time, this fraction dissociates readily and is accessible to tissues. Albumin-bound testosterone is therefore considered **bioavailable** [4, mechanism_review].

3. **Free (~2%, immediately active):** A small but physiologically critical fraction circulates unbound. Free testosterone can diffuse directly across cell membranes to bind intracellular androgen receptors and drive gene transcription. It represents the most immediately bioactive form [4, mechanism_review].

The **free-hormone hypothesis** holds that free plus albumin-bound testosterone constitutes the "bioavailable" fraction (approximately 52% of total). This bioavailable pool correlates more closely with androgen-sensitive clinical endpoints (bone mineral density, muscle mass, libido) than total testosterone alone in many studies, though total testosterone remains the recommended first-line diagnostic test due to assay standardization and cost [4, mechanism_review; 5, mechanism_review]. Clinicians should note that SHBG levels are influenced by many factors (obesity, insulin, thyroid disease, aging, medications), which can cause total testosterone to diverge substantially from the bioavailable fraction — particularly in older men and in women.

### Diurnal Rhythm and the Need for AM Sampling

Testosterone secretion follows a pronounced **circadian rhythm** driven by pulsatile LH release. In healthy young to middle-aged men, serum total testosterone peaks between **7:00 and 7:30 AM**, with values declining by at least 43% from peak to nadir by early evening [5, mechanism_review]. Free and bioavailable testosterone follow the same morning acrophase pattern. SHBG, by contrast, reaches its daily high in the mid-afternoon — partially buffering the circulating total, though the decline in free and bioavailable fractions through the day is still clinically meaningful.

This rhythm is the reason clinical guidelines (including the Endocrine Society) recommend drawing testosterone between **8:00 and 10:00 AM** for diagnostic evaluation. Samples drawn in the afternoon may yield values 20–35% lower than morning levels in the same individual, leading to false-positive diagnoses of hypogonadism. The diurnal amplitude is preserved in fit, healthy men into the seventh decade, though it may be partially attenuated in older or metabolically unwell individuals [5, mechanism_review; 20, mechanism_review].

### Peripheral Conversions: DHT and Estradiol

Testosterone is not merely a terminal effector — it serves as a **prohormone** for two distinct active metabolites produced in peripheral tissues:

- **Dihydrotestosterone (DHT):** The enzyme **5α-reductase** (types 1, 2, and 3) converts testosterone to DHT in androgen-sensitive tissues including the prostate, seminal vesicles, skin, hair follicles, and skeletal muscle. DHT binds the androgen receptor with approximately three times the affinity of testosterone itself, making it the dominant intratissue androgen in these organs [6, mechanism_review]. DHT cannot be aromatized to estradiol, so it remains a pure androgen signal. In men, DHT mediates virilization of the external genitalia, prostate growth, and scalp hair loss; 5α-reductase inhibitors (finasteride, dutasteride) reduce circulating DHT substantially while leaving total testosterone relatively intact.

- **Estradiol (E2):** The enzyme **aromatase** (CYP19A1), expressed in adipose tissue, liver, bone, brain, and gonads, converts testosterone to estradiol. In men, estradiol plays essential roles in regulating libido, bone mineral density, and hypothalamic feedback; loss of estradiol signaling (in men with aromatase mutations or CYP19A1 deficiency) produces osteoporosis and metabolic derangements despite normal testosterone levels. Rising adiposity increases aromatase activity, shifting the testosterone-to-estradiol ratio and contributing to secondary hypogonadism in obese men [3, mechanism_review].

### Testosterone in Women

Women produce testosterone at levels approximately 10- to 20-fold lower than men. Sources are roughly distributed as: **one quarter from the ovaries**, **one quarter from the adrenal glands**, and **approximately one half from peripheral conversion** of precursors (androstenedione, DHEA, DHEA-sulfate) in adipose and other tissues [7, mechanism_review]. Median serum total testosterone in reproductive-age women is approximately **0.34 nmol/L (approximately 10 ng/dL)**, with a wide reference interval of 0.04–1.01 nmol/L [7, mechanism_review]. Levels fluctuate across the menstrual cycle, peaking modestly at midcycle. In women, testosterone contributes to libido, bone density, muscle maintenance, and mood, and its assay interpretation is complicated by the fact that most immunoassays lack sufficient sensitivity at female concentration ranges.

### Age-Related Decline in Men

After approximately age 30–40, serum total testosterone in men declines at a rate of roughly **1% per year** [8, mechanism_review]. The decline reflects a dual deficit: reduced Leydig cell capacity to synthesize testosterone in response to LH stimulation, and age-related attenuation of hypothalamic GnRH pulse amplitude and frequency [8, mechanism_review]. Bioavailable and free testosterone decline more steeply — at approximately 2–3% annually — because SHBG concentrations rise with age, sequestering a larger fraction of the declining total pool. By the eighth decade, approximately 30% of men have total testosterone values in the hypogonadal range, and up to 50% have low free testosterone values. This progressive androgen decline underpins the clinical syndrome of late-onset hypogonadism (LOH) but should be distinguished from pathological primary or secondary hypogonadism, which may present at any age.

## Reference Ranges, Units & Thresholds

### The Harmonized Reference Range

For decades, clinical laboratories reported total testosterone using their own internally derived reference intervals, calibrated against different standards with different immunoassay platforms. The result was substantial interlaboratory disagreement: one demonstration showed 1,133 laboratories measuring the same hypogonadal sample produced values ranging from 45 to 365 ng/dL — a nearly eightfold spread [13, regulatory]. This fragmentation made cross-study comparisons unreliable and left clinicians without a defensible normal range.

The 2017 Travison harmonization study resolved this [9, cohort]. Using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) at the CDC's reference laboratory as the calibration anchor, researchers re-measured archived samples from four major population cohorts — the Framingham Heart Study (US), the European Male Aging Study (8 European centers), the Osteoporotic Fractures in Men Study (US), and the Sibling Study of Osteoporosis (Belgium) — totaling 9,054 community-dwelling men. Passing-Bablok regression translated each cohort's local assay values to the CDC standard, then pooled percentile curves were derived via constrained quantile regression.

**The harmonized reference range for healthy, non-obese men aged 19–39 years is 264–916 ng/dL (9.2–31.8 nmol/L).** This represents the 2.5th–97.5th percentile interval. The 5th percentile (often used as a more conservative lower cutoff) is 303 ng/dL (10.5 nmol/L); the median is 531 ng/dL (18.4 nmol/L) [9, cohort]. These figures replace the lab-to-lab idiosyncratic ranges that preceded them and are now the reference standard endorsed by the Endocrine Society.

### Units and Conversion

Total testosterone is reported in two unit systems. In the United States, ng/dL (nanograms per deciliter) predominates clinically. International literature uses nmol/L (nanomoles per liter). The conversion factor is:

> **1 ng/dL = 0.0347 nmol/L** (or equivalently, 1 nmol/L = 28.8 ng/dL)

| ng/dL | nmol/L |
|-------|--------|
| 264 | 9.2 |
| 300 | 10.4 |
| 531 | 18.4 (median) |
| 600 | 20.8 |
| 916 | 31.8 |

When reading a lab report, confirm which unit the reference range is expressed in — an unchecked unit swap is a common source of misinterpretation.

### The Hypogonadism Threshold: 300 ng/dL Is a Landmark, Not a Bright Line

A value of approximately 300 ng/dL (10.4 nmol/L) circulates widely in clinical practice as the lower diagnostic threshold for male hypogonadism. This is a reasonable clinical landmark, sitting close to the harmonized 2.5th percentile, but the 2018 Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline makes the diagnostic framework explicit [10, regulatory]:

**Diagnosis requires two conditions simultaneously:**

1. **Consistently low testosterone confirmed on ≥2 separate early-morning (AM) fasting samples.** The guideline specifies fasting, morning specimens because of testosterone's pronounced diurnal rhythm: levels peak between 06:00–08:00 and fall approximately 30–35% by afternoon in younger men [12, cohort]. A single sub-threshold afternoon draw does not establish hypogonadism.

2. **Clinical symptoms and signs consistent with testosterone deficiency.** These include reduced libido, erectile dysfunction, decreased energy, depressed mood, reduced muscle mass, increased body fat, and reduced bone density. An isolated low laboratory value in an asymptomatic man does not, by itself, warrant a diagnosis or treatment.

The Endocrine Society guideline specifically notes that roughly 30% of men who show a hypogonadal-range value on a first measurement will be within the normal range on a repeat sample. This biological variability — driven by pulsatile luteinizing hormone secretion, stress, illness, sleep disruption, and assay imprecision — is why serial morning sampling is mandatory, not optional. Testosterone exists on a biological continuum; there is no sharp metabolic threshold at which tissue responsiveness abruptly switches. The 300 ng/dL convention encodes probability of deficiency, not biochemical certainty.

### Female Reference Range

Total testosterone in women is substantially lower than in men, reflecting ovarian and adrenal contributions that are far smaller than testicular output. Reference ranges in healthy premenopausal adult women are approximately **15–70 ng/dL (0.5–2.4 nmol/L)** by LC-MS/MS-based assays, though published ranges vary by age group, menstrual cycle phase, and measurement method [11, regulatory].

The International Society for the Study of Women's Sexual Health (ISSWSH) notes that establishing a single universal female reference range is not currently possible, given that conventional immunoassays lack the sensitivity and specificity to reliably measure testosterone at female physiological concentrations (generally below 100 ng/dL) [11, regulatory]. LC-MS/MS is the preferred analytical method for female samples. Premenopausal concentrations decline with age even before menopause; values in women in their 40s are roughly half those of women in their 20s [11, regulatory].

### Age-Related Decline and Age-Adjusted Ranges

Total testosterone declines with age in men. The Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging, which followed 702 men with up to 10 serial measurements each, quantified this as approximately 1.6% per year across all age groups from the 30s onward — roughly 16% per decade [14, cohort]. By age 70, a substantial proportion of men who had normal levels at 40 will have drifted below the young-adult reference range.

This raises a clinically contested question: should age-adjusted reference intervals be used, making a 70-year-old man's 250 ng/dL "normal for his age"? The Endocrine Society guideline declines to endorse age-specific lower limits, citing two concerns [10, regulatory]:

- The cross-sectional data underlying age-specific ranges conflate aging with accumulating comorbidities (obesity, sleep apnea, metabolic syndrome) that suppress testosterone independently of age.
- Age-adjusted ranges risk normalizing levels at which older men still experience symptoms and adverse outcomes.

The practical consensus is that the harmonized 264 ng/dL lower bound remains the reference for all adult men, with age as clinical context rather than a numerical correction applied to the threshold.

### Assay Variation and Why a Single Low Value Is Not Diagnostic

Immunoassays — the workhorses of high-throughput clinical laboratories — have well-documented limitations at testosterone concentrations below 200–300 ng/dL: cross-reactivity with other steroids, variable calibration, and manufacturer-specific differences in antibody specificity [13, regulatory]. Even when two laboratories run the same platform, their reported values on the same sample can differ by 20–30%. The Endocrine Society position statement on testosterone measurement catalogues these limitations and calls for LC-MS/MS as the gold standard whenever diagnostic precision is required [13, regulatory].

Taken together, three forces explain why a single low testosterone value cannot establish hypogonadism: (1) diurnal rhythm — a single specimen drawn outside the reference collection window will be spuriously low; (2) day-to-day biological variability of 10–15% within an individual; and (3) assay imprecision that adds measurement error on top of biological variance. Only a pattern of low values on properly timed, replicated draws, paired with symptoms, supports the diagnosis.

## Measurement & Standardization

### The Immunoassay Problem

Serum total testosterone is among the most commonly ordered endocrine tests, yet for decades the dominant measurement technology — direct immunoassay — has carried a well-characterized accuracy failure at low concentrations. Immunoassays rely on competitive antibody binding: exogenous labeled testosterone competes with the patient's testosterone for a limited number of antibody binding sites. At low concentrations (roughly below 3.47 nmol/L / 100 ng/dL), the signal-to-noise ratio degrades and cross-reactivity with structurally similar steroids — dihydrotestosterone (DHT), androstenedione, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S), and other androgens — becomes proportionally meaningful [13, regulatory; 15, mechanism_review]. Because all immunoassays broadly overestimate testosterone at low concentrations, the populations most vulnerable are women (typical range 0.35–2.5 nmol/L / 10–70 ng/dL), prepubertal children, and hypogonadal men who fall below 100 ng/dL — precisely the groups where diagnostic accuracy matters most.

A 2007 Endocrine Society position statement benchmarked seven commercial immunoassays against isotope-dilution gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (ID/GC-MS) and found that below approximately 8.0 nmol/L (230 ng/dL), methods disagreed by up to 5-fold, with immunoassays uniformly overestimating testosterone concentration in women [13, regulatory]. A large proficiency survey involving 142 certified clinical laboratories using 16 immunoassays showed biases as high as 73.1% relative to the reference method at low concentrations [16, cohort]. The failure is not manufacturer-specific; it is intrinsic to the immunoassay architecture.

A further structural weakness of first-generation direct immunoassays is their incomplete release of testosterone from SHBG. Before antibody competition, testosterone must be displaced from its carrier proteins — primarily SHBG (high affinity) and albumin (lower affinity, high capacity). First-generation immunoassays use displacement reagents that incompletely strip testosterone from SHBG. The consequence is systematic error that scales with SHBG concentration: patients with high SHBG (elderly men, women on oral contraceptives, hyperthyroid individuals) are systematically under-reported, while patients with low SHBG (obese individuals, insulin-resistant men) are over-reported [16, cohort].

### LC-MS/MS: The Reference Method

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) is the reference and preferred method for serum total testosterone measurement, particularly at low concentrations. The technique physically separates testosterone from other steroids via liquid chromatography before identification by mass-to-charge ratio in tandem mass spectrometry — a process that is inherently agnostic to antibody cross-reactivity. When Moal et al. (2007) compared LC-MS/MS against five immunoassay techniques in 70 women and children, all five immunoassays overestimated testosterone relative to the LC-MS/MS reference, with correlation coefficients between 0.77 and 0.87 and slopes indicating positive bias across the board [15, mechanism_review].

The isotope dilution variant — isotope dilution LC-MS/MS (ID-LC-MS/MS) — uses a stable isotope-labeled internal standard (e.g., deuterated testosterone-d3) added to each sample before extraction, compensating for matrix effects and process losses. Thienpont et al. (2008) established the state-of-the-art for this approach in a 14-laboratory comparison against the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) reference measurement procedure (RMP), demonstrating that overall imprecision versus the RMP was below 15% at concentrations above 1.53 nmol/L (44 ng/dL) and below 34% at 0.3 nmol/L (8.65 ng/dL) — substantially better performance than immunoassay at all concentration strata [17, mechanism_review]. The CDC subsequently adopted an isotope dilution LC-MS/MS procedure as its own RMP, published in Clinica Chimica Acta by Wang et al. (2014), achieving total CVs of 3.7–4.8% across the concentration range and mean bias not significantly different from reference over two years of operation [18, regulatory].

### The CDC Hormone Standardization (HoSt) Program

Recognizing that assay variation was a clinical and public-health problem — not merely an analytical nuisance — a coalition of professional societies, government agencies, and industry convened to address it. The 2010 consensus statement by Rosner and Vesper et al., published in JCEM, established specific technical recommendations and launched what became the CDC Hormone Standardization (HoSt) Program for total testosterone [19, regulatory]. The Endocrine Society's 2018 clinical practice guideline adopted the HoSt-harmonized lower limit of normal in healthy nonobese young men — 264 ng/dL (9.2 nmol/L) — explicitly noting that for laboratories not HoSt-certified, reference ranges may vary considerably and may not accurately identify hypogonadism [10, regulatory].

The HoSt Program works by providing participating laboratories with a set of well-characterized reference sera traceable to the NIST ID-LC-MS/MS RMP. Laboratories submit results; performance criterion is ±6.4% mean bias versus the reference measurement procedure over the concentration range of 2.50–1,000 ng/dL. Certification requires two consecutive successful phases and is valid for one year, enforcing ongoing assay performance surveillance.

The program's measurable impact on the field: between 2007 and 2011, participation in HoSt activities reduced inter-laboratory measurement bias among mass spectrometry methods by approximately 50% [17, mechanism_review]. A 2016 retrospective analysis of accuracy-based survey data nonetheless found that commonly used immunoassays still exhibited unacceptable biases at low concentrations, and that the Beckman testosterone immunoassay demonstrated an R² of 0.16 versus the CDC RMP in the 7.4–37.6 ng/dL range — statistically indistinguishable from no correlation [16, cohort]. The Partnership for the Accurate Testing of Hormones (PATH), a collaboration between the Endocrine Society and the CDC, has pushed for all clinical testosterone assays to achieve HoSt certification.

### Analytical Interferences

Immunoassays carry several interference vulnerabilities that LC-MS/MS avoids.

**Biotin (vitamin B7).** Many immunoassay platforms use biotin-streptavidin capture chemistry. High-dose biotin supplementation (≥5 mg/day, common in hair/nail supplements) floods the streptavidin binding sites, blocking capture of biotinylated reagents. In competitive immunoassays this mechanism drives results falsely high; in sandwich immunoassays, falsely low. The FDA issued a safety communication on this interference in November 2017. Clinical guidance: testosterone should not be drawn until at least 8 hours (standard doses) or 72 hours (mega-doses) after the last biotin ingestion. LC-MS/MS has no biotin susceptibility.

**Heterophile and anti-animal antibodies.** Patients exposed to animal sera (veterinary workers, users of certain biologic therapies) may develop heterophile antibodies that cross-link immunoassay antibodies and produce artifactually elevated results. Case reports document falsely high testosterone values in women that resolved to the low-normal range on LC-MS/MS confirmation [22, open_label].

**DHT and androgen cross-reactivity.** Dihydrotestosterone (5α-reduced form of testosterone) shares high structural similarity and can cross-react with anti-testosterone antibodies in immunoassays. This is clinically relevant in individuals using exogenous DHT preparations. Anabolic-androgenic steroids with testosterone-like structure also vary in their immunoassay cross-reactivity.

### Pre-analytical Considerations

**Diurnal variation and timing.** Total testosterone follows a circadian rhythm driven by pulsatile LH secretion. In healthy young men, morning (08:00–10:00) concentrations are 15–20% higher than evening nadir values, with individual variation as large as 50% [20, mechanism_review]. The diurnal amplitude is blunted with advancing age and in men with primary testicular failure. Because reference ranges are established on morning samples, blood should be drawn between 08:00 and 10:00. The Endocrine Society 2018 guideline recommends two separate morning draws on different days before a diagnosis of hypogonadism is made [10, regulatory].

**Fasting status.** A glucose load acutely suppresses testosterone — oral glucose (75 g) has been shown to decrease serum testosterone by 25–30% within two hours in healthy men, likely via insulin-mediated suppression of LH pulsatility. Fasting samples are therefore preferred for diagnostic accuracy.

**Acute illness.** Intercurrent illness, surgery, and any acute physiological stress suppress hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal signaling; testosterone measurements during acute illness reflect the stress state, not the baseline androgen milieu, and should be deferred.

**Intra-individual biological variability.** Independent of diurnal rhythm, total testosterone carries a within-person coefficient of variation of approximately 15–20% across repeated measurements over weeks, even under controlled conditions. This variability is a principal reason clinical guidelines require two confirmatory measurements before treatment decisions.

### SHBG-Driven Discordance and Free Testosterone

Approximately 44% of circulating testosterone is tightly bound to SHBG (dissociation constant ~10⁹ L/mol), 54% is loosely bound to albumin (dissociation constant ~3×10⁴ L/mol), and only 1–3% circulates as free (unbound) testosterone. Biologically active testosterone is the sum of free and albumin-bound fractions (often called "bioavailable testosterone"), because albumin-bound testosterone dissociates readily at the capillary-tissue interface.

When SHBG is abnormal — which is common — total testosterone misrepresents the androgen milieu. **Conditions lowering SHBG** (obesity, type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance, hypothyroidism, androgen use, nephrotic syndrome, glucocorticoid excess) reduce total testosterone while free testosterone may remain in the normal range, leading to unnecessary treatment initiation if only total testosterone is reviewed. **Conditions raising SHBG** (aging, hyperthyroidism, cirrhosis, estrogen/oral contraceptive use, HIV, some anticonvulsants) can elevate total testosterone into the normal or high-normal range even when free testosterone is low, masking androgen deficiency [10, regulatory].

The Endocrine Society explicitly recommends measuring free or bioavailable testosterone in men with conditions known to alter SHBG [10, regulatory]. The gold standard measurement — equilibrium dialysis — is time-consuming, expensive, and limited to specialized laboratories. The practical alternative endorsed by clinical guidelines is **calculated free testosterone (cFT)** using the Vermeulen equation, which applies experimentally determined binding constants for testosterone's distribution among SHBG (Kd ~10⁹ L/mol) and albumin (Kd ~3.6×10⁴ L/mol) and the law of mass action. Vermeulen et al. (1999) validated this approach against apparent free testosterone by equilibrium dialysis across a wide range of SHBG concentrations — from low (hirsute women) to very high (hyperthyroid patients) — finding close agreement except in pregnancy, where estradiol occupies SHBG binding sites and causes the calculation to underestimate free testosterone [21, mechanism_review]. The calculation requires total testosterone (preferably by LC-MS/MS), SHBG, and albumin (or an assumed value of 4.3 g/dL). Because the Vermeulen cFT directly inherits all error in the total testosterone measurement, use of a non-standardized immunoassay for total testosterone substantially degrades the reliability of the calculated free testosterone. Direct analog-based free testosterone immunoassays — an older and simpler approach — are explicitly condemned by the Endocrine Society as inaccurate and should not be used [10, regulatory].

## Determinants & Clinical Significance

### Hypogonadism Classification: Localizing the Defect

When total testosterone is low, the first clinical task is to localize the defect using the gonadotropins LH and FSH. Two anatomically distinct failure modes produce the same low-T result but carry different diagnoses, prognoses, and treatment implications.

**Primary hypogonadism** (hypergonadotropic): the testes fail to produce testosterone despite adequate stimulation. The pituitary, sensing low androgen feedback, increases LH and FSH output — so primary hypogonadism presents with low T and HIGH LH/FSH. Common causes include Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), orchitis (viral or autoimmune), chemotherapy or radiation injury, surgical orchiectomy, and anorchia. The elevated gonadotropins are the diagnostic fingerprint [1, mechanism_review].

**Secondary (central/hypogonadotropic) hypogonadism**: the fault lies upstream — the hypothalamus fails to produce adequate GnRH, or the pituitary fails to release sufficient LH/FSH. Because the signal never reaches the testes, T is low AND LH/FSH are low or inappropriately normal (they should be elevated if T is truly low). Causes include pituitary adenoma (especially prolactinoma), hyperprolactinemia from any cause, Kallmann syndrome, opioid use, glucocorticoid excess, and severe systemic illness [1, mechanism_review].

**Functional/age-related hypogonadism**: a third category in which neither organ is irreversibly damaged. Obesity, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes (T2D), obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and intercurrent illness suppress the HPG axis through multiple overlapping mechanisms — elevated estradiol from peripheral aromatization in adipose tissue, hyperleptinemia, inflammatory cytokines, and hyperinsulinemia all reduce GnRH pulsatility. The result is low T with low-to-normal LH/FSH, phenotypically indistinguishable from central hypogonadism. Weight loss can normalize testosterone without any exogenous hormone [1, mechanism_review].

### Symptoms and the Diagnostic Threshold Controversy

The hallmark symptoms of testosterone deficiency — reduced libido, erectile dysfunction (particularly loss of morning erections), fatigue, decreased muscle mass, and reduced bone mineral density — are nonspecific and highly prevalent in aging men independent of testosterone status [23, cohort].

The **300 ng/dL (10.4 nmol/L) cutoff** popularized by the American Urological Association as a diagnostic threshold has been contested. The Endocrine Society's 2018 Clinical Practice Guideline sets the lower limit of normal at **264 ng/dL (9.2 nmol/L)**, derived from the harmonized reference range in healthy, nonobese young men calibrated to the CDC LC-MS/MS standard [10, regulatory]. The guideline requires both criteria: symptoms AND "unequivocally and consistently low" testosterone, confirmed on two separate fasting morning samples. A single low value is insufficient.

The European Male Aging Study (EMAS) provided the strongest population-level evidence on the symptom-threshold relationship. In 3,369 men aged 40–79, Wu et al. (2010) found that only three sexual symptoms — poor morning erection, erectile dysfunction, and low sexual desire — showed a specific, graded association with testosterone concentrations. Late-onset hypogonadism was best identified by the combination of at least three of these sexual symptoms PLUS total T < 11 nmol/L (< 317 ng/dL) AND free T < 220 pmol/L (< 64 pg/mL) [23, cohort]. This work shifted the field toward requiring both symptom and biochemical criteria rather than a single absolute number.

### Determinants That Lower Total Testosterone

**Aging** is the most prevalent driver of declining testosterone at the population level. Feldman et al. (2002) in the Massachusetts Male Aging Study (MMAS) quantified, from longitudinal data in 1,156 men followed 7–10 years, a within-subject decline of **1.6%/year for total T and 2–3%/year for bioavailable T** — rates steeper than cross-sectional estimates because men who developed comorbidities contributed disproportionately to the longitudinal slope [24, cohort]. A more recent analysis of 625 men in the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (Carto et al., 2023) further dissected this: after adjusting for comorbidities (diabetes, heart failure, obesity, anemia), age itself did not independently predict testosterone decline — the bulk of the apparent age effect was mediated by accumulating disease burden [25, cohort].

**Obesity and metabolic syndrome / T2D** suppress testosterone through two parallel pathways: (a) SHBG is lowered by hyperinsulinemia and fatty liver, reducing measured total T even when free T is relatively preserved; (b) adipose-derived estradiol, leptin, and inflammatory cytokines directly suppress GnRH pulsatility, producing a secondary (central) pattern with low LH/FSH [1, mechanism_review]. This two-punch mechanism — reduced SHBG AND suppressed central drive — makes obesity one of the most potent non-pharmacological suppressors of total testosterone.

**Opioids** are a major and underdiagnosed pharmacological cause of central hypogonadism. Kafel et al. (2025) reviewed the prevalence of opioid-induced androgen deficiency (OPIAD) in men: **20–80% of men on chronic opioids develop biochemically confirmed hypogonadism**, with the range reflecting dose, formulation, and duration. The mechanism is primarily central — opioids inhibit hypothalamic GnRH synthesis and secretion via μ-receptors, yielding the low-T + low/inappropriately-normal-LH/FSH signature of secondary hypogonadism [26, mechanism_review].

**Glucocorticoids** suppress testosterone through dual central and gonadal mechanisms. MacAdams et al. (1986) documented in a controlled study that testosterone fell in 14 of 16 men on chronic glucocorticoids, to a mean of **211 ± 93 ng/dL vs. 449 ± 111 ng/dL in matched controls** (p < 0.001), with a significant inverse dose-response (r = –0.78) [27, cohort]. Both hypothalamic GnRH suppression and direct Leydig cell inhibition of steroidogenic enzymes contribute.

**Sleep deprivation and OSA** associate with lower testosterone. Kim and Cho (2019) reviewed OSA cohort data finding consistent associations between OSA in middle-aged men and reduced testosterone secretion, compounded by obesity and aging [28, mechanism_review]. The directionality is partly bidirectional: low T may worsen sleep architecture, while sleep fragmentation disrupts the nocturnal pulsatile T surge that accounts for the physiologic morning peak.

**Anabolic-androgenic steroid (AAS)-induced hypogonadism (ASIH)**: exogenous androgens suppress the HPG axis via negative feedback, reducing GnRH, LH, and FSH, leading to testicular atrophy and near-complete cessation of endogenous testosterone synthesis. Rahnema et al. (2014) reviewed the clinical management of ASIH and noted that biochemical recovery of testosterone typically occurs over months to 1–2 years after cessation — but some individuals, particularly those with long or high-dose exposure, may not fully recover [29, mechanism_review]. This represents a potentially persistent secondary hypogonadism pattern.

Additional suppressors include: **heavy alcohol** (both acute — suppresses LH pulse amplitude — and chronic — direct Leydig cell toxicity); **marijuana** (cannabinoids inhibit GnRH/LH pulsatility); **hyperprolactinemia** (prolactin directly suppresses GnRH, a central mechanism); and **chronic systemic illness** (inflammatory cytokines and the associated catabolic state reduce gonadotropin drive).

### Determinants That Raise Total Testosterone

In men: **testosterone replacement therapy (TRT)** and other exogenous androgens are the most potent pharmacological elevators. **hCG** (human chorionic gonadotropin) mimics LH and directly stimulates Leydig cell testosterone production — relevant both in hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and in fertility-preserving alternatives to TRT. **Clomiphene citrate and other SERMs** block hypothalamic/pituitary estrogen receptors, reducing negative feedback and increasing endogenous LH/FSH and thereby T — often used in secondary hypogonadism when fertility preservation matters. **Weight loss in obese men** raises testosterone through the reverse of the obesity-suppression pathway: SHBG rises and central HPG suppression remits.

In **women**, elevated total testosterone signals androgen excess. The most common cause is **polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)**, which involves both ovarian and adrenal androgen excess. Other causes include androgen-secreting ovarian or adrenal tumors (often producing markedly elevated levels with rapid virilization), **congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH)** (particularly 21-hydroxylase deficiency, causing diversion of steroid precursors to androgens), and exogenous androgen administration.

### Associations and the CV-Safety Question

Low testosterone associates with metabolic syndrome, T2D, and all-cause mortality in multiple observational cohorts. The critical interpretive caveat is that much of this signal reflects **reverse causation and confounding by illness/obesity rather than T deficiency as an independent cause** [1, mechanism_review]. Chronic disease and adiposity independently suppress testosterone — so men who are sicker have lower T, but this does not establish that low T caused the adverse outcomes. Prospective observational data cannot resolve this, which is precisely why the TRAVERSE trial was designed.

The **TRAVERSE trial** (Lincoff et al., 2023, NEJM) was a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, noninferiority trial enrolling **5,246 men aged 45–80** with symptomatic hypogonadism (testosterone < 300 ng/dL on two fasting samples) and pre-existing cardiovascular disease or multiple CV risk factors. Men were randomized to transdermal testosterone (1.62% gel, titrated to 350–750 ng/dL) or placebo [30, rct]. **Primary result: MACE (cardiovascular death, nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke) occurred in 7.0% of the testosterone group vs. 7.3% of placebo (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.78–1.17), meeting the noninferiority criterion (p < 0.001)**. TRT did not increase major adverse cardiac events in this high-risk population.

**However, TRAVERSE revealed secondary safety signals**: the testosterone group had higher incidences of atrial fibrillation, pulmonary embolism, and acute kidney injury compared with placebo. These findings do not negate the MACE noninferiority result but underscore that TRT is not categorically safe in all domains and that arrhythmia and thromboembolic risk require consideration in individual patients [30, rct].

### SHBG and the Obligation to Interpret Contextually

Because ~44% of circulating testosterone is tightly bound to SHBG (and ~54% loosely bound to albumin), and because only the unbound free fraction (~2%) plus the loosely albumin-bound fraction (bioavailable fraction) is thought to be biologically active, **total testosterone is an unreliable surrogate when SHBG is abnormal** [10, regulatory].

Conditions that **raise SHBG** (thus elevating total T while free T may be normal or low): aging, hyperthyroidism, liver disease, HIV, certain anticonvulsants, and anorexia. Conditions that **lower SHBG** (depressing total T while free T may be relatively preserved): obesity, hypothyroidism, hyperinsulinemia, nephrotic syndrome, and exogenous androgens. The Endocrine Society recommends measuring free testosterone — either by equilibrium dialysis (gold standard) or by calculation from total T + SHBG + albumin — whenever SHBG abnormality is suspected [10, regulatory].
