# Free T3 (Free Triiodothyronine): Canonical Research Report

## Summary

Triiodothyronine (T3) is the biologically active thyroid hormone. It is the molecule that occupies nuclear thyroid hormone receptors (TRα, TRβ) and drives nearly all classical thyroid hormone effects — basal metabolic rate, cardiac output, thermogenesis, bone turnover, and neurological function. T4 (thyroxine), the thyroid gland's primary secretory product, functions predominantly as a prohormone; roughly 83% of circulating T3 is generated by peripheral deiodination of T4, not by direct thyroidal secretion [1, mechanism_review].

Free T3 (fT3) is the unbound, bioavailable fraction of circulating T3 — approximately 0.2–0.3% of total T3 — and the only form capable of crossing cell membranes and entering the nucleus. The conventional adult reference interval is approximately **2.3–4.2 pg/mL (3.5–6.5 pmol/L)** (unit conversion: pg/mL × 1.536 = pmol/L), though this is a platform-specific construct: every immunoassay manufacturer establishes its own interval from its own reference population, and intervals diverge materially across methods [6, regulatory; 8, cohort].

The primary clinical value of fT3 is in **hyperthyroidism**, specifically to confirm and grade thyrotoxicosis and to identify **T3 toxicosis** — a pattern of elevated fT3, normal fT4, and suppressed TSH that accounts for approximately 5% of thyrotoxicosis cases. fT3 has essentially **no diagnostic role in hypothyroidism**: fT3 is preserved until late-stage disease because upregulated type 2 deiodinase (D2) in peripheral tissues compensates for falling T4, and serum fT3 is the last parameter to fall [2, mechanism_review; 19, regulatory].

Low fT3 is ubiquitous in acute and chronic non-thyroidal illness (NTI), affecting 60–70% of critically ill patients. This low-T3 syndrome is a peripheral deiodinase-mediated phenomenon (not primary thyroid failure), carries significant prognostic weight in cardiac cohorts, but is a **severity marker, not a treatment target** — no large RCT has shown that T3 supplementation improves NTIS outcomes [13, mechanism_review; 20, meta_analysis].

fT3 measurement is technically challenging. It circulates at picomolar concentrations (~2–6 pmol/L), making routine immunoassays imprecise and susceptible to protein-binding artifacts, heterophile antibodies, anti-T3 autoantibodies, and high-dose biotin. The reference method — equilibrium dialysis followed by LC-MS/MS — avoids these artifacts but is not used for routine clinical practice. Standardization across platforms lags that of TSH and fT4: no widely adopted certified reference material for fT3 currently exists [11, mechanism_review; 27, regulatory].

## Physiology & What Free T3 Measures

### T3 as the Active Thyroid Hormone

Triiodothyronine (T3; 3,5,3'-triiodothyronine) is the biologically active form of thyroid hormone. While the thyroid gland secretes thyroxine (T4) as its primary product — at roughly 10-fold the rate of T3 by mass — T4 functions predominantly as a prohormone [1, mechanism_review]. T3 is the molecule that actually occupies nuclear thyroid hormone receptors and drives gene expression changes responsible for nearly all classical thyroid hormone effects: regulation of basal metabolic rate, cardiac output, thermogenesis, skeletal development, and neurological function [2, mechanism_review].

### Receptor Binding: Why T3 Is the Active Form

T3 exerts its genomic effects by binding nuclear thyroid hormone receptors, TRα (predominantly in brain, heart, and skeletal muscle) and TRβ (liver, pituitary, cochlea). T3 binds these receptors with approximately 7- to 15-fold greater affinity than T4 in direct binding assays; the functional potency gap is even larger — EC50 data show T4 requires ~30- to 50-fold higher concentrations than T3 to achieve equivalent transcriptional activation at TRα1 and TRβ1, respectively [3, mechanism_review]. Receptor binding triggers a conformational change in helix-12 of the TR ligand-binding domain, displacing corepressor complexes and recruiting coactivators that initiate polymerase-driven gene transcription [4, mechanism_review]. It is T3 occupancy — not T4 occupancy — that constitutes the predominant transcriptional signal under euthyroid conditions, with approximately 50% of liver and kidney thyroid hormone receptors occupied by T3 at normal circulating concentrations [1, mechanism_review].

### Source of Circulating T3: Peripheral Conversion Dominates

The thyroid gland contributes only a minority of circulating T3 directly. In a 70-kg adult, total daily T3 production is approximately 30 µg/day, of which only ~5 µg/day (~17%) derives from direct thyroidal secretion [1, mechanism_review]. The remaining ~25 µg/day (~83%) is generated by peripheral deiodination of T4 in extrathyroidal tissues [1, mechanism_review]. This means the body's T3 supply is tightly coupled to the adequacy of T4 production and the activity of the tissue deiodinase enzymes.

### The Deiodinase System: Activation and Inactivation

Three selenoenzyme deiodinases control thyroid hormone bioavailability at the tissue level [5, mechanism_review]:

**Type 1 deiodinase (D1)** is expressed primarily in liver, kidney, and thyroid. It performs outer-ring (5') deiodination to convert T4→T3, and also inner-ring deiodination to convert T4→reverse-T3 (rT3) and clear conjugated T3 metabolites. D1 has low T4 affinity (Km ~1–2 µM) and is induced by thyroid hormone itself. It is the main source of T3 appearing in the bloodstream, contributing ~5 µg/day to circulating T3 [1, mechanism_review].

**Type 2 deiodinase (D2)** is expressed in brain, pituitary, brown adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, heart, and vascular smooth muscle. D2 performs exclusively outer-ring deiodination (T4→T3) with very high T4 affinity (Km ~1 nM), supplying ~20 µg/day of T3 production — predominantly for intracellular use in D2-expressing cells rather than export to the bloodstream [1, mechanism_review]. D2's short half-life (~20 minutes in the presence of T4 substrate) makes it exquisitely responsive to local T4 availability and allows tissue-level fine-tuning of T3 supply independent of circulating T3 levels [1, mechanism_review]. D2 is the enzyme responsible for providing T3 to pituitary thyrotrophs and hypothalamic neurons, making it central to HPT-axis feedback sensing.

**Type 3 deiodinase (D3)** is the primary inactivating enzyme. It performs inner-ring deiodination, converting T4→reverse-T3 (inactive) and T3→3,3'-diiodothyronine (T2, inactive) [5, mechanism_review]. D3 is expressed in brain, skin, placenta, and fetal tissues, and is upregulated in critical illness (where it contributes to the "euthyroid sick syndrome" pattern of low T3). The balance between D2 (activating) and D3 (inactivating) in any given tissue is the primary local determinant of intracellular T3 concentration, independent of what circulating fT3 measures.

### Free T3: The Unbound, Bioavailable Fraction

Like other steroid and thyroid hormones, T3 circulates almost entirely bound to plasma carrier proteins — primarily thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG), with minor contributions from transthyretin (TTR) and albumin. T3 binds TBG with substantially lower affinity than T4, which is reflected in its larger free fraction: approximately 0.2–0.3% of circulating T3 is unbound (free), compared with ~0.01% for T4 [6, regulatory]. In absolute terms, a typical euthyroid adult has a serum free T3 concentration of approximately **2.3–4.2 pg/mL (3.5–6.5 pmol/L)**, with specific reference intervals varying by assay platform and population studied [6, regulatory]. (Unit conversion: pg/mL × 1.536 = pmol/L.)

The free fraction is the only form capable of crossing cell membranes (via thyroid hormone transporters, including MCT8 and OATP1C1), entering the nucleus, and activating thyroid hormone receptors. Protein-bound T3 acts as a circulating reservoir that buffers against rapid fluctuations in free hormone. Because T3's lower TBG binding affinity means it distributes more freely than T4, approximately two-thirds of total body T3 resides in tissues at any given moment, versus two-thirds of T4 remaining in plasma [2, mechanism_review].

### Why Serum fT3 Imperfectly Reflects Tissue Thyroid Status

A fundamental limitation of serum fT3 measurement is that it captures only the circulating pool — not intracellular T3, which in D2-expressing tissues can substantially exceed what serum concentrations would predict. Nuclear T3 concentrations have been estimated at 58- to 251-fold above cytosolic concentrations in liver, kidney, heart, and brain, reflecting active nuclear uptake and local D2 activity [1, mechanism_review]. This means thyroid hormone action at the tissue level is substantially shaped by local deiodinase activity that serum fT3 cannot capture.

Additionally, serum fT3 is a less sensitive primary signal for the HPT axis than fT4. The pituitary thyrotroph relies on intracellular D2-mediated T4→T3 conversion to generate the T3 that occupies pituitary TRβ2 receptors and suppresses TSH secretion. Because the pituitary "senses" T4 and converts it locally, TSH correlates more tightly with fT4 than with serum fT3 [7, mechanism_review]. This also explains why serum fT3 is often the last parameter to fall in evolving hypothyroidism — the pituitary and peripheral D2 upregulate in response to declining T4, sustaining T3 production at the expense of increased TSH drive, until T4 substrate becomes insufficient.

## Reference Ranges, Units & Thresholds

### Reference Intervals: Typical Adult Values

The conventional adult free T3 (fT3) reference interval is approximately **2.3–4.2 pg/mL (3.5–6.5 pmol/L)**. This range is not a fixed biological constant but a platform-specific construct: every immunoassay manufacturer establishes its own reference interval from its own population sample, and intervals shift materially across methods. In a Spanish population study using the Abbott ARCHITECT i2000 analyzer, the calculated healthy-adult interval was 1.48–3.37 pg/mL (2.27–5.18 pmol/L) [8, cohort]. A Turkish clinical-chemistry group using Roche Cobas electrochemiluminescence immunoassay reported a combined adult interval of 3.38–6.35 pmol/L in males and 3.39–5.99 pmol/L in females, with the manufacturer's stated range of 3.1–6.8 pmol/L differing noticeably from their population-derived values — and both diverging from the ARCHITECT numbers above [9, cohort]. A Chinese elderly-population study (DiaSorin LIAISON XL chemiluminescence) found fT3 of 3.40–6.44 pmol/L for adults under 65, declining to 2.82–5.57 pmol/L in those aged 80–100 [10, cohort].

These discordances are not minor rounding differences. They reflect genuine method-dependence: direct analogue immunoassays for free hormones are vulnerable to artifacts from altered binding proteins, assay matrix effects, and antibody cross-reactivity [11, mechanism_review]. The practical consequence is that a result of 3.2 pg/mL may be "normal" on one platform and flagged low on another. Clinicians should always interpret fT3 against the reference interval issued by the laboratory performing the assay, never a generic textbook number.

### Unit Conversion

Free T3 is reported in **pg/mL** (mass units) or **pmol/L** (molar units). The conversion factor is **×1.536**: 1 pg/mL = 1.536 pmol/L (molecular weight of T3 = 650.97 g/mol).

| pg/mL | pmol/L |
|-------|--------|
| 2.3   | 3.5    |
| 3.0   | 4.6    |
| 3.5   | 5.4    |
| 4.0   | 6.1    |
| 4.2   | 6.5    |

Some platforms (particularly European automated analyzers) report exclusively in pmol/L; US platforms commonly use pg/mL. Both units appear in the literature for the same analyte, and unit confusion is a known source of clinical error.

### Diagnostic Role: Where Free T3 Adds Value

**Hyperthyroidism — the core use case.** Free T3 is diagnostically useful in hyperthyroidism, specifically for identifying **T3 toxicosis**: a state of clinical thyrotoxicosis in which fT3 is elevated while fT4 remains within or only borderline above the reference range and TSH is suppressed. This occurs because the hyperactive thyroid preferentially secretes T3, and because elevated TSH-receptor stimulation (as in Graves' disease) upregulates the thyroidal T3/T4 secretion ratio [19, regulatory].

T3 toxicosis accounts for approximately 5% of all thyrotoxicosis cases in North America (some older series report up to 10%, but contemporary series with sensitive immunoassays cluster around 2–5%) [19, regulatory]. It appears most often in:

- **Early Graves' disease** — T3 excess may precede fT4 elevation, making fT3 the first detectable biochemical abnormality.
- **Autonomously functioning thyroid nodules** (solitary toxic adenoma or toxic multinodular goiter) — these preferentially secrete T3, and T3 toxicosis may represent the earliest stage before the nodule grows large enough to also suppress T4 feedback [15, mechanism_review].
- **Iodine deficiency goiter** becoming autonomous.

The key interpretive grid for hyperthyroidism evaluation:

| TSH | fT4 | fT3 | Interpretation |
|-----|-----|-----|----------------|
| Low/suppressed | High | High | Overt hyperthyroidism |
| Low/suppressed | Normal | High | T3 toxicosis |
| Low/suppressed | Normal | Normal | Subclinical hyperthyroidism (fT3 helps rule out T3 toxicosis) |
| Normal | Normal | Normal | Euthyroid |

The 2016 ATA Guidelines for hyperthyroidism note that "a suppressed or subnormal TSH level should be followed by assay of both free T4 and free T3 to distinguish subclinical from overt thyrotoxicosis and to identify T3 toxicosis." They also note that total T3 is often preferred over free T3 in this context because free T3 assays are "less widely validated and less robust" than free T4 assays, and total T3 measurement is frequently more reproducible across platforms [19, regulatory].

**Hypothyroidism — not a useful test.** Free T3 is not recommended for diagnosing or monitoring hypothyroidism. The ATA 2014 hypothyroidism treatment guidelines establish TSH as the primary monitoring parameter; fT3 is not a target for dose adjustment [18, regulatory]. The Endocrine Society, through its Choosing Wisely recommendations, explicitly advises against ordering free or total T3 when assessing levothyroxine dosing [16, cohort].

The physiological rationale is straightforward: fT3 is preserved until late in the course of hypothyroidism. Even with significantly impaired thyroidal output, peripheral deiodination of T4 to T3 by type 2 deiodinase (D2) — particularly in the brain and pituitary — maintains circulating fT3 within the reference range long after TSH has become elevated and fT4 has fallen. Free T3 is thus an insensitive hypothyroid marker. Conversely, patients on levothyroxine monotherapy who achieve a normal TSH often have a serum T3 at the lower end of, or slightly below, the reference range because endogenous thyroidal T3 secretion has been lost — this does not represent a deficiency requiring intervention.

### Non-Thyroidal Illness (Low-T3 Syndrome)

Low fT3 in the context of acute or chronic systemic illness is extremely common and does not indicate primary thyroid disease. This "low T3 syndrome" (also called non-thyroidal illness syndrome, NTIS, or euthyroid sick syndrome) affects an estimated 60–70% of critically ill patients [17, mechanism_review].

The mechanism is peripheral rather than thyroidal: proinflammatory cytokines downregulate type 1 deiodinase (D1), reducing T4→T3 conversion; type 3 deiodinase (D3) activity increases, shunting T4 toward reverse T3 (rT3) instead of active T3; hepatic uptake of T4 falls, further reducing substrate for peripheral T3 production. The net result is low serum T3, elevated rT3, and — crucially — **normal or only modestly altered TSH**, because the pituitary-thyroid axis itself is not the primary site of dysfunction [13, mechanism_review].

The hallmark distinguishing low-T3 syndrome from genuine primary hypothyroidism is the TSH: in NTIS, TSH is typically normal (or transiently suppressed in the acute phase), whereas primary hypothyroidism raises TSH.

### Age, Sex, and Pregnancy Variation

**Age.** fT3 shows a modest but consistent age-related decline. In the Roche ECLIA population study, upper-reference limits in subjects over 70 were approximately 0.6–1.0 pmol/L below those in adults aged 18–30 [9, cohort]. The elderly Chinese Han population study confirmed a similar progressive decline: subjects aged 65–79 had a reference upper limit of 5.91 pmol/L vs. 6.44 pmol/L for those under 65 [10, cohort]. Age-specific reference intervals should be used when evaluating thyroid function in older adults.

**Sex.** Most immunoassay-based studies show no clinically significant sex difference in fT3 in the adult range, though some population studies find slightly lower upper limits in females [9, cohort].

**Pregnancy.** fT3 reference ranges shift during gestation and are highly method-dependent. A Greek cohort study (IMMULITE 2000 immunoassay) reported first-trimester fT3 of 1.54–5.22 pg/mL (2.37–8.02 pmol/L) and second-trimester fT3 of 1.78–5.29 pg/mL (2.73–8.13 pmol/L), with a marginal trend toward declining fT3 as gestation advances [22, cohort]. A correlational analysis of immunoassay vs. mass spectrometry for fT3 in pregnant women found a correlation of only r = 0.397 — indicating that different platforms can give substantially different numeric results for the same sample [12, mechanism_review]. The 2012 Endocrine Society pregnancy guideline recommends using assay- and trimester-specific reference intervals when interpreting thyroid function in pregnancy and notes that fT3 measurement adds value primarily when TSH is suppressed and fT4 is normal, raising concern for T3 toxicosis [23, regulatory].

The late-pregnancy fT3 decline (third trimester relative to first) is well-described across multiple populations and is attributed to increased thyroid hormone binding, expanded plasma volume, and altered deiodinase activity — not to a fall in thyroidal secretion.

## Measurement & Interferences

### The Core Measurement Challenge

Free T3 circulates at remarkably low concentrations — roughly 2–6 pmol/L (approximately 1.3–3.9 pg/mL) — which is several-fold lower than free T4 and far lower than total T3 or total T4 [11, mechanism_review]. This picomolar range creates a fundamental analytical problem: any minor matrix perturbation, protein-binding artifact, or cross-reacting species introduces a proportionally large error in the reported free fraction. Welsh and Soldin noted that T3 concentrations are roughly ten-fold lower than T4, meaning "measuring T3 has always presented a greater sensitivity and precision challenge than measuring T4" [11, mechanism_review].

**Reference method.** The gold standard for fT3 measurement is equilibrium dialysis (ED) or ultrafiltration to physically separate the free fraction, followed by quantification via isotope-dilution liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (ID-LC-MS/MS). This approach avoids binding-protein matrix effects that confound immunoassays and is not susceptible to antibody cross-reactivity or endogenous binding protein variation [11, mechanism_review]. The key limitation of ED/LC-MS/MS is complexity and cost, rendering it impractical for routine high-throughput clinical use.

**Routine immunoassays.** The vast majority of clinical laboratories use analog one-step automated immunoassays that estimate fT3 indirectly. These assays introduce a labeled T3 analog into serum and infer the free fraction from competition kinetics — without physically separating the free hormone. Because they do not remove binding proteins before measurement, they are sensitive to any factor that alters the T3/protein equilibrium in the sample [14, mechanism_review]. Welsh and Soldin documented that multiple studies found "falsely normal values for T3, FT3 and FT4 by immunoassay that are below the reference interval when measured by ultrafiltration LC-MS/MS," highlighting systematic immunoassay bias relative to the reference method [11, mechanism_review].

**Imprecision at low concentrations.** Modern fT3 immunoassays have improved: Lin (2024) reported within-run imprecision of approximately 2.0% CV for fT3, somewhat better than total T3 (~3.3% CV) [13, mechanism_review]. However, these precision estimates apply to the central assay range; imprecision increases substantially at the low end — the very range that matters for detecting hypothyroidism or tracking illness-driven suppression.

### Total T3 versus Free T3: When to Use Each

Because fT3 immunoassays are imprecise at low concentrations and susceptible to protein-binding artifacts, total T3 (tT3) is sometimes preferred — particularly for detecting hyperthyroidism [11, mechanism_review]. In hyperthyroid states, both total and free T3 are substantially elevated, and the signal-to-noise ratio favors total T3 on many platforms. Clinical utility of T3 measurement (in any form) is highest for: (a) T3 toxicosis, where TSH is suppressed and fT4 is normal or low but T3 is disproportionately elevated; and (b) early or subclinical hyperthyroidism where fT4 remains within range [13, mechanism_review]. Lin's institutional audit found T3 thyrotoxicosis occurred in only 1.6% of cases with concurrent TSH/fT4/fT3 results, supporting selective rather than reflex T3 ordering [13, mechanism_review].

For hypothyroidism monitoring, neither fT3 nor tT3 adds meaningful diagnostic information beyond TSH and fT4 in most patients. The major review of thyroid assay methodology explicitly states that T3 measurements "have no value for diagnosing or monitoring treatment for hypothyroidism" [11, mechanism_review]. Free T3 becomes clinically relevant in hypothyroidism primarily when investigating combination T4/T3 replacement therapy outcomes.

### Interferences

**Biotin (Vitamin B7).** Excess circulating biotin interferes with streptavidin-biotin capture systems that most automated immunoassays employ. In competitive fT3 assays (one-step platforms), excess biotin displaces the biotinylated capture complex, causing **falsely elevated** fT3 results. The direction of interference in TSH sandwich assays is opposite — biotin causes falsely low TSH [21, mechanism_review]. Platform dependency is critical: Roche Cobas platforms are affected across TSH, fT4, and fT3; Beckman platforms primarily affect fT4/fT3; Ortho platforms primarily affect TSH [21, mechanism_review]. Patients taking high-dose biotin supplements (≥10 mg/day or higher, as used in multiple sclerosis trials) should discontinue biotin for at least 48 hours before thyroid function testing.

**Heterophile antibodies and anti-animal antibodies.** Human anti-mouse antibodies (HAMA) and other heterophile antibodies can cross-link assay antibodies, producing falsely elevated or falsely low results depending on assay architecture. Two-site sandwich assays (used for TSH) are more susceptible than competitive immunoassays used for fT3 [21, mechanism_review]. However, heterophile antibody interference in fT3 assays has been documented and can cause clinically significant elevation.

**Anti-streptavidin and anti-ruthenium antibodies.** Endogenous antibodies directed against streptavidin (a capture reagent) or ruthenium (an electrochemiluminescence label used in Roche Cobas platforms) interfere in a manner that mimics biotin effect but via different mechanisms. Anti-streptavidin antibodies typically cause decreased TSH with elevated fT4/fT3; effects can persist for 18–24 months after initial detection. Anti-ruthenium antibody effects are more heterogeneous — typically elevated fT4/fT3 with decreased TSH but can be reversed — and may not be correctable by standard blocking reagents [21, mechanism_review]. Favresse et al. (2018) reported that ≥50% of documented thyroid interferences led to misdiagnosis or inappropriate management, underscoring the clinical stakes of these analytical pitfalls [21, mechanism_review].

**Anti-T3 autoantibodies.** Autoantibodies against T3 are a specific and often underrecognized fT3 pitfall. Their effect on immunoassay results depends critically on assay design:

- **One-step (competitive) assays:** Anti-T3 autoantibodies bind both the endogenous T3 and the labeled analog tracer, causing **falsely elevated** fT3 results — sometimes dramatically so, leading to a clinical picture resembling T3 toxicosis [24, mechanism_review].
- **Two-step assays:** Because patient serum and labeled tracer do not contact each other simultaneously, two-step formats are largely insensitive to anti-T3 autoantibodies, and measured fT3 is falsely low or undetectable [21, mechanism_review].

This assay-design dependency means discordant fT3 results across platforms — or fT3/TSH dissociation — should prompt investigation for anti-T3 autoantibodies. Courcelles et al. (2023) documented a case of falsely elevated T3 on two different immunoassay platforms (Roche Cobas 8000 and Abbott Architect i2000), ultimately identified via radioimmunoprecipitation and PEG precipitation [25, mechanism_review]. Detection is confirmed by repeating the assay after polyethylene glycol precipitation or by direct measurement using ED/LC-MS/MS, which is immune to antibody interference.

**Drugs displacing T3 from binding proteins.** Several commonly used drugs compete with T3 for binding sites on TBG, albumin, and transthyretin, artificially raising the apparent free T3 fraction in immunoassays [14, mechanism_review]:

- **Furosemide** (doses >80 mg/day, especially IV): displaces T4 and T3 from TBG and albumin
- **Salicylates and NSAIDs**: compete for binding sites
- **Phenytoin**: displaces thyroid hormones and may additionally reduce total hormone concentrations
- **Heparin**: activates lipoprotein lipase, releasing free fatty acids that displace T3/T4 from binding proteins; effect appears within 2–15 minutes and is an in vitro artifact of sample handling, not an in vivo phenomenon [14, mechanism_review]
- **Amiodarone**: inhibits D1-mediated T4-to-T3 conversion, lowering fT3 via a physiological (not assay) mechanism

**Abnormal binding proteins.** Conditions that alter TBG, albumin, or transthyretin concentrations or binding affinity — including familial dysalbuminaemic hyperthyroxinaemia (FDH), TBG excess or deficiency, and hypoalbuminaemia — can cause divergence between immunoassay-measured fT3 and the true free fraction [14, mechanism_review]. Reference methods (ED/LC-MS/MS) are unaffected because they physically remove binding proteins before quantification.

### Non-Thyroidal Illness Syndrome (NTIS): The Dominant Confounder

The largest source of low fT3 outside true thyroid disease is non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS), also termed the euthyroid sick syndrome or low-T3 syndrome. During systemic illness, caloric restriction, or fasting, fT3 can fall substantially while TSH remains normal or slightly suppressed — a pattern that can be mistaken for central hypothyroidism or subclinical hypothyroid-T3 deficiency if the clinical context is not considered [26, mechanism_review; 13, mechanism_review].

Two deiodinase changes drive NTIS: (1) **D1 (type 1 deiodinase) downregulation** in liver and skeletal muscle reduces the peripheral conversion of T4 to the active T3 — the dominant pathway for circulating T3 production [26, mechanism_review]; (2) **D3 (type 3 deiodinase) upregulation** — particularly in critical illness — inactivates both T4 and T3 by converting them to reverse T3 (rT3) and other inactive iodothyronines [26, mechanism_review; 13, mechanism_review].

The net result is low serum T3 (both total and free), often with elevated rT3. Note, however, that rT3 elevation is not universal: Fliers and Boelen (2021) caution that rT3 "may be normal or even reduced in some NTIS cases," particularly in prolonged critical illness [26, mechanism_review].

**Fasting and caloric restriction.** Even in healthy individuals, fasting reduces serum T3 within 24–48 hours, with decreases proportional to the severity and duration of energy restriction. Very low calorie diets, prolonged fasting, and ketogenic diets all suppress T3 via D1 inhibition — a normal adaptive response to reduce metabolic rate during energy deficit [13, mechanism_review]. Any fT3 value obtained during active weight loss, illness, or hospitalization must be interpreted with caution; it reflects the sum of thyroid secretion AND peripheral deiodination status, not thyroid gland function alone.

**Sample handling.** fT3 immunoassays require serum or lithium heparin plasma in plastic transport tubes; EDTA, oxalate, and citrate anticoagulants are contraindicated. Sample stability is limited, and prolonged room-temperature exposure or lipemia can alter results on some platforms.

### Standardization Status

The IFCC Committee for Standardization of Thyroid Function Tests (C-STFT) has developed a conventional reference measurement procedure (cRMP) for fT4 based on equilibrium dialysis + ID-LC-MS/MS, and has indicated the same framework extends to fT3 [27, regulatory]. However, fT3 standardization lags fT4: the fT3 reference procedure is less mature, inter-laboratory harmonization is substantially worse than for TSH or fT4, and no widely adopted certified reference material for fT3 currently exists. The consequence is that reported fT3 reference intervals and absolute values are not directly comparable across laboratory platforms or between institutions — a point of particular importance when tracking individual patients across different laboratory systems.

## Determinants & Clinical Significance

### Primary Clinical Role: Confirming and Grading Hyperthyroidism

Free T3 occupies a specific and well-defined diagnostic position: it is most useful in the workup and monitoring of **hyperthyroidism**, not hypothyroidism. In overt thyrotoxicosis, both fT4 and fT3 are elevated with a suppressed TSH (<0.01 mU/L on a third-generation assay). Because the hyperthyroid thyroid preferentially secretes T3 relative to T4, fT3 is often disproportionately elevated and its magnitude roughly tracks with disease severity. The 2016 ATA guidelines for hyperthyroidism establish that elevated fT3, fT4, or both — in the context of a subnormal TSH — constitutes overt hyperthyroidism [19, regulatory].

**T3-toxicosis** is a clinically important pattern that fT3 is uniquely positioned to detect: isolated elevation of fT3 with normal fT4 and suppressed TSH. According to the ATA guidelines, this represents either the earliest phase of Graves' disease (after TSH becomes suppressed but before fT4 rises) or a characteristic feature of autonomously functioning toxic nodules, where it occurs in an estimated 5–46% of cases [19, regulatory]. In relapsing Graves' disease, T3-toxicosis is also a recognized early-recurrence pattern. The guidelines note that because fT3 assays are less widely validated than fT4 assays, total T3 is frequently preferred in clinical practice for this purpose — a practical caveat that reflects assay imprecision at low concentrations rather than any conceptual limitation of the free fraction [19, regulatory].

Drugs and conditions that raise fT3 include: active hyperthyroidism (Graves', toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma), exogenous T3 ingestion (liothyronine, desiccated thyroid), recovery phase of non-thyroidal illness, and hCG-driven gestational thyrotoxicosis.

### Limited Role in Hypothyroidism

fT3 plays essentially no diagnostic role in hypothyroidism. In primary hypothyroidism, TSH rises first and fT4 falls before fT3 does, because the body maintains T3 production through upregulation of type 2 deiodinase (D2) in peripheral tissues — compensating until late-stage disease. As a result, fT3 remains within the normal range in all but severe, long-standing primary hypothyroidism, making it insensitive for diagnosis or treatment monitoring.

The 2014 ATA guidelines on thyroid hormone replacement (Jonklaas et al.) are explicit on this point: serum T3 or fT3 measurement is not recommended for the diagnosis of hypothyroidism, and TSH remains the primary monitoring tool during levothyroxine therapy [18, regulatory]. The core reason is quantitative: the serum T3 pool is buffered by peripheral deiodination, so serum levels do not reliably track tissue thyroid status in hypothyroid patients on LT4.

### Non-Thyroidal Illness (NTI) / Low-T3 Syndrome

In any significant acute or chronic illness — infection, cardiac failure, surgery, malignancy, starvation, major trauma — circulating fT3 falls, sometimes dramatically, without primary thyroid pathology. This is called **non-thyroidal illness syndrome** (NTIS) or "low-T3 syndrome." The mechanism involves downregulation of type 1 deiodinase (D1) in liver and kidney (which normally supplies ~80% of circulating T3 from T4), concurrent upregulation of type 3 deiodinase (D3, which inactivates T3 to T2 and T4 to reverse T3), and suppressed hypothalamic–pituitary TRH/TSH signaling. The net result is low fT3, low-to-normal fT4, and a TSH that is inappropriately normal or mildly suppressed — a pattern that can superficially resemble central hypothyroidism [20, meta_analysis; 34, mechanism_review].

**Prognostic signal.** Low fT3 in illness carries robust prognostic weight across multiple cardiac cohorts:

- **Iervasi et al. (2003), n = 573 cardiac patients:** fT3 was the single strongest predictor of cumulative one-year mortality by Cox regression (HR 3.582, P < 0.0001). Patients with fT3 < 3.1 pmol/L had substantially higher mortality than those with normal fT3 [28, cohort].
- **Sato et al. (2019), n = 911 hospitalized HF patients:** Low fT3 (< 2.3 pg/mL) predicted all-cause death (HR 2.304, 95% CI 1.736–3.058, P < 0.001) and cardiac death (HR 1.926, 95% CI 1.268–2.927) over a median 991-day follow-up [29, cohort].
- **Zhao et al. (2021), n = 594 acute decompensated HF patients:** Low T3 syndrome was independently associated with 1-year all-cause mortality (adjusted HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.21–2.82, P = 0.005) [30, cohort].
- **Asai et al. (2020), n = 1,190 acute HF patients:** Low fT3 independently predicted 365-day mortality (HR 1.429, 95% CI 1.013–2.015) and HF events [31, cohort].
- **Vidart et al. (2022), meta-analysis of 25 studies, n = 6,869 critically ill patients:** NTIS was independently associated with in-hospital mortality (pooled OR 2.21, 95% CI 1.64–2.97) [20, meta_analysis].

**Caution on interpretation.** This prognostic signal does not imply a causal path from low fT3 to death, nor does it establish that T3 replacement improves outcomes. Low fT3 in critical illness is best understood as a **marker of illness severity** — the sicker the patient, the more depressed the deiodinase system. No large RCT has demonstrated that T3 or T4 supplementation in NTIS reduces mortality. The adaptive-vs-maladaptive debate remains unsettled: while the low-T3 state may be physiologically adaptive (protein sparing), prolonged critical-illness NTIS is increasingly characterized as a maladaptive neuroendocrine failure [20, meta_analysis]. Current guidelines from major endocrine societies do not recommend T3 or T4 supplementation for NTIS outside of specific investigational settings.

### The LT4 + LT3 Combination-Therapy Debate

A clinically relevant consequence of the serum-tissue fT3 disconnect emerges in levothyroxine (LT4)-treated hypothyroid patients. The thyroid normally secretes both T4 and T3 in a ~20:1 molar ratio; LT4 monotherapy relies entirely on peripheral deiodination to supply T3. In the athyreotic patient, this conversion may be insufficient to restore normal serum fT3, even when TSH is well-controlled — a finding documented in studies of post-thyroidectomy patients.

The **DIO2 Thr92Ala polymorphism** — present in ~12–16% of the population — reduces D2 activity and has been associated with impaired psychological well-being on LT4 monotherapy and differential response to combination LT4+LT3 (liothyronine) therapy. In genotyping studies, preference for combination therapy rose from 42% in non-carriers to 100% in homozygous carriers [32, mechanism_review].

Despite this biologically plausible mechanism, **RCT evidence for combination therapy has been largely neutral.** A meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials found no significant difference between LT4 monotherapy and combination therapy on quality-of-life, mood, or cognitive outcomes. Wiersinga (2021) characterizes this as "an unsolved problem of increasing magnitude" and cautions that enthusiasm has outpaced evidence [32, mechanism_review]. The 2012 European Thyroid Association guidelines permit combination therapy only on an experimental basis in selected patients with persistent symptoms who have confirmed euthyroid TSH and no comorbid explanation. The 2014 ATA guidelines (Jonklaas et al.) similarly affirm LT4 as the standard of care while acknowledging ongoing uncertainty about combination therapy for a subset of patients [18, regulatory].

**The clinical relevance of fT3 in this context:** patients on LT4 with persistent hypothyroid symptoms and low-normal fT3 (with normal TSH and fT4) form the candidate population for this debate. fT3 measurement is one of the tools used to identify this pattern, though it is not currently a decision-making criterion in mainstream guidelines.

### Factors That Alter fT3

**Raise fT3:**
- Graves' disease, toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma
- Exogenous T3 (liothyronine, desiccated thyroid extract)
- T3-toxicosis (early/relapsing Graves', toxic nodule)
- Recovery phase of NTI (rebound deiodination)
- hCG stimulation (gestational thyrotoxicosis, hyperemesis)

**Lower fT3:**
- Late / severe primary hypothyroidism
- Non-thyroidal illness / NTIS (any acute or chronic illness)
- Caloric restriction and fasting — a 53% drop in serum T3 was observed with total fasting, with rT3 rising reciprocally; diets providing ≥ 50 g carbohydrate daily did not significantly alter T3 [33, rct]
- **Drugs inhibiting type 1 deiodinase (D1):** propylthiouracil (PTU, a specific uncompetitive D1 inhibitor), amiodarone (strong D1 inhibitor in liver, routinely lowers T3 and raises rT3), glucocorticoids (suppress TRH/TSH and D1 activity), beta-blockers (propranolol inhibits peripheral T3 production by a partially characterized mechanism distinct from beta-blockade per se), and iodinated radiographic contrast agents (iopanoic acid, sodium ipodate — competitive inhibitors of D1 and D2)

### Assay Limitations and Interpretive Pitfalls

Several factors limit the clinical utility of fT3 beyond its core hyperthyroid application:

- **Assay imprecision at low concentrations:** fT3 circulates at roughly 1/40th the total T3 concentration (~2–6 pg/mL / 3.1–9.2 pmol/L). Most immunoassay platforms have coefficients of variation that are disproportionately large near the lower reference bound, reducing confidence in borderline results.
- **NTI confounding:** Because low fT3 is ubiquitous in acute illness, an fT3 result in a hospitalized or acutely unwell patient reflects NTI physiology at least as much as thyroid status. Interpreting fT3 in isolation without clinical context risks misdiagnosis.
- **Poor serum-to-tissue correlation:** Circulating fT3 is a surrogate for tissue thyroid hormone action, not a direct measure of it. Intracellular T3 depends on local deiodinase activity, transporter expression (MCT10, MCT8), and receptor isoform distribution — none of which are captured by serum fT3.
- **Not indicated for routine hypothyroid workup:** The 2014 ATA guidelines explicitly exclude fT3 from hypothyroid diagnosis and standard monitoring [18, regulatory]. Ordering fT3 reflexively in hypothyroid patients is a common practice that adds cost without changing management in the vast majority of cases.
