# TSH (Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone): Canonical Research Report

## Summary

Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH, thyrotropin) is the single most sensitive first-line test of thyroid status. Produced by thyrotroph cells of the anterior pituitary under hypothalamic thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) drive, TSH regulates all steps of thyroid hormone biosynthesis and secretion. Its pivotal diagnostic utility rests on the **log-linear inverse relationship between TSH and free T4 (fT4)**: across the physiological range, a small proportional decrease in circulating fT4 produces a disproportionately large amplified rise in TSH — roughly a two- to threefold TSH shift per 1 pmol/L change in fT4 [4, cohort]. Because the pituitary thyrotroph integrates thyroid hormone exposure over hours to days rather than responding to instantaneous levels, TSH rises or falls well before fT4 becomes overtly abnormal in early thyroid dysfunction [3, mechanism_review]. This amplification makes TSH a far more powerful screening signal than peripheral thyroid hormone measurements alone.

The widely applied adult population reference interval is approximately **0.4–4.0 mIU/L** (some assays report 4.5 mIU/L as the upper limit). TSH follows a **log-normal (right-skewed) distribution** in healthy populations [9, cohort]; the geometric mean in NHANES III was 1.40 mIU/L. Two important modifiers bound all interpretations: (1) the upper reference limit **rises substantially with age**, reaching a 97.5th-percentile of ~7.49 mIU/L in adults ≥80 years [14, cohort], so that ~70% of older individuals with TSH >4.5 mIU/L are within their age-appropriate range; (2) **pregnancy requires trimester-specific ranges** (first trimester: 0.1–2.5 mIU/L by older ATA 2011 guidance; 2017 ATA guidelines prefer locally derived population-specific limits, with a pragmatic fallback of non-pregnant upper limit minus 0.5 mU/L) [15, regulatory].

The upper-limit debate remains clinically active. The 2003 NACB practice guidelines proposed narrowing the upper limit to 2.5 mIU/L on the grounds that autoimmune thyroid disease contaminates standard reference cohorts [10, regulatory]; most major endocrine societies, including the 2012 AACE/ATA joint guidelines, have declined to adopt this cutoff as a treatment threshold given the lack of clear benefit from treating mild subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) in most populations [12, regulatory].

TSH must be interpreted alongside fT4 in any evaluation of possible hypothyroidism. The critical exception — **central (secondary/tertiary) hypothyroidism** — produces a paradoxically low or inappropriately normal TSH paired with low fT4, a pattern that TSH alone will miss or misinterpret. TSH also has a **4–8 week lag** after thyroid hormone dose changes; premature rechecks yield misleading values [26, regulatory]. Modern third-generation immunoassays (functional sensitivity ≤0.01–0.02 mIU/L) support detection of fully suppressed TSH [17, regulatory]; however, several interference patterns — particularly high-dose biotin (≥5 mg/day) on streptavidin-based platforms (falsely low TSH), and heterophile antibodies or macro-TSH (falsely elevated TSH) — can generate results that mimic primary thyroid disease [20, mechanism_review].

## Physiology & The HPT Axis

### TSH: Molecular Identity

Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), also called thyrotropin, is a heterodimeric glycoprotein of approximately 28 kDa produced by thyrotroph cells of the anterior pituitary [1, mechanism_review]. Like the other pituitary glycoprotein hormones — luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) — TSH is composed of two non-covalently linked polypeptide subunits: a common α-subunit shared across this family and a hormone-specific β-subunit that confers binding selectivity for the TSH receptor (TSH-R) on thyroid follicular cells [1, mechanism_review]. Both subunits carry asparagine-linked oligosaccharide chains: two on the α-subunit (at Asn-52 and Asn-78) and one on the β-subunit (at Asn-23) [2, mechanism_review]. These carbohydrates account for roughly 15% of the molecule's mass and are essential for correct folding, heterodimerization, protection from intracellular degradation, circulatory half-life, and full biological activity. TRH is specifically required for proper glycosylation of TSH; when TRH is deficient (as in central hypothyroidism), circulating TSH measured by immunoassay may appear normal but is biologically subpotent [1, mechanism_review]. Mature TSH is sulfated and sialylated at the termini of its oligosaccharide chains, modifications that regulate both receptor affinity and metabolic clearance rate [2, mechanism_review].

### The Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Thyroid (HPT) Axis

TSH production is governed by a three-tier neuroendocrine circuit: the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis [3, mechanism_review]. The conceptual framework for this circuit was established across seven decades of pituitary and thyroid research, with key milestones including the isolation of TRH, the cloning of the TSH-R, and the development of sensitive TSH immunoassays summarized in the historical perspective by Hershman and Beck-Peccoz [7, mechanism_review].

**Hypothalamic drive.** Parvocellular neurons in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus synthesize thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), a tripeptide that travels via the hypothalamic-hypophyseal portal system to the anterior pituitary [3, mechanism_review]. TRH binds Gq-coupled receptors on thyrotrophs, activating phospholipase C, generating IP3 and DAG, mobilizing intracellular calcium, and ultimately driving TSH gene transcription, synthesis, and secretion [1, mechanism_review]. TRH is also obligatory for proper TSH glycosylation; without it, bioactive TSH output falls even if immunoreactive levels appear preserved [1, mechanism_review].

**Pituitary output.** Thyrotrophs respond to TRH by releasing TSH into the systemic circulation. TSH then acts on the basolateral membrane of thyroid follicular cells via TSH-R, stimulating all steps of thyroid hormone biosynthesis and secretion: iodide uptake, thyroid peroxidase-mediated organification, thyroglobulin synthesis, and proteolytic release of T4 and T3 [3, mechanism_review]. The thyroid secretes predominantly thyroxine (T4), with triiodothyronine (T3) representing roughly 20% of direct glandular output; the majority of circulating T3 arises from peripheral deiodination of T4 by type 1 and type 2 deiodinases in liver, kidney, and other tissues [3, mechanism_review].

**Negative feedback.** The axis is closed by a well-characterized negative feedback loop. Circulating free T4 (fT4) and free T3 (fT3) inhibit both TRH secretion at the hypothalamus (where tanycytes convert T4 to T3 via type 2 deiodinase, and the resulting T3 suppresses proTRH gene expression via thyroid hormone receptor-β2) and TSH secretion at the pituitary (where intrapituitary T4-to-T3 conversion also occurs) [3, mechanism_review]. T3 is the predominant intracellular effector of TSH suppression; its affinity for the thyroid hormone receptor is approximately 10-fold greater than that of T4 [1, mechanism_review]. Additional modulatory inputs include dopamine and somatostatin (both inhibitory) and leptin signaling (stimulatory), but TRH stimulation and thyroid hormone negative feedback remain the dominant controls under physiological conditions [3, mechanism_review].

### The Log-Linear Inverse Relationship: Why TSH Is the Most Sensitive Thyroid Test

The central quantitative feature of the HPT axis is the **log-linear inverse relationship between TSH and free T4**: across the physiological range, a relatively small proportional change in circulating fT4 produces a disproportionately large, amplified change in TSH in the opposite direction [4, cohort]. Specifically, when log(TSH) is plotted against fT4, the relationship is approximately linear — a 1 pmol/L decrease in fT4 within the reference range is associated with a two- to threefold rise in TSH concentration [4, cohort].

This amplification is the mechanistic basis for TSH being the single most sensitive first-line test of thyroid status. Because the pituitary thyrotroph acts as a biological integrator that continuously monitors circulating fT4, even subtle departures from an individual's homeostatic set point — departures too small to shift fT4 outside the broad population reference interval — produce detectable TSH excursions. In practice, TSH rises or falls well before fT4 becomes overtly abnormal in early thyroid dysfunction [3, mechanism_review].

It is important to note that while the log-TSH/fT4 relationship is approximately linear within an individual, cross-sectional population data reveal a more complex, nonlinear curve shaped by the superposition of many individual set points at different positions within the population range [4, cohort]. The within-person relationship is predominantly log-linear in those with the most longitudinal data points [4, cohort].

### Pulsatile and Circadian Secretion

TSH is not secreted continuously. It is released in a **pulsatile** pattern with a pulse frequency of approximately 10–12 bursts per 24 hours (roughly one pulse every 2 hours) and an amplitude of approximately 0.6 mIU/L per pulse under basal conditions [5, cohort]. Superimposed on this ultradian rhythm is a pronounced **circadian pattern**: TSH concentrations rise progressively through the late afternoon and evening, reach a nocturnal surge peaking between 21:00 and 02:00, and fall to a nadir in the mid-afternoon [5, cohort]. The difference between afternoon nadir and nocturnal peak is typically 1–3 mIU/L in healthy euthyroid adults.

Sleep onset normally blunts any further TSH rise; when sleep is deprived, TSH can climb to 5–10 mIU/L [1, mechanism_review]. The nocturnal TSH surge is not fully reflected in peripheral thyroid hormone concentrations because the TSH molecules secreted at night are more heavily sulfated and therefore less bioactive than daytime TSH [1, mechanism_review]. The circadian rhythm of TSH is generated partly by varying dopaminergic tone and pulsatile TRH input, and is maintained in mild thyroid dysfunction but abolished in severe hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism [1, mechanism_review]. For clinical sampling, this circadian variability is a reason to standardize collection timing; most reference intervals are established from morning draws.

Van der Spoel et al. corroborated and quantified the diurnal amplitude: the mean within-person diurnal swing is approximately 140% of the nadir value, with a peak roughly 2.4-fold higher than the trough; approximately 13 pulsatile TSH bursts occur per 24 hours (mean pulse mass ~0.90 mU/L, duration ~20 minutes) [23, cohort].

### The Individual Set Point: Population Range vs. Personal Range

The commonly cited population TSH reference interval (approximately 0.4–4.0 mIU/L) reflects substantial **between-person** variation arising from genetic, environmental, and age-related differences in HPT axis calibration. Within a single healthy individual, however, TSH varies across a much narrower personal range [6, cohort]. Andersen et al. demonstrated, in a 12-month longitudinal study of 16 healthy men with monthly sampling, that each person's intra-individual 95% confidence interval for TSH was approximately half the width of the population reference interval [6, cohort]. The index of individuality for TSH (the ratio of within-person to between-person variance) was 0.49 — well below 1.4, the threshold at which population reference intervals perform well for individual surveillance [6, cohort]. A single result within the broad population reference interval is therefore insufficient to confirm that an individual is at their personal set point.

TSH ultimately indexes whether thyroid hormone availability is **sufficient at the tissue level for that individual**. The thyrotroph integrates the history of fT4 exposure over hours to days, not merely the instantaneous fT4 level, making TSH a more reliable chronic signal of thyroid sufficiency than a spot fT4 measurement. Because an individual's set point is genetically influenced and reproducible over time [5, cohort], a shift in TSH away from that person's habitual range carries clinical significance even when the absolute value remains within the population reference interval [6, cohort].

## Reference Ranges, Units & Thresholds

### Units

TSH is reported in **milli-international units per litre (mIU/L)**, which is numerically equivalent to **µIU/mL** (micro-international units per millilitre). The two notations are interchangeable; older literature and some US laboratory reports use µIU/mL while most current guidelines use mIU/L.

### Standard Adult Reference Interval

The most widely applied adult reference interval for TSH is approximately **0.4–4.0 mIU/L** (some assays report an upper limit of 4.5 mIU/L). The precise limits are both assay-dependent and population-dependent; each laboratory is expected to validate its own interval against a locally derived reference cohort [8, meta_analysis].

The foundational US population data come from NHANES III (1988–1994), a nationally representative survey of 17,353 individuals aged ≥12 years [9, cohort]. Excluding those with known thyroid disease, goitre, or thyroid medications yielded a disease-free population of ~16,533 participants; after further restriction to exclude individuals with thyroid antibodies or biochemical thyroid dysfunction, a reference subset of 13,344 remained [9, cohort]. In that reference subset the **geometric mean TSH was 1.40 ± 0.02 mIU/L**, with a notably higher value in white non-Hispanics (1.45 mIU/L) compared with Black Americans (1.18 mIU/L) and Mexican Americans (1.37 mIU/L) [9, cohort]. The use of a geometric mean reflects a defining feature of TSH measurement: the hormone follows a **log-normal (right-skewed) distribution** in healthy populations. Raw TSH values pile up at lower concentrations and have a long right tail; log-transformation normalises the distribution, and the back-transformed geometric mean approximates the true median. This has two practical consequences — the arithmetic mean exceeds the median, and reference intervals derived without log-transformation will have asymmetric tails [9, cohort].

### The Upper-Limit Debate

**The case for a lower upper limit (~2.5 mIU/L).** In 2003, the National Academy of Clinical Biochemistry (NACB) published laboratory practice guidelines recommending that the upper reference limit for TSH be revised downward [10, regulatory]. The rationale was that "greater than 95% of rigorously screened normal euthyroid volunteers" had TSH concentrations between 0.4 and 2.5 mU/L, and that the conventional limit of 4.0–4.5 mIU/L reflected contamination of reference cohorts by individuals with undiagnosed occult autoimmune thyroid disease (subclinical Hashimoto thyroiditis) [10, regulatory]. Wartofsky and Dickey elaborated the same argument in a 2005 JCEM commentary, noting that African-American populations — who have substantially lower rates of Hashimoto thyroiditis — cluster at lower TSH values, and that when autoimmune-positive individuals are excluded the upper end of the distribution contracts toward 2.5 mIU/L [11, mechanism_review].

**The case against narrowing the range.** The counter-position has two arms: empirical and clinical. Empirically, the upper reference limit is not a fixed biological constant — it drifts upward substantially with age (see below), varies by ethnicity, and shifts with iodine status and assay methodology [8, meta_analysis]. A single narrow range applied uniformly ignores these sources of variation and risks classifying physiologically normal variation in older adults or certain ethnic groups as disease. Clinically, narrowing the upper limit expands the diagnosis of subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) to a much larger fraction of the population without a corresponding evidence base for treatment benefit. The 2012 joint AACE/ATA clinical practice guidelines for hypothyroidism in adults retained a conventional TSH upper limit and explicitly declined to endorse the 2.5 mIU/L cutoff as a treatment threshold, noting that mild TSH elevations (4.5–9.9 mU/L) have uncertain benefit when treated [12, regulatory]. A 2021 clinical debate illustrates the remaining controversy: the pro-treatment side cites cardiovascular and fertility data; the anti-treatment side cites spontaneous reversion in ~60% of mild SCH cases within five years and lack of symptom improvement in most randomised trials [13, mechanism_review].

The current pragmatic consensus, reflected in most major society guidelines, is that the conventional range (~0.4–4.5 mIU/L) remains the operational standard, but clinicians should factor in age, autoimmune status, and clinical context when interpreting values near the upper boundary.

### Age-Specific Drift

TSH reference distributions shift upward progressively with age, even in individuals without thyroid disease or thyroid antibodies. Surks and Hollowell used the NHANES III reference population to quantify this shift: the **97.5th-percentile upper reference limit was 3.56 mIU/L in 20-to-29-year-olds**, but rose to **7.49 mIU/L in adults aged ≥80 years** — a more than two-fold increase across six decades [14, cohort]. The corresponding mean TSH rose from 1.43 mIU/L to 2.44 mIU/L (a 69% increase) over the same span [14, cohort]. A subsequent meta-analysis confirmed this pattern across multiple populations, finding that the female TSH upper limit rose notably after age 40, and the male upper limit after age 60 [8, meta_analysis].

The clinical implication is substantial: applying a fixed population-wide upper limit of 4.5 mIU/L to older adults causes systematic over-diagnosis of subclinical hypothyroidism. Surks and Hollowell estimated that **~70% of older individuals with TSH >4.5 mIU/L fell within their age-appropriate reference range**, meaning the great majority of apparent SCH in the elderly represents age-related set-point adjustment rather than pathology [14, cohort].

### Pregnancy: Trimester-Specific Ranges

TSH physiology changes substantially during pregnancy. Human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG), which peaks in the first trimester, cross-reacts with the TSH receptor and stimulates thyroid hormone production, causing TSH to fall — sometimes below the standard non-pregnant lower limit. This physiological suppression is most marked in the first trimester and attenuates as hCG falls in the second and third trimesters.

The **2011 ATA guidelines** recommended fixed trimester-specific limits:
- First trimester: 0.1–2.5 mIU/L
- Second trimester: 0.2–3.0 mIU/L
- Third trimester: 0.3–3.5 mIU/L

The **2017 ATA guidelines** (Alexander, Pearce, et al.) represented a significant revision [15, regulatory]. Recognising that trimester-specific ranges vary by population, ethnicity, iodine status, and assay, the 2017 guidelines recommend using **population- and assay-specific reference intervals derived from local iodine-replete pregnant women** whenever available. The preferred approach is a locally validated reference derived from TPOAb-negative, iodine-sufficient pregnant women [15, regulatory]. Where local population-specific data are unavailable, the 2017 ATA guidelines propose a pragmatic fallback: the **upper reference limit is the non-pregnant upper limit minus 0.5 mU/L** (yielding an upper limit of approximately 4.0 mU/L in the first trimester), rather than the prior fixed 2.5 mIU/L cutoff [15, regulatory]. This change reflected accumulating evidence that the 2.5 mIU/L first-trimester upper limit was too restrictive for many populations and was leading to over-diagnosis and over-treatment of hypothyroidism in pregnancy. The core principle remains: pregnancy TSH targets are **lower than non-pregnant targets**, most critically in the first trimester when fetal thyroid function is not yet established and maternal thyroid hormone supply is the sole source.

### Diagnostic Patterns by TSH Result

| Pattern | TSH | Free T4 | Free T3 | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Subclinical hypothyroidism | Above reference range | Normal | Normal | Compensated early thyroid failure; pituitary senses sub-threshold thyroid output |
| Overt hypothyroidism | Elevated (often >10 mIU/L) | Low | (Low) | Frank thyroid failure; TSH typically markedly elevated |
| Subclinical hyperthyroidism | Below reference range (<0.4 mIU/L); Grade 1: 0.1–0.39; Grade 2: <0.1 | Normal | Normal | Excess thyroid activity suppresses TSH without yet raising hormone levels |
| Overt hyperthyroidism | Suppressed (<0.1 mIU/L) | Elevated | Elevated | Frank thyrotoxicosis |
| Central hypothyroidism | Low or low-normal | Low | — | Pituitary/hypothalamic failure; TSH cannot be interpreted alone |

Subclinical hypothyroidism is formally defined by a serum TSH above the statistically defined upper reference limit in combination with a free T4 within the normal range, provided the HPT axis is intact and the measurement is not taken during acute illness or in the immediate post-operative period [12, regulatory]. Subclinical hyperthyroidism is the mirror image: TSH below the reference lower limit with normal free T4 and free T3 [16, regulatory].

## Measurement & Interferences

### Assay Design and Generations

Modern TSH measurement relies on third-generation immunometric sandwich assays. The operating principle is straightforward: two antibodies directed at distinct TSH epitopes bracket the analyte — one capture antibody immobilizes the complex on a solid phase, and one signal antibody generates a detectable readout. Because signal is proportional to analyte concentration, sandwich formats produce high dynamic range, and the use of two high-affinity monoclonal or polyclonal antibodies confers the specificity needed for a hormone with complex glycoform heterogeneity.

The evolution of TSH assays across three generations was organized around a single quantitative criterion: **functional sensitivity**, defined as the lowest TSH concentration measured with a between-run coefficient of variation (CV) ≤20%, established over a 6–8 week period [17, regulatory]. This metric is more stringent than the analytical limit of detection because it accounts for the day-to-day imprecision that governs clinical use. Nicoloff and Spencer formalized the generational framework, with each successive generation representing a tenfold improvement in functional sensitivity [18, mechanism_review]:

- **First generation** (radioimmunoassays, ~1960s–early 1980s): functional sensitivity ~1–2 mIU/L; adequate only for detecting overt hypothyroidism.
- **Second generation** (immunoradiometric assays, IRMA; ~1986): functional sensitivity ~0.1 mIU/L; first assays capable of distinguishing suppressed from normal TSH.
- **Third generation** (non-isotopic immunometric assays, ~1990s–present): functional sensitivity ≤0.01–0.02 mIU/L; the current standard worldwide [17, regulatory].

Third-generation assays are predominantly **chemiluminescent** (immunochemiluminometric assay, ICMA) or **electrochemiluminescent** (ECLIA, as in Roche Elecsys platforms). The Roche ECLIA system uses a ruthenium-based luminophore triggered by electrochemical voltage; Siemens ADVIA Centaur uses an acridinium ester. Abbott ARCHITECT and Beckman Access platforms use direct chemiluminescence. The functional sensitivity of third-generation ICMAs in routine use is typically 0.005–0.02 mIU/L [19, mechanism_review].

### Interferences: Falsely LOW TSH

**Biotin (High-Dose Supplements).** The most clinically important and now prevalent source of falsely low TSH is **exogenous biotin** taken at pharmacological doses (≥5 mg/day for cosmetic or neurological applications; doses up to 300 mg/day are used in multiple sclerosis protocols). Biotin is a small molecule that binds streptavidin with femtomolar affinity — exactly the binding pair exploited by biotin-streptavidin immunoassay platforms (Roche Elecsys; estimated >50% of global immunoassay reagents use this system) [20, mechanism_review].

The mechanism: excess circulating biotin competes with the biotinylated capture antibody for streptavidin binding sites on the solid phase. In a **sandwich** assay (TSH), this competition reduces the capture efficiency of the antibody-TSH complex, shrinking the signal and producing a **falsely low TSH**. In a **competitive** assay (fT4, fT3), the same competition displaces label from binding sites and produces a **falsely elevated** result. The combined pattern — suppressed TSH with elevated fT4/fT3 — exactly mimics Graves' disease [21, cohort]. A 63-year-old woman taking 300 mg/day biotin for multiple sclerosis presented with this biochemical picture; normalization within three days of stopping biotin confirmed assay interference [21, cohort]. Biotin concentrations of ≥20–50 µg/L have been shown to generate interference on Roche platforms, and pharmacological doses routinely exceed this threshold [21, cohort].

**Practical guidance:** Patients should stop biotin supplements for a minimum of 48–72 hours before thyroid testing. The interference is platform-specific; Abbott, Siemens, and Beckman platforms using direct (non-streptavidin) chemiluminescence are substantially less affected.

**Anti-Streptavidin Antibodies.** A minority of patients develop **endogenous anti-streptavidin antibodies** — immunoglobulins that bind the streptavidin layer directly. Their effect mirrors exogenous biotin: they block biotinylated antibody binding and produce falsely low TSH and falsely high fT4/fT3. These antibodies persist for 18–24 months or longer, and unlike biotin, cannot be removed by supplement cessation [20, mechanism_review]. Detection requires testing on a non-streptavidin platform.

### Interferences: Falsely HIGH TSH

**Heterophile and Human Anti-Animal Antibodies (HAAAs).** Heterophile antibodies — broadly reactive immunoglobulins against animal protein epitopes, often stimulated by prior exposure to animal proteins (via pet contact, therapeutic monoclonal antibodies, or dietary exposure) — cross-bridge the capture and signal antibodies in a sandwich TSH assay, generating a falsely elevated signal even in the absence of TSH antigen. Human anti-mouse antibodies (HAMA) are the most common HAAA subtype; because many immunoassay antibodies are murine, HAMA are particularly disruptive to TSH measurement [20, mechanism_review]. Estimated interference rate is 0.05–6% depending on assay, with TSH-specific rates near 0.4%. The effect is almost always falsely **elevated** TSH.

A striking example: a single post-thyroidectomy patient showed TSH values of 5.52 µIU/mL on Siemens, 0.54 on Abbott, 0.12 on Roche, and <0.015 on Beckman — reflecting highly platform-specific HAAA affinity [22, cohort]. Heterophilic antibody blocker pretreatment collapsed all values toward zero, confirming interference. Rheumatoid factor (anti-IgG IgM) can similarly bridge detection antibodies, and its prevalence in autoimmune thyroid disease patients creates a clinical trap. Detection strategies include serial dilution testing (non-linearity across dilutions), heterophilic blocking tubes (HBT), and parallel testing on a second platform.

**Macro-TSH.** Macro-TSH refers to high-molecular-weight TSH circulating as an immune complex — TSH bound to anti-TSH IgG autoantibodies. Prevalence is approximately 0.6–1.6% of patients with persistently elevated TSH who are otherwise asymptomatic [20, mechanism_review]. Because the complex is TSH-antigen-positive but biologically inert, the TSH assay reads it as elevated TSH while fT4 and fT3 remain normal. This pattern exactly mimics subclinical hypothyroidism and has led to inappropriate levothyroxine initiation in documented cases. Detection by polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation — which preferentially precipitates large immune complexes — or gel filtration chromatography separates macro-TSH from authentic free TSH.

**Anti-Ruthenium Antibodies.** Endogenous antibodies targeting the ruthenium chelate used as the electrochemiluminescent signal molecule in Roche ECLIA platforms can produce variable interference — most commonly falsely elevated TSH. Prevalence is <0.1–0.24% [20, mechanism_review]. These are detected by switching to a non-ruthenium platform.

### Non-Thyroidal Illness (Sick Euthyroid / NTIS)

In patients with acute or severe systemic illness, TSH follows a characteristic biphasic pattern that is not reflective of intrinsic thyroid dysfunction. During the acute phase of critical illness, inflammatory cytokines, elevated cortisol, and dopamine all suppress TSH at the level of the thyrotroph and hypothalamus; serum TSH may fall below 0.1 mIU/L in up to 3% of acutely ill hospitalized patients, even in the absence of true hyperthyroidism [20, mechanism_review]. The parallel fall in T3 (conversion to reverse-T3 replaces the deiodination pathway) and T4 completes the "sick euthyroid" pattern.

During **recovery**, TSH may rebound transiently above the reference range — sometimes into a range suggesting subclinical or overt hypothyroidism — before returning to normal. Measuring TSH during the rebound phase of an acute illness without knowledge of the preceding course has led to unnecessary levothyroxine initiation. The clinical rule: **interpret TSH only in the context of the acute illness trajectory**, and confirm any abnormal TSH with a repeat measurement after recovery unless there is compelling reason to act immediately.

### Diurnal Variation and Sampling Timing

TSH secretion follows a robust circadian rhythm. The nocturnal surge peaks between 02:00–04:00 h; the daytime nadir falls between 07:00–14:00 h. The mean diurnal amplitude is approximately 140% of the nadir value — a peak roughly 2.4-fold higher than the trough — with approximately 13 pulsatile TSH bursts per 24 hours (mean pulse mass ~0.90 mU/L, duration ~20 minutes) [23, cohort].

In healthy individuals with TSH near the population midpoint, the diurnal swing does not typically produce diagnostic misclassification. However, for values near the upper or lower reference limits — the zones where subclinical hypo- or hyperthyroidism is considered — sampling time can shift a borderline result into or out of the normal range. Most reference intervals are derived from morning fasting samples; TSH values drawn in the late afternoon should be interpreted with this in mind.

### Drug Effects on TSH

Several drug classes suppress TSH through central mechanisms and can produce a biochemical picture of central hypothyroidism when the cause is not recognized [24, mechanism_review]:

- **Dopamine and dopamine agonists** suppress TSH via D2 receptor activation on thyrotrophs, inhibiting both TRH signaling and Tshb transcription. Infused dopamine at ICU doses (≥2 µg/kg/min) reliably suppresses TSH. This compounds the NTIS-related suppression in critically ill patients.
- **Glucocorticoids** at high doses suppress hypothalamic pro-TRH mRNA and blunt TSH secretion transiently; chronic physiologic replacement doses do not cause significant TSH suppression.
- **Somatostatin analogues** and **rexinoids** (bexarotene) can cause clinically significant central hypothyroidism [24, mechanism_review].
- **Amiodarone** is a special case: its high iodine content inhibits thyroid hormone synthesis (Wolff-Chaikoff effect) and may cause true hypo- or hyperthyroidism, not only a laboratory artifact.

### TSH–fT4 Discordance Patterns

The log-linear inverse relationship between TSH and fT4 is load-bearing for interpretation. When this relationship is violated, specific diagnoses must be considered:

| Pattern | Likely cause |
|---------|-------------|
| High TSH, low fT4 | Primary hypothyroidism (the canonical pattern) |
| Low TSH, high fT4 | Primary hyperthyroidism or exogenous T4 excess |
| Low TSH, low/normal fT4 | Central hypothyroidism; NTIS acute phase; dopamine/glucocorticoid suppression |
| Normal/high TSH, high fT4 | Assay interference (biotin, anti-Ru); TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma; thyroid hormone resistance |
| High TSH, normal fT4 | Subclinical hypothyroidism; recovery phase NTIS; macro-TSH; heterophile antibody interference |

Central hypothyroidism deserves particular note: in this condition the log-linear relationship is dissolved because the pituitary produces biologically less potent TSH isoforms; serum fT4 — not TSH — becomes the monitoring parameter for levothyroxine adequacy.

### Assay Standardization and Harmonization

TSH immunoassays are not harmonized across analytical platforms — no commutable certified reference material is in routine use for TSH, meaning assay-specific biases cannot be eliminated by simple recalibration to a common standard. The WHO International Standard for TSH (e.g. IS 80/558 and its successors) serves as a reference preparation for biological potency assignment, but method-specific differences persist because commercial assays use different antibody pairs, signal chemistries, and calibration approaches. Consequently, TSH results and their associated reference intervals are method-dependent and are not freely interchangeable across laboratories or platforms; a value of 3.8 mIU/L on one system does not carry the same clinical meaning as 3.8 mIU/L on another [25, mechanism_review]. The IFCC Committee for Standardization of Thyroid Function Tests (C-STFT) has documented between-assay biases of up to ~39% across major platforms and continues to pursue harmonization through patient-sample-based calibration traceability, though full commutability-based standardization remains an unresolved goal [25, mechanism_review].

## Determinants & Clinical Significance

### TSH as First-Line Screening Test

Serum TSH is the recommended initial test for evaluating thyroid function in virtually all outpatient clinical scenarios [12, regulatory]. Because TSH responds exponentially to small changes in circulating thyroid hormone — a 2-fold change in free T4 produces a 100-fold shift in TSH — the pituitary integrates thyroid hormone exposure over weeks and provides a far more sensitive signal than peripheral hormone levels alone [26, regulatory]. Clinical practice follows a reflex-testing model: an abnormal TSH triggers automatic add-on measurement of free T4 (fT4), and in select cases free T3 (fT3), to characterize the direction and severity of dysfunction.

- **Overt hypothyroidism:** TSH markedly elevated (often > 10 mIU/L) + low fT4.
- **Subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH):** TSH mildly elevated (typically 4.5–9.9 mIU/L) + fT4 within the reference range.
- **Overt hyperthyroidism:** TSH suppressed (usually < 0.1 mIU/L) + elevated fT4 and/or fT3.
- **Subclinical hyperthyroidism:** TSH suppressed + normal fT4 and fT3.

### Elevated TSH: What It Means and Predicts

**Primary Hypothyroidism.** Elevated TSH with low fT4 defines overt primary hypothyroidism, most commonly caused by autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto's). The TSH rise precedes fT4 suppression, which is why mild TSH elevation with preserved fT4 defines the subclinical stage.

**Subclinical Hypothyroidism: Cardiovascular Risk.** Whether SCH carries clinically meaningful cardiovascular risk depends strongly on the degree of TSH elevation. The Thyroid Studies Collaboration individual-participant data (IPD) meta-analysis — Rodondi et al., 11 prospective cohorts, 55,287 participants, 542,494 person-years — demonstrated a graded TSH-dependent increase in coronary heart disease (CHD) risk [27, meta_analysis]:

| TSH level (mIU/L) | CHD events HR (95% CI) | CHD mortality HR (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|
| 4.5–6.9 | 1.00 (0.86–1.18) | 1.09 (0.91–1.30) |
| 7.0–9.9 | 1.17 (0.96–1.43) | 1.42 (1.03–1.95) |
| 10–19.9 | **1.89 (1.28–2.80)** | **1.58 (1.10–2.27)** |

The clearest signal emerged at TSH ≥ 10 mIU/L, where CHD event risk was approximately doubled. No significant increase in all-cause mortality was observed across the SCH range as a whole. These findings underpin the clinical consensus that TSH ≥ 10 mIU/L is a stronger treatment trigger than mild SCH in younger adults.

**The Treatment Controversy: The TRUST Trial.** Despite the epidemiological associations, the pivotal TRUST (Thyroid hormone Replacement for Untreated older adults with Subclinical hypothyroidism — a randomised placebo controlled Trial) provides the strongest evidence against routine levothyroxine replacement in older individuals with SCH [28, rct]. In 737 adults aged ≥65 years (mean age 74.4) with persistent SCH (TSH 4.60–19.99 mIU/L), participants were randomized to levothyroxine dose-titration to normalize TSH vs. placebo. After 1 year, the primary outcome — Hypothyroid Symptom score and Tiredness score — showed no difference between groups (between-group difference: −0.2 points, 95% CI −1.5 to 1.0, and 0.2 points, 95% CI −1.0 to 1.5 respectively). There were no significant differences in secondary cardiovascular outcomes, quality of life, or muscle strength. The TRUST trial materially shifted clinical practice: in older patients with mild SCH, routine treatment is not supported by evidence of symptomatic benefit, even when TSH is normalized biochemically.

**Risk stratification summary:** Treatment decisions in SCH weigh TSH level, age, symptom burden, and comorbidities. The TSH ≥ 10 mIU/L threshold — particularly in younger patients — retains the strongest case for treatment. In those > 70 years with TSH 5–10 mIU/L, watchful waiting is the predominant evidence-based approach.

### Suppressed TSH: What It Means and Predicts

**Overt and Subclinical Hyperthyroidism.** TSH suppression to < 0.1 mIU/L with elevated fT4 (and often fT3) defines overt hyperthyroidism, most commonly Graves' disease or a toxic nodular goiter. Subclinical hyperthyroidism (sCHyper) — suppressed TSH with normal fT4/fT3 — can be endogenous (Graves', autonomous nodule) or exogenous (over-replacement with levothyroxine).

**Atrial Fibrillation and Mortality Risk.** The Thyroid Studies Collaboration IPD analysis by Collet et al. (10 cohorts, 52,674 participants) quantified the sCHyper risk for hard cardiovascular endpoints [29, meta_analysis]:

- All-cause mortality: **HR 1.24 (95% CI 1.06–1.46)**
- CHD mortality: **HR 1.29 (95% CI 1.02–1.62)**
- Atrial fibrillation: **HR 1.68 (95% CI 1.16–2.43)**

Risk was concentrated in those with TSH < 0.10 mIU/L, consistent with the Cappola et al. cohort study of 3,233 adults ≥65 years, in which suppressed TSH was associated with atrial fibrillation at 67 vs. 31 events per 1,000 person-years (adjusted HR 1.98) [30, cohort].

**Fracture Risk.** Subclinical hyperthyroidism accelerates bone turnover via direct TSH-receptor-mediated effects on osteoclasts as well as thyroid hormone effects on cortical bone. The Blum et al. IPD meta-analysis (13 cohorts, 70,298 participants) reported [31, meta_analysis]:

- Hip fracture: HR 1.36 (95% CI 1.13–1.64)
- Any fracture: HR 1.28 (95% CI 1.06–1.53)
- Spine fracture: HR 1.51 (95% CI 0.93–2.45)

Among participants with TSH < 0.10 mIU/L, hip fracture risk climbed to HR 1.61 (95% CI 1.21–2.15), any-fracture risk to HR 1.98 (95% CI 1.41–2.78), and spine fracture risk to HR 3.57 (95% CI 1.88–6.78). No significant fracture excess was found with subclinical hypothyroidism. Fracture risk from sCHyper is most clinically relevant in postmenopausal women, in whom cortical bone loss may be additive to estrogen-deficiency remodeling.

### Determinants That Raise TSH

- **Autoimmune (Hashimoto's) thyroiditis:** the leading cause of primary hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient regions; progressive lymphocytic infiltration reduces functional thyroid mass, driving compensatory TSH rise.
- **Iodine deficiency or excess:** Iodine deficiency impairs thyroid hormone synthesis; paradoxically, iodine excess can precipitate hypothyroidism (Wolff-Chaikoff effect), particularly in individuals with pre-existing autoimmune thyroid disease.
- **Recovery from non-thyroidal illness (NTI) / sick euthyroid syndrome:** TSH may transiently rebound above the normal range during the recovery phase after acute illness, even when the steady-state is euthyroid.
- **Drugs:** Lithium inhibits thyroid hormone secretion and release; amiodarone (37% iodine by mass) causes hypothyroidism in 5–22% of recipients, particularly in iodine-sufficient regions; immune-checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1/PD-L1, anti-CTLA-4) trigger autoimmune thyroiditis in up to 15–20% of users; interferon-alpha induces thyroid autoimmunity.
- **Thyroid surgery / radioiodine ablation:** permanent reduction of thyroid mass leads to obligate post-treatment hypothyroidism in most patients.
- **Aging:** the population TSH distribution shifts upward with age; TSH levels of 5–8 mIU/L are common in healthy octogenarians without representing pathological SCH [14, cohort].
- **TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma (rare):** TSH inappropriately normal or elevated in the face of high thyroid hormones — a key diagnostic exception.

### Determinants That Lower TSH

- **Graves' disease / toxic nodule:** stimulatory TSH-receptor antibodies (in Graves') or autonomous thyroid hormone secretion suppress the pituitary TSH axis.
- **Central (secondary) hypothyroidism — the critical exception:** Damage to the hypothalamus or anterior pituitary (tumor, surgery, radiation, Sheehan syndrome) impairs TRH/TSH secretion. The result is a paradoxically low or inappropriately normal TSH paired with low fT4 — the opposite of what the TSH alone would suggest. This pattern requires paired fT4 measurement for diagnosis.
- **Exogenous thyroid hormone over-replacement:** the most common cause of suppressed TSH in clinical practice.
- **Glucocorticoids and dopamine/dobutamine:** suppress TSH secretion at the pituitary level — documented in hospitalized patients on vasopressors or high-dose steroids [24, mechanism_review].
- **First-trimester pregnancy:** placental hCG cross-reacts with the TSH receptor (structural homology), transiently suppressing maternal TSH at nadir around 10–12 weeks; gestational reference ranges apply [15, regulatory].
- **Severe non-thyroidal illness / sick euthyroid syndrome (acute phase):** TSH is suppressed during the acute phase of critical illness, then may rebound above the upper limit during recovery [20, mechanism_review].

### Key Limitations of TSH Measurement

**1. TSH alone misses central hypothyroidism.** In secondary/tertiary (central) hypothyroidism, the pituitary cannot mount an appropriate TSH response to falling thyroid hormones. A TSH in the "normal" or even low range paired with a low fT4 is the diagnostic footprint. Any evaluation of hypothyroidism that stops at TSH without fT4 will falsely reassure in this clinically important minority.

**2. TSH lags acute thyroid changes by 4–6 weeks.** The pituitary thyrotrope population adapts to new steady-state thyroid hormone levels over a period of approximately 4–8 weeks. Remeasuring TSH within 4 weeks of a dose change yields a misleading value that reflects neither the prior nor the new steady state [26, regulatory]. The practical implication: after any levothyroxine dose adjustment, the earliest reliable re-check is at 6–8 weeks, and premature rechecks frequently trigger unnecessary further dose changes.

**3. Diurnal and seasonal variation.** Within-individual variation of approximately 50% around a personal set point — from pulsatile and circadian secretion patterns — makes single out-of-range values most interpretable in the context of a prior personal baseline [23, cohort].
