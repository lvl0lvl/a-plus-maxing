# Serum Potassium — Canonical Research Report

## Summary

Potassium (K⁺) is the principal intracellular cation: approximately 98% of total body potassium (~3,500 mmol in a 70 kg adult) resides inside cells at a concentration of 140–150 mmol/L, while only ~2% occupies the extracellular fluid, maintained within the narrow reference interval of 3.5–5.0 mmol/L [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review]. This steep ~30–40:1 intracellular-to-extracellular gradient, actively defended by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, is the primary determinant of resting membrane potential in excitable cells — which makes potassium the most acutely dangerous common electrolyte. Both hyperkalemia and hypokalemia destabilize the cardiac action potential and can precipitate ventricular fibrillation or asystole. The mortality risk is bidirectional and U-shaped: population data from over a million patients confirm elevated mortality at both low and high extremes, with the nadir near 4.0–4.5 mmol/L [15, meta_analysis; 13, cohort; 14, cohort].

Serum K⁺ is not a measure of total-body potassium stores. Because the extracellular pool is tiny (~70 mmol), large transcellular shifts — driven by acid-base changes, insulin, catecholamines — can produce clinically significant swings in serum concentration without altering whole-body content. Conversely, substantial total-body depletion can exist with serum K⁺ near the lower limit of normal, because intracellular K⁺ leaches out to buffer the falling extracellular pool. Interpreting serum K⁺ always requires integration with renal function (eGFR), acid-base status, magnesium level, the full medication list, and an ECG.

The single most important pre-interpretive step for any elevated result is exclusion of **pseudohyperkalemia** — the most common cause of apparent hyperkalemia referred to clinical laboratories [20, mechanism_review; 21, mechanism_review]. In-vitro hemolysis is the dominant mechanism: each 1 g/L of free hemoglobin raises measured potassium by approximately 0.23–0.33 mmol/L [22, cohort; 23, cohort; 24, cohort], and moderately hemolysed specimens can push a true 4.5 mmol/L into apparent danger-range hyperkalemia. Other forms — fist-clenching, thrombocytosis, delayed analysis, familial pseudohyperkalemia — require awareness and a confirmation hierarchy before treatment.

The central clinical tension in managing hyperkalemia is the RAAS-inhibitor dilemma: the drugs that most powerfully reduce cardiorenal mortality (ACEi, ARBs, mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonists) are exactly those most likely to raise potassium, particularly in patients with CKD, diabetes, or heart failure. Novel potassium binders (patiromer, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate) have changed the management equation by enabling continued or up-titrated RAAS-inhibitor therapy in patients who would previously have had those drugs reduced or stopped [29, rct; 30, rct].

Serum K⁺ is reported in mmol/L, numerically identical to mEq/L (no conversion needed; K⁺ is monovalent). Serum runs ~0.2–0.5 mmol/L higher than plasma because of platelet K⁺ release during clotting; in thrombocytosis the gap widens substantially and plasma measurement is preferred [12, mechanism_review].

## Physiology & What Serum Potassium Measures

### The Principal Intracellular Cation

Potassium is the dominant cation of the intracellular fluid (ICF). Approximately 98% of total body potassium resides inside cells — predominantly in skeletal muscle, liver, and erythrocytes — at an intracellular concentration of ~140–150 mmol/L [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review]. The extracellular fluid (ECF), which includes plasma, holds only ~2% of total body K⁺, maintained within the narrow reference range of 3.5–5.0 mmol/L [1, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review]. Total body potassium is roughly 55 mmol/kg body weight; for a 70 kg adult this is approximately 3,500 mmol, of which only ~70 mmol circulates in the ECF [2, mechanism_review].

This asymmetric distribution produces a steep **~30–40:1 intracellular-to-extracellular concentration gradient** [1, mechanism_review; 4, mechanism_review]. The gradient is actively maintained by the **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** (Na-K pump) — a ubiquitous plasma-membrane enzyme that extrudes 3 Na⁺ ions out of the cell in exchange for 2 K⁺ ions in, consuming one molecule of ATP per cycle [2, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review]. Without the continuous expenditure of energy by the Na-K pump, the gradient dissipates; it is not a passive equilibrium but an actively defended steady state.

### Why Serum K⁺ Is a Small and Unreliable Window on Total-Body Potassium

Because the ECF pool is so small (~70 mmol) relative to the ICF reservoir (~3,430 mmol), **serum K⁺ does not directly reflect total-body potassium stores** [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review]. Two clinically consequential mismatches follow:

1. **Depletion can be masked.** A patient with a large total-body K⁺ deficit — caused by weeks of poor intake, vomiting, or diuretic use — may still present with serum K⁺ near the lower end of the normal range (e.g., 3.6 mmol/L), because K⁺ leaks out of cells to partially buffer the falling ECF pool. The serum value underestimates the severity of the deficit.

2. **Serum K⁺ can shift acutely without any change in body stores.** Acute redistributive processes — acid-base perturbations, hormonal surges, osmotic changes — cause K⁺ to move between the ICF and ECF within minutes, producing a clinically significant change in the serum value while total body content remains constant [2, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review]. These two phenomena — depletion masking and redistributive shifts — define the interpretive challenge of the serum K⁺ measurement.

### The Transmembrane K⁺ Gradient and Resting Membrane Potential

The steep K⁺ gradient is the principal determinant of the **resting membrane potential (Vm)** of excitable cells [3, mechanism_review; 5, mechanism_review]. Because cell membranes are highly permeable to K⁺ at rest (through inwardly rectifying K⁺ channels), K⁺ leaks down its concentration gradient from ICF to ECF, leaving behind a net negative charge inside the cell. This leak drives Vm toward the K⁺ equilibrium potential (approximately −90 mV in cardiac myocytes under normal conditions) [5, mechanism_review].

The Nernst relationship makes the dependence explicit: a fall in extracellular K⁺ (hypokalemia) hyperpolarizes the membrane (Vm becomes more negative), increasing the gap between resting potential and threshold and prolonging repolarization; a rise in extracellular K⁺ (hyperkalemia) depolarizes the membrane (Vm shifts toward zero), moving it closer to threshold and initially increasing excitability before eventually inactivating fast sodium channels and impairing conduction [5, mechanism_review]. Both extremes create conditions permissive for **life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias** — the basis of the U-shaped mortality curve associated with serum K⁺ in cardiovascular disease [5, mechanism_review]. Neuromuscular excitability follows the same physics: severe hypokalemia causes muscle weakness and paralysis; severe hyperkalemia causes muscle paralysis by a different mechanism (persistent membrane depolarization inactivating Na⁺ channels).

### Acute Regulation — Internal/Transcellular Shifts

The first line of defense against transient perturbations in serum K⁺ is the rapid redistribution of K⁺ between the ICF and ECF. This occurs on a timescale of minutes to hours, well before renal adjustments complete. Four main effectors drive transcellular K⁺ shifts [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review]:

**Insulin.** After a potassium-containing meal, the rise in plasma K⁺ directly stimulates pancreatic β-cell insulin secretion. Insulin, independently of its glucose-lowering action, activates the Na-K ATPase in skeletal muscle and liver, driving K⁺ from the ECF into cells and blunting the postprandial rise in serum K⁺ [2, mechanism_review]. This feedforward mechanism ensures that a single mixed meal does not produce dangerous hyperkalemia even before renal excretion has time to act. Clinicians exploit the same biology therapeutically: IV insulin plus glucose is first-line management of acute hyperkalemia precisely because it rapidly shifts K⁺ into cells [2, mechanism_review].

**β₂-Adrenergic catecholamines.** Epinephrine and β₂-agonists (salbutamol, albuterol) stimulate the Na-K ATPase via cyclic AMP, driving K⁺ into cells [2, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review]. Physiologically, catecholamine release during stress (e.g., physical exertion, hemorrhage) prevents K⁺ released from working muscle from reaching dangerous serum levels. Clinically, nebulized salbutamol is an adjunct to insulin for acute hyperkalemia management.

**Acid-base status.** Metabolic acidosis shifts K⁺ OUT of cells into the ECF, raising serum K⁺. The classical explanation is that excess extracellular H⁺ enters cells in exchange for K⁺ (to buffer the intracellular compartment), and that reduced Na-K ATPase activity in acidosis further reduces cellular uptake [3, mechanism_review]. Metabolic alkalosis has the opposite effect, driving K⁺ into cells and lowering serum K⁺. The acid-base/K⁺ coupling is of direct clinical relevance: patients with diabetic ketoacidosis may present with apparently normal or even elevated serum K⁺ despite profound total-body K⁺ depletion, because severe acidosis masks the deficit by shifting K⁺ out of cells; insulin therapy and fluid correction will unmask the deficit as pH normalizes.

**Plasma tonicity/osmolality.** Hyperosmolality (e.g., hyperglycemia) causes water to leave cells osmotically; K⁺ follows the solvent drag, raising serum K⁺ despite constant body stores.

### Chronic Regulation — External Balance and Renal Excretion

While transcellular shifts buffer acute fluctuations, **chronic K⁺ balance is achieved almost entirely by the kidney** [3, mechanism_review; 4, mechanism_review]. The kidney excretes approximately 90% of daily K⁺ intake; the colon handles most of the remainder [3, mechanism_review].

The proximal tubule and loop of Henle reabsorb the bulk of filtered K⁺ (roughly 60–70% and a further fraction, respectively), leaving relatively little K⁺ to reach the distal nephron. Regulation of net excretion is accomplished primarily in the **connecting tubule (CNT) and cortical collecting duct (CCD)**, where principal cells secrete K⁺ in a tightly controlled manner [4, mechanism_review; 6, mechanism_review].

The dominant regulator is **aldosterone**. When serum K⁺ rises even slightly, the adrenal cortex secretes aldosterone, which acts on mineralocorticoid receptors in principal cells to upregulate two transporters in concert: (1) the basolateral Na-K ATPase (which draws Na⁺ into the cell and K⁺ out of the tubular lumen into the cell), and (2) the apical epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) and luminal K⁺ channels (primarily ROMK) [1, mechanism_review; 4, mechanism_review; 6, mechanism_review]. ENaC-mediated Na⁺ reabsorption creates an electronegative lumen that provides the electrical driving force for K⁺ secretion into the tubular fluid via ROMK. The result is enhanced urinary K⁺ excretion. Aldosterone also acts on a smaller **colonic excretion arm** — an arm that becomes increasingly important as kidney function declines in CKD, where upregulation of colonic large-conductance K⁺ channels (BK/Maxi-K) can partially compensate for impaired renal excretion [4, mechanism_review].

Distal Na⁺ delivery and tubular flow are important co-modulators: high distal Na⁺ delivery increases ENaC-driven Na⁺ reabsorption, amplifying the lumen-negative potential and K⁺ secretion. This is the mechanism by which loop and thiazide diuretics — which increase Na⁺ delivery to the distal nephron — cause hypokalemia.

## Reference Ranges, Units & Interpretation

### Units and the Serum–Plasma Distinction

Serum potassium (K⁺) is reported in millimoles per litre (mmol/L), which is numerically identical to milliequivalents per litre (mEq/L) because potassium is monovalent (charge +1). No conversion factor is needed between the two unit systems.

The canonical adult reference interval for **serum** K⁺ is **3.5–5.0 mmol/L**; some laboratories report the upper bound as 5.2 mmol/L [7, regulatory]. The intracellular K⁺ concentration (~150 mmol/L) vastly exceeds the extracellular compartment, a gradient maintained by Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and exploited by excitable tissues for action-potential generation [8, mechanism_review].

**Serum versus plasma:** Serum K⁺ is systematically ~0.2–0.5 mmol/L higher than plasma K⁺ because potassium is released from platelets during the clotting process that generates serum [12, mechanism_review]. In thrombocytosis, the serum–plasma K⁺ difference (Dk) widens substantially and correlates with platelet count in a non-linear fashion: Sevastos et al. reported Dk ranging from 0.40 to 2.61 mmol/L across patients with thrombocytosis, and introduced the Dk100 index (Dk × 100,000 ÷ platelet count per mm³) precisely because measured Dk was disproportionately lower than expected at very high platelet counts [12, mechanism_review]. This is why a **plasma** sample drawn into a heparinised tube is the preferred specimen whenever pseudohyperkalemia from thrombocytosis is clinically suspected.

### Severity Classification

**Hyperkalemia (serum K⁺ > 5.0–5.5 mmol/L)**

A pragmatic expert-consensus classification used in emergency settings divides severity as follows [9, regulatory]:

| Grade | K⁺ (mmol/L) | Clinical urgency |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|
| Mild | 5.0–6.0 | Monitor; identify and treat cause |
| Moderate | 6.1–6.5 | Urgent ECG; pharmacological treatment |
| Severe | > 6.5 | Immediate intervention; cardiac monitoring mandatory |

**Hypokalemia (serum K⁺ < 3.5 mmol/L)**

| Grade | K⁺ (mmol/L) |
|-------|-------------|
| Mild | 3.0–3.4 |
| Moderate | 2.5–2.9 |
| Severe | < 2.5 |

Severe hypokalemia (< 2.5 mmol/L) carries substantial risk of life-threatening arrhythmia and respiratory muscle weakness [11, mechanism_review]. Life-threatening hypokalemia (< 2.0 mmol/L) may occur in untreated distal renal tubular acidosis and similar states of extreme potassium wasting [11, mechanism_review].

### The First Interpretive Question: Real or Spurious?

Before any clinical action is taken on an elevated K⁺ result, **pseudohyperkalemia must be excluded**. Common causes [12, mechanism_review]:

- **Hemolysis** during venipuncture or sample transport (K⁺ leaks from red cells; the most frequent cause)
- **Prolonged sample transport or delayed centrifugation** at room temperature (K⁺ leaks from red and white cells)
- **Fist-clenching** during blood draw (local muscle activity releases K⁺)
- **Marked leukocytosis or thrombocytosis** (K⁺ released from fragile or activated cells during clotting)
- **Familial pseudohyperkalemia** (rare; temperature-dependent K⁺ leakage from red cells)

A serum–plasma K⁺ discrepancy > 0.4 mmol/L on simultaneously drawn samples is the diagnostic threshold for pseudohyperkalemia [12, mechanism_review]. When genuine pseudohyperkalemia is confirmed, clinical decisions should be based on plasma (or whole-blood) K⁺, not serum.

Once a true K⁺ elevation is confirmed, the second interpretive step is to classify its mechanism: **transcellular shift** (redistribution without change in total body K⁺ — e.g., acidosis, insulin deficiency, beta-blockade, cell lysis) versus **true total-body excess** (renal retention, excessive intake) versus **impaired renal handling** (hypoaldosteronism, CKD, medications such as ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim).

### ECG Correlation: Why Potassium Is the Most Acutely Dangerous Electrolyte

The cardiac action potential depends critically on transmembrane K⁺ gradients. Both hyperkalemia and hypokalemia alter resting membrane potential and ion-channel kinetics in predictable but imperfectly correlated ways [8, mechanism_review].

**Hyperkalemia: Progressive ECG Changes**

As K⁺ rises above 5.5–6.0 mmol/L, a classic sequence of ECG abnormalities unfolds [8, mechanism_review]:

1. **Peaked (tented) T waves** — the earliest and most recognised sign; symmetrical, narrow, high-amplitude T waves in the precordial leads reflect global shortening of action-potential duration as inward-rectifier K⁺ current (IK1) is enhanced [8, mechanism_review].
2. **PR interval prolongation** — slowed atrioventricular conduction.
3. **P-wave flattening and eventual loss** — atrial muscle is more sensitive to depolarisation block than ventricular tissue; P waves broaden, flatten, and disappear as K⁺ approaches 6.5–7.0 mmol/L.
4. **QRS widening** — reduced conduction velocity in the His-Purkinje system and ventricular myocardium; QRS > 120 ms indicates severe toxicity.
5. **Sine-wave pattern** — progressive widening and merging of the QRS and T wave; the ECG resembles a sinusoidal wave.
6. **Ventricular fibrillation or asystole** — terminal event.

Two critical caveats from the literature: (a) **ECG changes are poorly sensitive** — in a retrospective series of 90 hyperkalemic patients with K⁺ ≥ 6.0 mmol/L, sensitivity across ECG criteria ranged from 18% (strict: new peaked and symmetric T waves) to 52% (any ECG change), and the authors concluded that no reliable diagnostic threshold could be established [10, cohort]; when physician readers were tested using ECGs from patients with K⁺ > 6.5 mmol/L, sensitivity reached only 55–62%, meaning 38–45% of severe cases had ECGs that readers did not recognise as abnormal [10, cohort]. (b) **The rate of rise matters as much as the absolute level.** Chronic hyperkalemia at 6.5 mmol/L may produce minimal ECG changes because compensatory mechanisms have had time to adapt; the same concentration rising acutely can be immediately life-threatening [8, mechanism_review]. The ECG therefore guides urgency but cannot rule out dangerous hyperkalemia when normal.

**Hypokalemia: ECG Changes and Arrhythmia Risk**

Hypokalemia reduces outward K⁺ currents (IK1, IKr, IKs, Ito), prolonging action-potential duration and increasing the risk of triggered arrhythmias [8, mechanism_review]:

- **Decreased T-wave amplitude / T-wave flattening or inversion** — earliest change
- **Prominent U waves** — positive deflections following the T wave, best seen in V2–V4; arise from delayed repolarisation of the Purkinje system
- **Apparent QT/QU prolongation** — often reflects T-U wave fusion rather than true QT lengthening; creates the functional equivalent of a long-QT state
- **ST-segment depression**
- **Increased ventricular ectopy** — premature ventricular contractions, polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (torsades de pointes), ventricular fibrillation at severe levels (K⁺ < 2.5 mmol/L) [11, mechanism_review]

ECG changes from hypokalemia are typically absent until K⁺ falls below about 2.7–3.0 mmol/L; they become reliable markers only in moderate-to-severe hypokalemia [11, mechanism_review].

**Digoxin toxicity amplification:** Hypokalemia dramatically potentiates digoxin toxicity. Digoxin inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase; low extracellular K⁺ impairs reactivation of that pump independently. The combination produces intracellular Ca²⁺ overload and ventricular arrhythmias at digoxin levels that would be non-toxic in a normokalemic patient [11, mechanism_review]. Concomitant QT-prolonging drugs (antiarrhythmics, certain antipsychotics, macrolides) compound the arrhythmia risk.

### The Hypokalemia–Hypomagnesemia Link

A critical and frequently under-recognised relationship: **hypomagnesemia causes refractory hypokalemia** that does not correct with potassium repletion alone [17, mechanism_review]. The mechanism involves magnesium-dependent inhibition of ROMK (renal outer medullary potassium) channels in the distal nephron collecting duct. When intracellular Mg²⁺ falls, this inhibitory block is released, leading to unrestrained renal K⁺ secretion regardless of serum K⁺ status [17, mechanism_review]. In any patient with persistent hypokalemia despite adequate K⁺ supplementation, serum magnesium should be measured and, if low, corrected first. Magnesium repletion is often the key that unlocks potassium retention [17, mechanism_review].

## Measurement & Standardization

### Ion-Selective Electrode: The Standard Method

Serum and plasma potassium is measured universally by **ion-selective electrode (ISE)**, a potentiometric method that generates a voltage proportional to the activity of K⁺ ions in solution. Two configurations exist, and the distinction matters:

- **Indirect ISE** (the dominant mode on main laboratory chemistry analysers): the sample is diluted 1:20 to 1:34 with an ionic-strength buffer before measurement. This reports potassium *concentration* in plasma or serum (mmol/L), calibrated against aqueous standards. It is susceptible to the electrolyte-exclusion effect when large macromolecules displace plasma water (relevant for sodium in extreme dyslipidaemia or hyperproteinaemia; less consequential for K⁺ in normal plasma).
- **Direct ISE** (blood-gas analysers and point-of-care [POC] devices): the undiluted whole-blood or plasma sample contacts the electrode membrane directly. It measures ion *activity* in plasma water (mmol/kg H₂O). Because no dilution step intervenes, direct ISE is free of the electrolyte-exclusion artefact and is the method of choice when a rapid, matrix-faithful reading is required — notably for confirming a spuriously elevated result from a routine-lab sample.

Both modes use liquid-junction or solid polymer membranes selective for K⁺ (typically valinomycin-based). Results from direct and indirect ISE agree closely in normal samples; discordance is itself diagnostically informative.

### Pseudohyperkalemia: The Dominant Source of Error

Potassium is the electrolyte most vulnerable to pre-analytic artefact, because K⁺ is overwhelmingly intracellular: erythrocyte [K⁺] is approximately 100–115 mmol/L (≈25–40× the plasma concentration of ~4 mmol/L). Any event that ruptures cells or impairs the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase *after* blood draw will release K⁺ into the surrounding plasma or serum, producing a falsely elevated result. This artefact — **pseudohyperkalemia** — is the single most common cause of an apparent hyperkalemia referred to the laboratory, and recognising its five forms prevents unnecessary and potentially harmful treatment [20, mechanism_review; 21, mechanism_review].

**1. Hemolysis — the leading cause**

In-vitro hemolysis is responsible for more spurious K⁺ elevations than all other causes combined. Even mild hemolysis — from a difficult draw, a small-gauge needle, excessive negative pressure (vacuum tube), tube underfill, or vigorous mixing — liberates K⁺ from ruptured erythrocytes. The intracellular-to-plasma concentration ratio of approximately 25–40 means that lysis of a small fraction of the red cell mass produces a disproportionately large rise in measured K⁺.

Quantitatively, the relationship is near-linear: each **1 g/L of free hemoglobin (HI = 100 mg/dL)** raises measured potassium by approximately **0.23–0.33 mmol/L** — roughly 0.26–0.29 mmol/L in the DiToro ISE cohort [22, cohort], ~0.23–0.25 mmol/L at the threshold levels studied by Balasubramanian et al. on a blood-gas analyser [23, cohort], and ~0.33 mmol/L averaged over the 0–4.5 g/L range in the Koseoglu interference study [24, cohort]. A moderately hemolysed specimen (free Hb 1–4.5 g/L) can elevate K⁺ by 1.5 mmol/L or more, pushing a true 4.5 mmol/L into apparent danger-range hyperkalemia [24, cohort]. The **hemolysis index (HI)**, a spectrophotometric estimate of free hemoglobin concentration reported by most modern chemistry analysers, is the first-line flag; laboratories apply specimen-specific HI rejection thresholds (often 20–100 mg/dL) calibrated to a clinically significant ΔK of 0.1–0.3 mmol/L [22, cohort; 23, cohort].

**2. Fist-clenching and prolonged tourniquet — the classic outpatient artefact**

Repeated forearm-muscle contraction (fist-pumping) during phlebotomy elevates local K⁺ through two mechanisms: depolarisation-driven efflux from muscle cells and local venous stasis (tourniquet longer than ~60 seconds compounds the effect by reducing pH and impairing the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase). The rise is real at the sampling site even though the patient is not systemically hyperkalemic. The magnitude is typically 0.2–2.0 mmol/L but can exceed 3 mmol/L in prolonged or forceful contractions [20, mechanism_review; 25, cohort]. The fix is a single hand-closure to raise the vein, then complete relaxation before needle insertion, with tourniquet time minimised [20, mechanism_review].

**3. Thrombocytosis and extreme leukocytosis — the serum-specific artefact**

Platelets are rich in intracellular K⁺ and release it during the clotting process that converts blood to serum. At normal platelet counts (~150–400 × 10⁹/L) this contributes the physiological serum-plasma offset of 0.2–0.4 mmol/L [26, cohort]. When platelet counts are markedly elevated — primary thrombocythaemia, polycythaemia vera, post-splenectomy states — the released load becomes clinically significant. The serum-plasma K⁺ difference rises with platelet count and can exceed 2 mmol/L when platelets exceed 1,000 × 10⁹/L [21, mechanism_review; 27, cohort]. A case report illustrates the extremes vividly: a patient with essential thrombocythaemia (platelets 1,305 × 10⁹/L) had a serum K⁺ of 6.3 mmol/L, lithium-heparin plasma K⁺ of 4.6 mmol/L, and whole-blood direct-ISE K⁺ of 3.4 mmol/L — a 2.9 mmol/L gap attributable entirely to clotting artefact [27, cohort].

Extreme leukocytosis (WBC > 100 × 10⁹/L, as in acute leukaemia) similarly causes K⁺ release if the white cells lyse during centrifugation. The practical corrective: whenever thrombocytosis or marked leukocytosis is present, collect a **lithium-heparin (plasma) tube and centrifuge immediately** rather than waiting for a serum tube to clot.

**4. Delayed analysis, cold storage, and pneumatic-tube transport**

The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase that actively maintains K⁺ inside erythrocytes is temperature-sensitive. When blood sits uncentrifuged at room temperature for more than ~60–90 minutes, or is refrigerated (which strongly inhibits the pump), K⁺ leaks out of cells continuously. In winter, specimens transported over long distances to a central laboratory can show pseudohyperkalemia purely from cold-induced pump inhibition — a seasonal pattern documented in rural and remote settings [20, mechanism_review]. Pneumatic-tube transport can mechanically haemolyse fragile leukaemic cells, creating a pseudohyperkalemia that would not appear in hand-carried specimens [28, cohort]. The pre-analytic standard is centrifugation within 30–60 minutes of collection and analysis of separated plasma/serum within 4 hours, with no refrigeration of whole blood prior to centrifugation.

**5. Hereditary pseudohyperkalemia**

A rare autosomal-dominant condition (linked to mutations in *PIEZO1*, *KCNN4*, or *ABCB6* in some pedigrees) in which the red-cell membrane shows a temperature-dependent passive K⁺ leak. At 37 °C the in-vivo plasma K⁺ is normal; after the blood cools to room temperature during transport and storage, K⁺ exits red cells progressively. Affected individuals are non-anaemic, have no true hyperkalemia, and are identified by the diagnostic hallmark of serum K⁺ normalising when blood is processed at 37 °C. This is a diagnosis of exclusion; it should be considered when all extrinsic pre-analytic causes have been ruled out and the family history is positive [20, mechanism_review].

### Confirming a True Value

When pseudohyperkalemia is suspected, the confirmation hierarchy is:

1. **Whole-blood direct ISE on a blood-gas or POC analyser**, collected into a heparinised syringe and measured immediately: bypasses every post-collection artefact. This is the fastest and most reliable confirmatory test.
2. **Parallel serum vs. lithium-heparin plasma comparison**: a serum-plasma K⁺ difference > 0.5 mmol/L in the absence of visible hemolysis is the classical definition of pseudohyperkalemia [21, mechanism_review; 26, cohort].
3. **Hemolysis index**: if elevated, quantify expected ΔK and judge whether the measured excess is explained.
4. **Repeat draw** with meticulous technique — no fist-clenching, tourniquet ≤ 60 s, large-gauge needle, immediate gentle inversion, rapid transport, prompt centrifugation.

**Reverse pseudohyperkalemia** — plasma K⁺ exceeding serum K⁺ — is a rare artefact seen in haematological malignancies (typically chronic lymphocytic leukaemia) where fragile leukaemic cells survive intact in the fibrin clot of a serum tube but lyse during centrifugation of the heparinised plasma tube. Recognition requires comparing both specimen types [28, cohort].

### Standardization, Traceability, and the Serum–Plasma Offset

ISE calibration is traceable to NIST Standard Reference Material **SRM 956d** (Electrolytes in Frozen Human Serum), which provides certified K⁺ concentrations for accuracy verification. IFCC/CLSI guidelines specify calibration frequency, electrode maintenance intervals, and quality-control acceptance criteria.

The **serum-plasma offset** (serum K⁺ typically 0.2–0.4 mmol/L higher than lithium-heparin plasma) is a reproducible, method-dependent phenomenon arising from platelet K⁺ release during clotting [26, cohort]. Despite this, most laboratories apply identical reference intervals to both specimen types — a practice that leads to systematic miscategorisation of borderline results [26, cohort]. The correct approach is specimen-type-specific reference intervals; at minimum, clinicians should know which matrix their laboratory reports.

Reference intervals for serum K⁺ (typically 3.5–5.1 mmol/L in adults) are established by indirect ISE on fasting, correctly collected, promptly processed samples. Pre-analytic quality is not a footnote: the literature consistently identifies pre-analytic factors — hemolysis, delayed centrifugation, fist-clenching, cold transport — as the dominant source of erroneous potassium results in routine practice, dwarfing analytical imprecision from the ISE itself [20, mechanism_review; 22, cohort; 24, cohort].

## Determinants & Clinical Significance

Serum potassium occupies a narrow physiological window (3.5–5.0 mmol/L) despite total-body stores of roughly 3,500 mmol, almost all intracellular. Because potassium balance depends on both transcellular distribution and net renal excretion, many common drugs and diseases can push serum concentration to lethal extremes. The clinical danger is bidirectional — both hyperkalemia and hypokalemia generate life-threatening arrhythmias — and a large body of cohort and meta-analytic evidence establishes a U-shaped mortality curve at both ends of the distribution [13, cohort; 14, cohort; 15, meta_analysis].

### Hyperkalemia (>5.0–5.5 mmol/L)

**Decreased Renal Excretion (Dominant Mechanism)**

The kidney normally excretes 90–95% of absorbed potassium under aldosterone control. Any process that impairs glomerular filtration or tubular secretion raises serum potassium. Acute and chronic kidney disease are the single most common substrate for clinically significant hyperkalemia; as eGFR falls below 30 mL/min/1.73 m², the kidneys lose reserve excretory capacity and dietary potassium accumulates.

Drugs that interrupt the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) are the second major cause. ACE inhibitors and ARBs blunt angiotensin II–driven aldosterone synthesis, reducing distal tubular potassium secretion. In patients without CKD or other risk factors, the risk is modest (≤2%). In the presence of heart failure, CKD, or diabetes, hyperkalemia rates rise to 5–10% with monotherapy and higher with combination blockade [16, mechanism_review]. Mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonists (MRAs) — spironolactone, eplerenone, finerenone — directly block aldosterone's effect on principal cells and carry a clinically meaningful hyperkalemia burden: in EMPHASIS-HF, serious hyperkalemia (≥6 mmol/L) occurred more frequently in the eplerenone arm than placebo, though it was generally manageable by dose reduction. Potassium-sparing diuretics (amiloride, triamterene) block the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) in the collecting duct, impairing the electrochemical gradient that drives potassium secretion. NSAIDs suppress prostaglandin-mediated renin release, lower aldosterone, and reduce glomerular filtration — a triple hit. Calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine, tacrolimus) cause hyperkalemia through direct suppression of Na/K-ATPase and blunted aldosterone responsiveness. Trimethoprim blocks ENaC similarly to amiloride and is a significant and underappreciated cause of drug-induced hyperkalemia. Heparin and low-molecular-weight heparin suppress aldosterone synthesis directly, sometimes producing clinically relevant rises in potassium during prolonged use.

Hypoaldosteronism from any cause — Addison's disease, bilateral adrenalectomy, or the hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronemic form known as Type 4 renal tubular acidosis (RTA) — abolishes the main drive for distal potassium excretion. Type 4 RTA is particularly prevalent in elderly diabetics with mild-to-moderate CKD and frequently co-exists with RAAS-inhibitor use, creating additive risk.

**Transcellular Shift Out of Cells**

Metabolic acidosis drives potassium out of cells via H⁺/K⁺ exchange across cell membranes; each 0.1-unit fall in arterial pH raises serum potassium approximately 0.4–0.6 mmol/L, though inorganic acidoses (HCl, lactic) produce larger shifts than organic (ketoacidosis). Insulin deficiency in diabetic ketoacidosis causes profound transcellular release: serum potassium is often elevated at presentation despite total-body depletion, masking the degree of deficiency that emerges as insulin is replaced.

Massive tissue breakdown floods the extracellular space with intracellular potassium. Rhabdomyolysis (crush injury, severe exertion, statins with drug interactions), tumor lysis syndrome (rapid lysis of lymphoma or leukemia cells), large burns, and intravascular hemolysis are the classical scenarios. In tumor lysis, the speed of cell breakdown and degree of accompanying oliguric renal failure determine whether hyperkalemia is mild or acutely lethal.

Non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol) impair the beta-2-adrenergic stimulation of Na/K-ATPase that normally drives potassium into cells, raising serum potassium modestly. Digoxin toxicity poisons Na/K-ATPase globally, producing severe transcellular release that is particularly dangerous because the same toxicity sensitizes the myocardium to arrhythmia. Succinylcholine, used for neuromuscular blockade, depolarizes the motor endplate and causes potassium to exit all muscle cells simultaneously; in patients with denervation injury, burns, or immobilization, the potassium efflux can be massive and rapidly fatal. Hyperkalemic periodic paralysis is a rare channelopathy (SCN4A mutations) producing episodic attacks.

**Pseudohyperkalemia — Must Be Excluded First**

Before treating, pseudohyperkalemia must be excluded. Potassium is released from platelets during clotting (serum > plasma by 0.1–0.4 mmol/L normally; far more with thrombocytosis), from leukocytes in extreme leukocytosis, and from red cells with hemolysis during phlebotomy (tight tourniquet, small-gauge needle, fist-clenching, delayed processing). A repeat measurement in plasma (or promptly processed whole blood) resolves most cases. Pseudohyperkalemia carries no arrhythmia risk; treating it risks iatrogenic hypokalemia.

### Hypokalemia (<3.5 mmol/L)

**Renal and Gastrointestinal Losses**

Loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide) and thiazides are the leading pharmacological causes of hypokalemia in ambulatory patients. Both increase distal tubular flow and sodium delivery, augmenting aldosterone-driven potassium secretion. Patients on chronic diuretic therapy for heart failure or hypertension routinely develop potassium depletion, and supplementation is often required [18, mechanism_review].

Vomiting causes hypokalemia primarily through renal loss (metabolic alkalosis raises aldosterone and bicarbonaturia drives potassium excretion), not gastric fluid loss per se. Diarrhea and laxative abuse cause direct GI potassium loss. Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome) produces classic hypokalemia with hypertension; secondary hyperaldosteronism (heart failure, cirrhosis, renal artery stenosis) causes hypokalemia via the same aldosterone mechanism. Cushing's syndrome and exogenous glucocorticoids produce mineralocorticoid-like effects. Bartter syndrome (loop-of-Henle transporters) and Gitelman syndrome (distal convoluted tubule NCC transporter) are hereditary salt-wasting conditions that present with hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, and, in Gitelman's, concurrent hypomagnesemia. Renal tubular acidosis types 1 (distal) and 2 (proximal) both impair acid excretion and drive renal potassium wasting. Amphotericin B damages tubular membranes, creating an ionic leak.

**Transcellular Shift Into Cells**

Insulin drives potassium into cells via Na/K-ATPase; exogenous insulin administration and the endogenous insulin surge of glucose loading are clinically important. Beta-2 agonists (salbutamol/albuterol, used in asthma and for acute hyperkalemia treatment) stimulate the same ATPase. Metabolic alkalosis drives potassium intracellularly via H⁺/K⁺ exchange. Refeeding syndrome after prolonged starvation produces acute hypokalemia (and hypophosphatemia, hypomagnesemia) as anabolism restarts and cells re-uptake electrolytes. Thyrotoxic periodic paralysis is a dramatic form occurring disproportionately in East Asian males; thyroid hormone up-regulates beta-adrenergic sensitivity of Na/K-ATPase.

**Hypomagnesemia → Refractory Hypokalemia**

A clinically critical association: hypomagnesemia causes renal potassium wasting that renders hypokalemia refractory to potassium replacement alone [17, mechanism_review]. The mechanism is molecular: intracellular magnesium tonically blocks the apical ROMK channel in the distal nephron. When intracellular Mg²⁺ falls — as in alcoholism, diuretic use, proton-pump-inhibitor use, or diarrheal illness — ROMK channel pore inhibition is lifted, driving unrestrained potassium secretion into the tubular lumen. The kidney cannot retain potassium regardless of how much is administered. The clinical rule: in any patient with refractory hypokalemia, measure and replace magnesium before concluding the potassium deficit cannot be corrected.

### Cardiac Arrhythmia and Sudden Death

At both extremes of the potassium range, the resting membrane potential and action potential duration of cardiac myocytes are disturbed in ways that lower the threshold for malignant arrhythmia. Hyperkalemia depolarizes the resting membrane, initially producing peaked T-waves, then PR prolongation, QRS widening, and — at severe levels — a sinusoidal pattern preceding ventricular fibrillation or asystole. Hypokalemia prolongs cardiac repolarization (QT interval, U-wave emergence) and promotes torsades de pointes, ventricular fibrillation, and potentiates digoxin toxicity by competing with the drug's binding site on Na/K-ATPase. The arrhythmia risk of hypokalemia is well-established in acute MI populations [13, cohort].

### U-Shaped Mortality Across Populations

The mortality risk of potassium dysregulation is not limited to extremes; even modest deviations from the 4.0–4.5 mmol/L optimum carry measurable excess mortality. Three complementary bodies of evidence establish this:

**In Acute MI:** Goyal et al. analyzed 38,689 patients hospitalized with biomarker-confirmed AMI at 67 US hospitals (2000–2008). In multivariable analysis, potassium ≥5.5 mmol/L carried an adjusted OR for in-hospital mortality of 6.44 (95% CI 4.27–9.70) versus the 3.5–4.0 mmol/L reference; even the 4.5–5.0 mmol/L range carried OR 1.99 (95% CI 1.68–2.36), demonstrating that mortality risk rises steeply with potassium at both ends of the distribution [13, cohort].

**In Heart Failure:** Núñez et al. tracked 2,164 patients with chronic heart failure over a median follow-up of 2.8 years (16,116 potassium observations; 1,090 deaths). On a continuous scale, the association between potassium and mortality was U-shaped. Adjusted hazard ratios versus normokalemia: hypokalemia HR 2.35 (95% CI 1.40–3.93); hyperkalemia HR 1.55 (95% CI 1.11–2.16). Dynamic changes in potassium also predicted mortality; normalization was independently associated with lower risk [14, cohort].

**Across the Full Kidney-Function Spectrum:** The CKD Prognosis Consortium meta-analysis (Kovesdy, Matsushita, Grams et al.) pooled 27 international cohorts including 1,217,986 participants from general population, high-cardiovascular-risk, and CKD cohorts, followed a mean of 6.9 years. The risk relationship was U-shaped, lowest at 4–4.5 mmol/L. Compared to a reference of 4.2 mmol/L, adjusted HR for all-cause mortality was 1.22 (95% CI 1.15–1.29) at 5.5 mmol/L and 1.49 (95% CI 1.26–1.76) at 3.0 mmol/L. Findings were consistent across eGFR strata, albuminuria categories, and RAAS-inhibitor use — indicating the mortality signal is not confounded by drug use [15, meta_analysis].

A broad comorbidity analysis (Collins et al., n = 911,698) further demonstrated that all-cause mortality over 18 months was U-shaped in every disease subgroup: optimal range 4.0–5.0 mmol/L in controls; mortality rose sharply with both low and high potassium in heart failure (22%), CKD (17%), diabetes (7%), and combined disease (30%) populations [19, cohort].

### The RAAS-Inhibitor–Hyperkalemia Dilemma

The drugs that most powerfully reduce cardiorenal mortality — ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and MRAs — are exactly the drugs most likely to cause hyperkalemia, especially in patients already at risk (CKD, diabetes, older age). This tension is the central management dilemma in nephrology and cardiology. Palmer's review quantified the risk gradient: hyperkalemia rates are ≤2% in uncomplicated hypertension on RAAS monotherapy but rise to 5–10% with CKD or heart failure, and higher still with combinations [16, mechanism_review]. The practical consequence is that many high-risk patients have RAAS inhibitors inadequately dosed or prematurely discontinued when hyperkalemia appears — forfeiting mortality benefit.

Two novel potassium binders have changed the management equation. Patiromer (a polymer cation exchanger) was evaluated in 243 patients with CKD on at least one RAAS inhibitor and hyperkalemia: potassium fell by 1.01 mmol/L at 4 weeks and 76% achieved target normokalemia; in the withdrawal phase, patiromer-treated patients had significantly lower recurrence of hyperkalemia than placebo (p<0.001) [29, rct]. Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (ZS-9), a highly selective inorganic microporous zirconium silicate, achieved normokalemia in 80–94% of hyperkalemic patients at 48 hours across 5 g and 10 g doses in a phase-3 double-blind trial (n=753) [30, rct]. Both agents enable continued or up-titrated RAAS-inhibitor therapy without persistent hyperkalemia — a therapeutic strategy directly addressing the U-shaped mortality curve by allowing the drugs that prevent cardiorenal events to remain in place.

### Limitations

Serum potassium is a concentration, not a measure of total-body stores. Because roughly 98% of total potassium is intracellular, large transcellular shifts can dramatically change serum potassium without altering whole-body content — and vice versa, large depletions may occur with a seemingly near-normal serum level. Pseudohyperkalemia (hemolysis, extreme thrombocytosis, extreme leukocytosis, tourniquet artefact) must always be excluded before treatment. Potassium results must be interpreted in context: renal function (eGFR), acid-base status, magnesium level, the full medication list, ECG changes, and clinical signs integrate the picture in a way the serum value alone cannot.
