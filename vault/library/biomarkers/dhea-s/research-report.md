# DHEA-S (Dehydroepiandrosterone Sulfate): Canonical Research Report

## Summary

Dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S) is the sulfated, water-soluble storage form of dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) and together they constitute the most abundant steroid hormones in human circulation [1, mechanism_review]. DHEA-S is produced almost exclusively by the adrenal zona reticularis, making it the canonical biomarker of adrenal androgen secretory output. Its most important clinical property is that it exhibits **no clinically significant diurnal variation**: the long plasma half-life of approximately 7–10 hours means serum concentrations are stable throughout the day, unlike cortisol or free DHEA, so a specimen collected at any time — morning or afternoon — reliably reflects adrenal androgen output [3, mechanism_review]. This makes it the most practical timed-measurement-independent adrenal biomarker in routine use.

DHEA-S circulates at concentrations of roughly 100–400 µg/dL (2.7–10.9 µmol/L) in young adults — three orders of magnitude above cortisol and several-fold above testosterone — but this level is not static across the lifespan. DHEA-S follows a distinctive and dramatic age trajectory: it rises from near-zero in childhood during adrenarche (zona reticularis maturation, ~age 6–8), peaks in the mid-20s to early 30s, then declines progressively at approximately **2–5% per year** — a phenomenon termed adrenopause [2, mechanism_review]. By the 70s and 80s, values represent only 10–20% of the young adult peak [1, mechanism_review]. **A DHEA-S value is clinically meaningless without its age band.** Values that are normal at age 25 are elevated at age 70, and values normal at 70 represent profound adrenal androgen deficiency at 25.

Units: DHEA-S is reported in µg/dL (conventional, North American) or µmol/L (SI, European). Conversion: µg/dL × 0.02714 = µmol/L (derived from the molecular weight of DHEA-S free acid, ~368.5 g/mol; consistent with published pharmacopeial monographs and the pharmacokinetic literature [6, mechanism_review]).

The supplementation evidence must be stated honestly: oral DHEA supplements are widely marketed as anti-aging agents, but the evidence does not support this claim in healthy older adults. A meta-analysis of 25 placebo-controlled RCTs in elderly men (n=1,353) found a small body-composition signal that disappeared after adjusting for downstream hormonal conversions, with no meaningful benefit on lipids, glycemia, bone health, or quality of life [30, meta_analysis]. The 2023 Endocrine Society Scientific Statement on Hormones and Aging states explicitly that available studies "do not support the widespread use of DHEA supplementation as an anti-aging agent" [34, regulatory]. DHEA replacement in women with documented primary adrenal insufficiency experiencing androgen deficiency symptoms has some guideline-supported evidence but is not universally effective and is not an established indication in healthy populations [31, regulatory].

## Physiology & What DHEA-S Measures

### The Sulfated Storage Form of the Body's Most Abundant Steroid

DHEA-S is the sulfated, water-soluble ester of DHEA, constituting together the most abundant steroid hormones in human circulation [1, mechanism_review]. Circulating at roughly 100–400 µg/dL (2.7–10.8 µmol/L) in young adults, DHEA-S's abundance reflects its biological role as a stable, mobile reservoir of androgen and estrogen precursor material that peripheral tissues draw upon on demand.

### Synthesis: Adrenal Zona Reticularis Under ACTH Drive

The great majority of circulating DHEA-S originates in the **zona reticularis**, the innermost layer of the adrenal cortex [2, mechanism_review]. Synthesis follows the canonical steroidogenic pathway — cholesterol → pregnenolone → 17α-OH pregnenolone → DHEA — catalyzed by CYP11A1, CYP17A1, and particularly the 17,20-lyase activity of CYP17A1 (which the zona fasciculata largely lacks due to lower expression of the allosteric coactivator cytochrome b5). DHEA is then sulfated almost immediately by **sulfotransferase SULT2A1**, expressed at high levels in the adrenal reticularis, liver, and small intestine, converting free DHEA to the anionic sulfate conjugate that dominates the bloodstream [1, mechanism_review]. The gonads provide a small additional contribution; the adrenal source is the dominant driver of circulating DHEA-S, particularly in women.

ACTH is the primary trophic regulator of zona reticularis function and DHEA-S output, explaining why DHEA-S falls sharply in adrenal insufficiency and rises in states of ACTH excess such as untreated congenital adrenal hyperplasia. However, ACTH alone does not explain the dramatic age-related DHEA-S decline: ACTH and cortisol secretion remain roughly preserved into old age while DHEA-S falls precipitously — an uncoupling that defines adrenopause and remains incompletely understood at the molecular level [2, mechanism_review].

### The DHEA ⇌ DHEA-S Interconversion

DHEA and DHEA-S interconvert reversibly in vivo through two enzyme classes operating in opposite directions:

- **Sulfotransferases (SULT2A1):** sulfate DHEA → DHEA-S; active in adrenal, liver, gut
- **Steroid sulfatase (STS / arylsulfatase C):** desulfate DHEA-S → DHEA; expressed widely in peripheral tissues including breast, prostate, bone, skin, and brain

This desulfation step is critical because free DHEA — not DHEA-S — is the biologically active precursor that enters the steroidogenic pathway in target tissues. The sulfate group increases water solubility (facilitating plasma transport) and dramatically slows metabolic clearance; STS activity in target tissues releases DHEA on demand, giving each tissue local control over androgen and estrogen production.

### Why DHEA-S Is Measured Instead of DHEA

Clinicians measure DHEA-S rather than free DHEA for pharmacokinetic reasons [3, mechanism_review]:

1. **Half-life:** DHEA-S has an endogenous plasma half-life of approximately **7–10 hours** — substantially longer than free DHEA's half-life of only 15–30 minutes [3, mechanism_review]. This extended half-life results from tight albumin binding and slow desulfation (metabolic clearance rate ~5–20 L/day vs DHEA's ~2,000 L/day). (Exogenous pharmacokinetic figures from oral DHEA supplementation studies such as Legrain et al. [4, open_label] apply after dosing and are not the source of the endogenous half-life figure.)

2. **No significant diurnal variation:** Unlike cortisol — which peaks within 30–60 minutes of awakening and falls through the day — and unlike free DHEA, which partly mirrors the ACTH/cortisol diurnal rhythm, **DHEA-S shows minimal diurnal fluctuation** [3, mechanism_review]. A blood draw at 8 AM or 4 PM gives essentially the same result. Rosenfeld et al. (1975) established this directly: while DHEA and cortisol were episodic and synchronous across 24 hours, DHEA-S displayed a flat, non-pulsatile pattern [25, mechanism_review].

3. **High, stable plasma concentrations:** The high circulating DHEA-S pool (~100-fold greater than free DHEA) means measurement error is small relative to signal [5, mechanism_review].

4. **Adrenal specificity:** Because SULT2A1 activity in the adrenal reticularis is the dominant driver, serum DHEA-S serves as a reliable index of **adrenal androgen secretory capacity** without contamination from gonadal contributions [5, mechanism_review].

The practical consequence: a single random serum DHEA-S sample, collected at any time of day without fasting or special preparation, reliably reflects adrenal androgen output — a diagnostic convenience not shared by any other adrenal steroid.

### DHEA-S as a Prohormone Reservoir: Intracrinology

Neither DHEA-S nor its immediate precursor DHEA has known direct receptor-mediated activity at physiological concentrations. Their biological effects are executed after local conversion in target tissues to active androgens (testosterone, dihydrotestosterone) and estrogens (estradiol, estrone) [6, mechanism_review]. This tissue-based steroidogenesis — termed **intracrinology**, a concept developed by Fernand Labrie and colleagues — means hormonal actions of DHEA-S are not exerted systemically but are regulated cell-autonomously within each tissue [7, mechanism_review].

The scale of intracrine contribution is substantial: in premenopausal women, peripheral intracrine synthesis accounts for roughly 40–50% of active androgens; after menopause, when ovarian estrogen secretion ceases, virtually **100% of estrogens** in most target tissues (breast, uterus, bone, skin) are synthesized intracrinally from the DHEA-S → DHEA → androstenedione → estrone/estradiol pathway [7, mechanism_review]. In adult men, approximately 40–50% of active androgens are produced intracrinally. Serum DHEA-S functions as a circulating "fuel tank" for peripheral steroidogenesis, with each tissue drawing from it according to its own local enzyme expression profile.

Key enzymes in peripheral conversion include HSD3B (3β-HSD, converts DHEA → androstenedione), HSD17B (17β-HSD family, converts androstenedione → testosterone), 5α-reductase (converts testosterone → DHT), and aromatase CYP19A1 (converts androstenedione → estrone, testosterone → estradiol). Expression patterns differ across tissues, so the same circulating DHEA-S pool yields androgen-dominant products in skin and prostate but estrogen-dominant products in breast tissue.

### The Age Trajectory: Adrenarche to Adrenopause

DHEA-S follows a dramatic and distinctive lifespan trajectory [2, mechanism_review]:

- **Early childhood (0–5 years):** Near-absent. The zona reticularis is morphologically undeveloped and SULT2A1 activity is low.
- **Adrenarche (~age 6–8 years):** DHEA-S begins to rise in a process called adrenarche, coinciding with zona reticularis maturation — characterized by reduced 3β-HSD (HSD3B2) expression relative to the zona fasciculata, which shifts flux toward C19 androgens rather than cortisol [8, mechanism_review]. Adrenarche precedes gonadarche (puberty proper) by 2–3 years and is independent of GnRH/LH/FSH.
- **Young adult peak (20s–30s):** DHEA-S reaches peak concentrations of roughly 200–400 µg/dL (5.4–10.9 µmol/L) in men and 150–300 µg/dL (4.1–8.1 µmol/L) in women.
- **Progressive decline (30s onward):** Unlike cortisol secretion, which is largely maintained into old age, DHEA-S declines at approximately **2–5% per year** after the peak — adrenopause [2, mechanism_review]. Mechanisms include progressive zona reticularis involution, altered intra-adrenal steroidogenic enzyme expression, and possibly reduced sensitivity to ACTH-independent trophic factors.
- **Old age (70s–80s):** Serum DHEA-S falls to approximately **10–20% of the young adult peak** [1, mechanism_review] — values of 20–80 µg/dL (0.5–2.2 µmol/L) are typical — representing a loss of roughly 80–90% of adrenal androgen reserve. This decline is proportionally far greater than the age-related decline in any other major adrenal or gonadal hormone and occurs in both sexes, though women tend to reach lower absolute values in old age.

Unlike menopause, adrenopause has no discrete biological event marking its onset; it is continuous and gradual, beginning in the 30s. Its clinical significance — whether the DHEA-S decline contributes causally to age-associated changes in body composition, bone density, cognition, immune function, and vitality — remains under active investigation.

## Reference Ranges, Units & Thresholds

### Units

DHEA-S is reported in two unit systems:

- **Conventional unit:** µg/dL (micrograms per deciliter) — dominant in North American clinical labs
- **SI unit:** µmol/L (micromoles per liter) — preferred in European and international reporting
- **Occasional variants:** µg/mL and ng/mL appear in older literature; 1 µg/mL = 100 µg/dL; ng/mL × 0.1 = µg/dL

### Pediatric and Adrenarche Context

Before puberty, DHEA-S is very low. **Adrenarche** — zona reticularis maturation — is biochemically defined as DHEA-S rising above approximately **40 µg/dL (1.09 µmol/L)** [11, cohort]. In a European cohort study of 531 healthy children aged 1 month to 18 years, this threshold was reached at approximately age 7–8 years in both boys and girls [11, cohort]. Infants have transiently elevated DHEA-S in the first months of life (reflecting fetal zone activity) that then declines before adrenarche begins.

Premature adrenarche — DHEA-S rising above the adrenarche threshold before age 6 in girls or age 7 in boys — warrants evaluation to exclude nonclassic congenital adrenal hyperplasia [11, cohort].

At mid-puberty (Tanner stage P2):
- Girls: median DHEA-S ~82 µg/dL
- Boys: median DHEA-S ~133 µg/dL

(From a large Indian pediatric cohort [12, cohort].)

### Diagnostic Thresholds

**>700 µg/dL (19.0 µmol/L):** Red flag for adrenal androgen-secreting tumor, particularly adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) [13, cohort]. Not a hard diagnostic cutpoint but a rule-of-thumb above which adrenal neoplasm must be excluded. In women, >600 µg/dL has been proposed as a threshold to prompt evaluation for androgen-secreting adrenal cortical adenoma [17, regulatory].

**60–70 µg/dL cutoff (age-sex adjusted):** A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found that at a DHEA-S cutoff of 60–70 µg/dL, sensitivity and specificity for mild autonomous cortisol secretion (MACS) from adrenal incidentalomas were each approximately 82% [18, meta_analysis].

**~100 µg/dL (age-sex adjusted):** A DHEA-S above ~100 µg/dL makes adrenal insufficiency very unlikely; a value below ~25 µg/dL supports it; intermediate values require dynamic cosyntropin stimulation testing [18, meta_analysis].

**No single universal cutpoint:** A value of 130 µg/dL is unremarkable in a 25-year-old man and frankly elevated in a 70-year-old woman. Reference intervals should always be sourced from the reporting laboratory's population data or an age-matched normative dataset; the decade-banded pattern documented by Orentreich et al. [10, cohort] is the foundational normative standard.

## Measurement & Standardization

### Measurement Methods: Immunoassay vs. LC-MS/MS

Two analytical approaches dominate clinical DHEA-S measurement: automated competitive immunoassay and liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Each carries distinct accuracy and precision profiles, and the choice of method has direct consequences for result interpretation.

**Automated immunoassay** (ECLIA, CMIA, and enzyme immunoassay formats) runs on platforms such as the Roche cobas e, Abbott Alinity, and Beckman Access. These handle large volumes at low per-test cost and predominate in routine laboratories. Their limitation is antibody specificity: a competitive immunoassay's single antibody cannot distinguish DHEA-S from structurally similar sulfated steroids, so cross-reactivity with free DHEA, pregnenolone sulfate, androsterone sulfate, androstenedione, and estrone-3-sulfate introduces platform-specific positive bias [19, cohort].

**LC-MS/MS**, particularly isotope-dilution LC-MS/MS (ID-LC-MS/MS), separates DHEA-S by chromatographic retention time and identifies it by mass transitions specific to its molecular structure. Cross-reactivity from co-eluting steroids is either absent or flagged by atypical ion ratios. It is the reference-grade method against which immunoassay performance is benchmarked [19, cohort; 20, cohort].

### Inter-Method Variation: Quantitative Evidence

The practical gap between platforms is not trivial. Büttler et al. (2013) compared seven commercial DHEA-S immunoassays to ID-LC-MS/MS across 75 serum samples spanning 0.06–20.6 µmol/L (approximately 2.2–748 µg/dL). Three assays agreed acceptably (regression slopes 0.92–1.07; R = 0.93–0.99); four showed standardization failures with slopes of 0.84, 1.14, 1.20, and 1.28 — representing biases ranging from −16% to +28% relative to the reference. One immunoassay exhibited an intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) of 18%, far exceeding the ≤5.5% seen in the remaining methods and the 4.1% of ID-LC-MS/MS itself. These findings were "in agreement" with data from three national External Quality Assessment Schemes (EQAS) — Dutch, German, and British — indicating the discordance is systemic, not a local artefact [19, cohort].

Bias is larger at the extremes. In a multisteroid LC-MS/MS validation against the Roche Elecsys ECLIA, DHEA-S immunoassay showed approximately +21.5% mean positive bias — relatively constant across the measurement range, suggesting miscalibration rather than proportional cross-reactivity [23, mechanism_review]. In neonates the divergence is dramatic: Livie et al. (2023) found the Abbott Alinity DHEA-S immunoassay ran at +166% positive bias versus LC-MS/MS in infants under 52 weeks, as fetal adrenal and placental steroid sulfates cross-react with assay antibodies; the bias decreased with age [20, cohort]. LC-MS/MS is the method of choice for neonatal DHEA-S measurement.

### The Standardization Gap

Unlike cholesterol (CRMLN network) or testosterone and estradiol (CDC Hormone Standardization Program, HoSt), DHEA-S does not have a widely adopted reference-method-based standardization program with mandatory or widely certified participation [21, regulatory]. The CDC HoSt program covers testosterone and estradiol; DHEA-S appears in CDC materials only as an orientation value (without a certified reference method), not as a harmonized measurand [21, regulatory; 24, mechanism_review].

This gap has direct consequences. Without a single accepted higher-order calibration anchor, each manufacturer calibrates against its own working standards. Traceable calibration in FDA 510(k) submissions for DHEA-S immunoassays has been documented as relying on "gravimetrically produced master calibrators" or internally assigned secondary standards, explicitly noting the absence of "an internationally recognized reference standard or method for DHEA-S analysis" [22, regulatory]. The result: reference intervals are method-specific and platform-specific — a result of 200 µg/dL on a Roche Elecsys may not be clinically equivalent to 200 µg/dL on an Abbott Alinity or Siemens Centaur. Clinicians should interpret DHEA-S results using the reference range established by the specific analyzer in their reporting laboratory.

### A Genuine Pre-Analytic Advantage: Sampling Time Is Not Critical

One significant practical advantage of DHEA-S over many other adrenal hormones is its pharmacokinetic stability in the circulation. DHEA-S has a long plasma half-life — approximately 7–10 hours — arising from high albumin binding, very low renal clearance, and a slow metabolic clearance rate of approximately 13 L/day compared to DHEA's ~2,000 L/day. Because DHEA-S turns over slowly, serum concentrations do not fluctuate acutely in response to pulsatile ACTH secretion that drives diurnal patterns in cortisol and, to a lesser extent, free DHEA.

The clinical implication contrasts directly with cortisol: **DHEA-S has no clinically significant diurnal variation**. Levels are stable throughout the day, do not exhibit a morning peak, and do not respond acutely to ACTH administration. Rosenfeld et al. (1975) established this directly: while DHEA and cortisol were episodic and synchronous across 24 hours, DHEA-S displayed a flat, non-pulsatile pattern [25, mechanism_review]. Sampling time is therefore not critical — a morning draw is conventional but a late-afternoon or random-time specimen is equally valid. Fasting is not required. Serum stability is excellent: consistent with manufacturer and laboratory technical data, DHEA-S is stable at ambient temperatures for several days, refrigerated for approximately two weeks, and frozen for up to 12 months, reflecting the analyte's inherent chemical stability as a sulfated, water-soluble molecule.

### Interferences

Several analytic interferences warrant attention when reviewing DHEA-S immunoassay results:

**Biotin (high-dose).** Automated DHEA-S immunoassays use streptavidin-biotin solid-phase chemistry. Exogenous biotin from high-dose supplements (commonly 5–10 mg/serving) competes with biotinylated reagents, producing falsely elevated results in the competitive format. Specimens should not be collected until ≥12 hours after the last biotin dose [24, mechanism_review].

**Heterophile antibodies.** Human anti-animal antibodies (HAMA and related HAAs) can bridge assay reagent antibodies and produce spuriously elevated or depressed results. The interference is unpredictable and patient-specific; LC-MS/MS is the definitive confirmatory approach [26, mechanism_review].

**Cross-reactivity with steroid sulfates.** Free DHEA, pregnenolone sulfate, androsterone sulfate, and androstenedione all produce variable degrees of cross-reactivity depending on the assay platform [19, cohort; 27, mechanism_review]. A quantitative study of the Roche Elecsys DHEA-S assay found no compound exceeded 5% cross-reactivity, with pregnenolone sulfate (clinically relevant in pregnancy) and estropipate the only compounds above 0.5% [27, mechanism_review] — but platform generalization is unsafe; manufacturer package insert data apply only to that assay.

**DHEA-S as an interferent in testosterone assays.** Elevated DHEA-S can cross-react in unextracted direct testosterone immunoassays, causing falsely elevated testosterone results in women and children where DHEA-S levels are high relative to testosterone. Middle (2007) documented this across multiple platforms including Abbott Architect, Roche Elecsys, Beckman Access, and Immulite 2000 — illustrating the general specificity problem in direct steroid hormone immunoassay [28, cohort].

### Age- and Sex-Matched Reference Intervals: The Critical Interpretive Factor

DHEA-S secretion declines approximately 2–5% per year after peak production in the third decade, producing a 5- to 8-fold span across adult life strata. Male reference ranges from major US reference laboratories illustrate the scale: ~164–530 µg/dL at ages 20–24, narrowing to ~21–226 µg/dL at age ≥75. Applying the wrong age band produces a false impression of deficiency or excess. Neonatal DHEA-S is dramatically elevated at birth (fetal zone of the adrenal cortex) and falls precipitously in the first months — physiological, but indistinguishable from pathologic elevation if adult ranges are applied [20, cohort].

Because reference intervals are method-specific (see Standardization Gap), serial monitoring should use the same platform and that platform's age-stratified intervals. US labs report µg/dL; European labs often use µmol/L (conversion: 1 µg/dL = 0.02714 µmol/L).

## Determinants & Clinical Significance

### Causes of Elevated DHEA-S

#### Adrenal Androgen-Secreting Tumors and Adrenocortical Carcinoma

Markedly elevated DHEA-S is one of the most clinically urgent findings in androgen-excess evaluation. A serum DHEA-S above approximately 700 µg/dL in a woman warrants adrenal imaging to exclude an androgen-secreting adrenal neoplasm [29, mechanism_review]. Adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) is particularly associated with very high DHEA-S, as malignant adrenal cells retain the capacity to produce adrenal androgen precursors while losing the ability to convert them efficiently to downstream androgens such as testosterone. DHEA and DHEA-S are elevated in more than 90% of ACC cases, making them among the most sensitive biochemical markers of this neoplasm alongside urinary 17-ketosteroids. Elevated DHEA-S is found more often in malignant adrenal tumors than in benign adenomas [29, mechanism_review].

#### Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (CAH)

In 21-hydroxylase deficiency (CYP21A2), the most common form of CAH, enzymatic blockade diverts steroid precursors into the adrenal androgen pathway, producing marked elevations of androstenedione, DHEA, and DHEA-S alongside high 17-hydroxyprogesterone and low cortisol [29, mechanism_review]. The 2018 Endocrine Society CAH Clinical Practice Guideline identifies DHEA-S overproduction as a consequence of shunted steroidogenesis [16, regulatory]. In the rarer 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 deficiency, DHEA-S elevation is particularly pronounced and androstenedione may be low or normal — a pattern that helps distinguish this form from 21-hydroxylase deficiency. Both classic and non-classic CAH are important causes of elevated DHEA-S in adolescents and young adults with hyperandrogenism.

#### Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

DHEA-S is modestly elevated in approximately 20–33% of women with PCOS, reflecting a component of adrenal androgen excess in this heterogeneous condition [29, mechanism_review]. Adrenal hyperandrogenism is more prevalent in women with non-classic PCOS phenotypes (B and C, defined by the Rotterdam criteria), with elevated DHEA-S appearing in approximately 20–30% of affected women [14, cohort]. The degree of DHEA-S elevation in PCOS is usually mild-to-moderate and rarely reaches the thresholds associated with adrenal tumors.

#### Premature Adrenarche

Premature adrenarche — early activation of the zona reticularis before age 8 in girls or age 9 in boys — produces elevated DHEA-S for chronological age. It is generally a benign variant of normal development but should be distinguished from true precocious puberty or a pathological androgen-secreting process [11, cohort].

#### Exogenous DHEA Supplementation

Oral DHEA is sold over the counter in the United States and is widely used as a putative "anti-aging" compound. Supplementation reliably raises serum DHEA-S into or above the young-adult reference range; this source of elevation must be considered in any patient presenting with unexpectedly high DHEA-S, particularly in older adults in whom the age-expected trajectory is a gradual decline [30, meta_analysis].

### Causes of Low DHEA-S

#### Adrenal Insufficiency

Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease) and secondary adrenal insufficiency (ACTH deficiency from pituitary or hypothalamic disease) both impair adrenal androgen production. In women, the adrenal cortex is the dominant source of circulating androgens, making adrenal insufficiency a principal cause of androgen deficiency in female patients. A low DHEA-S supports the diagnosis of adrenal insufficiency and tracks the loss of adrenal androgen output — a feature not captured by cortisol replacement monitoring alone. The 2016 Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline on Primary Adrenal Insufficiency (Bornstein et al.) recommends a trial of DHEA replacement (25–50 mg/day) in women with primary adrenal insufficiency who report low libido, depressed mood, or low energy despite optimized glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement, with monitoring via morning serum DHEA-S aiming at the mid-normal range [31, regulatory].

#### Hypopituitarism

Loss of anterior pituitary function reduces ACTH secretion, suppressing zona reticularis activity and producing chronically low DHEA-S. Low DHEA-S in the appropriate clinical context (other pituitary hormone deficiencies, history of pituitary pathology) is a supportive finding for hypopituitarism.

#### Glucocorticoid and Exogenous Steroid Suppression

Exogenous glucocorticoids suppress CRH and ACTH secretion via negative feedback on the HPA axis. Because DHEA-S production is ACTH-dependent, even modest doses — including inhaled, topical, and intra-articular preparations absorbed systemically — can substantially reduce DHEA-S. Glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency is the most common cause of secondary HPA suppression in clinical practice, and DHEA-S is a sensitive downstream marker of this suppression [29, mechanism_review].

#### Aging (Adrenopause)

DHEA-S peaks in early adulthood (typically the mid-20s) and then declines progressively at approximately 2–5% per year, falling by 70–80% between ages 25 and 75 [32, cohort]. This decline is one of the most consistent and reproducible age-related hormonal changes in both sexes. Age-appropriate reference ranges are mandatory: a DHEA-S of 60 µg/dL is within the expected range for a 75-year-old woman and would not prompt further evaluation, whereas the same value in a 35-year-old warrants investigation.

#### Chronic Illness and Anorexia Nervosa

Chronic inflammatory disease, critical illness, and severe nutritional restriction (particularly anorexia nervosa) are associated with suppressed DHEA-S. In these contexts, low DHEA-S may be a marker of physiological stress and HPA dysregulation rather than primary adrenal pathology.

### The Hyperandrogenism Localization Role

DHEA-S has a well-established function in the clinical workup of androgen excess in women: it helps localize the probable glandular source. Because DHEA-S is produced almost exclusively by the adrenal cortex (unlike testosterone, which originates from both adrenal and ovary), its level provides a first-pass organ discriminator:

- **Elevated DHEA-S + elevated testosterone** → adrenal origin likely; adrenal imaging and suppression testing are appropriate next steps
- **Elevated DHEA-S + normal testosterone** → isolated adrenal source
- **Normal DHEA-S + elevated testosterone** → ovarian source more probable (PCOS, ovarian stromal hyperthecosis, androgen-secreting ovarian tumor)

The 2025 Society for Endocrinology guideline on androgen excess in women recommends measuring DHEA-S in all patients with clinical or biochemical androgen excess as an adjunct to distinguish adrenal from ovarian etiology [15, regulatory]. The dexamethasone suppression test can further refine source attribution: suppression of DHEA-S >60% alongside cortisol, with failure of testosterone to suppress, points toward an ovarian source; persistent non-suppression of DHEA-S raises concern for adrenal carcinoma or Cushing syndrome.

The 2018 Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline on Hirsutism incorporates this principle in the workup of women with rapidly progressive virilization, recommending DHEA-S measurement alongside total testosterone to evaluate for an androgen-secreting adrenal tumor when DHEA-S exceeds approximately 700 µg/dL [33, regulatory].

The 2010 French Endocrine Society recommendations proposed that DHEA-S >600 µg/dL in women should prompt evaluation for androgen-secreting adrenal cortical adenoma — the marker's near-exclusive adrenal origin makes a highly elevated result a direct pointer to adrenal pathology [17, regulatory].

### The DHEA Supplementation Evidence: An Honest Appraisal

Oral DHEA is one of the most heavily marketed dietary supplements in the United States, commonly promoted for anti-aging, cognitive enhancement, and improved body composition. The evidence does not support these claims in healthy older adults.

A meta-analysis of 25 placebo-controlled RCTs by Corona et al. (PMID 23824417) enrolling 1,353 elderly men (mean follow-up 36 weeks) found that DHEA supplementation produced a small, statistically significant reduction in fat mass, but this effect disappeared after adjusting for DHEA-related conversion to testosterone and estradiol [30, meta_analysis]. No meaningful benefit was observed for lipid or glycemic metabolism, bone health, sexual function, or quality of life. The authors concluded any body composition effect operates indirectly via downstream hormonal metabolites rather than through DHEA itself.

The 2023 Endocrine Society Scientific Statement "Hormones and Aging" (Cappola AR et al., PMID 37326526) states explicitly that available studies "do not support the widespread use of DHEA supplementation as an anti-aging agent, despite claims otherwise to be found on the internet" [34, regulatory]. Moderately sized trials using 25–50 mg/day in older women demonstrated restoration of DHEA-S to young-adult levels, with minimal if any improvement in muscle mass, strength, or quality of life.

**Legitimate investigational roles** are more limited: DHEA replacement in women with primary adrenal insufficiency experiencing androgen deficiency symptoms has guideline-supported evidence [31, regulatory], though even here the evidence base is modest and supplementation is not universally effective. The randomized placebo-controlled trial by Arlt et al. in women with adrenal insufficiency demonstrated improved well-being and sexuality scores — one of the few RCTs with a positive signal in this indication [9, rct]. Potential roles in selected conditions (premature ovarian insufficiency, anorexia nervosa) remain under active investigation and are not established indications for routine use.

**Androgenic side effects** — acne, hirsutism, voice deepening, clitoral enlargement — are documented with supplemental doses, particularly in women, and represent a real safety consideration that the marketing of DHEA consistently underemphasizes.

### Associations with Age-Related Outcomes: Interpreting the Epidemiology

Low DHEA-S has been associated in observational cohorts with increased all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, depression, and lower bone density. These associations must be interpreted with important caveats.

**Mortality:** In the prospective MrOS Sweden cohort, 2,644 Swedish men aged 69–81 were followed for 4.5 years; low DHEA-S (lowest quartile) predicted all-cause mortality (HR 1.54, 95% CI 1.21–1.96) and cardiovascular mortality independent of inflammatory markers [32, cohort]. This prospective cohort finding is the best-characterized evidence linking low DHEA-S to mortality risk in elderly men.

**Depression:** In the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA), 3,083 participants at baseline and 3,009 at 4-year follow-up were examined. Low baseline DHEA-S predicted incident depression at 4-year follow-up; those who developed depression showed DHEA-S approximately 19% lower than those who did not, with the association persisting after adjustment for health and behavioral confounders [35, cohort].

**Causal interpretation is not established.** Confounding by age and illness severity is profound — DHEA-S declines with age and with chronic disease, both of which independently predict mortality and mood disorders. The current evidence is consistent with DHEA-S as a **marker** of overall biological aging and systemic health rather than a causal protective factor. No intervention trial has demonstrated that raising DHEA-S in elderly individuals with low levels reduces mortality, improves cardiovascular outcomes, or prevents depression.

### Analytical and Clinical Limitations

- **Age-dependence is extreme.** DHEA-S concentrations span more than 10-fold across the human lifespan. Any result must be interpreted against an age- and sex-matched reference range; a value normal at age 30 is elevated at age 70.
- **Assay variation.** Immunoassay-based DHEA-S measurements can diverge from LC-MS/MS reference methods, particularly near the lower and upper extremes. Retesting, or reflex to a higher-specificity platform, should be considered before clinical action on borderline values.
- **Biological marker, not a standalone diagnosis.** Elevated DHEA-S narrows the differential and guides imaging or suppression testing; it does not diagnose adrenal tumors, CAH, or PCOS in isolation. Low DHEA-S supports adrenal insufficiency but requires corroborating stimulation testing and clinical context.
- **Modest elevations are nonspecific.** DHEA-S in the upper-normal or mildly elevated range is common in young adults, PCOS, and overweight individuals and does not mandate further workup in the absence of other hyperandrogenism signs.
