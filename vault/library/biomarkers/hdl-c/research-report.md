# HDL-C — HDL Cholesterol: Canonical Research Report

## Summary

HDL-C (high-density lipoprotein cholesterol) is the mass of cholesterol carried within circulating high-density lipoprotein particles, reported in mg/dL (US) or mmol/L (most of the world; the cholesterol conversion factor is 1 mmol/L ≈ 38.67 mg/dL, equivalently mg/dL ÷ 38.67 = mmol/L) [16, regulatory]. It is a single scalar — the cholesterol cargo of HDL — and it is NOT a direct measure of HDL particle number, particle size, composition, or function. That distinction is the through-line of this report: HDL-C the *concentration* and HDL the *functional particle* are not interchangeable, and most of the apparent contradictions in the HDL literature dissolve once the two are kept separate [6, mechanism_review].

The single most important fact about HDL-C is that it is a **strong inverse risk MARKER but NOT a validated causal or treatment TARGET**. HDL-C is one of the most reproducible inverse epidemiologic risk markers in cardiovascular medicine — in pooled prospective cohorts, a 1-SD higher usual HDL-C was associated with a myocardial-infarction odds ratio of 0.62 (95% CI 0.58–0.66) [1, meta_analysis] — yet three independent lines of evidence show that *raising* HDL-C does not lower cardiovascular risk. Mendelian randomization is null: genetic variants that raise HDL-C without affecting LDL or triglycerides do not lower MI risk (a 14-SNP HDL genetic score gave an MI OR per 1-SD of 0.93, 95% CI 0.68–1.26, p=0.63) [1, meta_analysis]. Every major HDL-C-raising drug trial failed — CETP inhibitors (torcetrapib caused *harm*, evacetrapib futile) and niacin (AIM-HIGH and HPS2-THRIVE both null) [10, rct][5, rct][4, rct][3, rct]. And the HDL-C–mortality relationship is **U-shaped**: very high HDL-C tracks *increased* all-cause mortality, not extra protection [2, cohort][9, cohort].

The mechanistic resolution is that whatever protective signal exists is carried by HDL *function* — cholesterol efflux capacity and particle quality — not by HDL-C *mass*. Cholesterol efflux capacity predicts cardiovascular events independently of, and beyond, HDL-C concentration and even HDL particle number [7, cohort]. This report synthesizes the identity and physiology of HDL-C (reverse cholesterol transport), the causal-evidence base for the marker-not-target verdict, the reference ranges and units, the measurement methods, the genetic and lifestyle determinants of the level, and its correct clinical use as a risk-stratification input rather than a treatment target.

## Identity & Physiology

HDL-C is the mass of cholesterol carried within high-density lipoprotein particles, reported in mg/dL or mmol/L [16, regulatory]. HDL is the smallest and densest of the lipoprotein classes. The single most consequential conceptual point is that HDL-C quantifies the cholesterol cargo of HDL, not the carriers: it is not a measure of HDL particle number, size, or composition, and — critically — it is not a measure of HDL *function* [6, mechanism_review].

HDL is biogenated when lipid-poor apolipoprotein A-I (apoA-I) acquires cellular cholesterol and phospholipid. Its best-characterized atheroprotective role is **reverse cholesterol transport (RCT)**: the efflux of excess cholesterol from peripheral cells — notably the lipid-laden macrophage foam cells in the arterial wall — onto HDL, with transport back to the liver for biliary and ultimately fecal excretion [6, mechanism_review]. Macrophage cholesterol efflux proceeds through several parallel pathways: the ATP-binding cassette transporters **ABCA1** (which effluxes cholesterol and phospholipid to lipid-poor apoA-I and is the rate-limiting step of HDL biogenesis) and **ABCG1**, the scavenger receptor **SR-BI**, and passive aqueous diffusion; hepatic SR-BI then mediates the selective uptake of HDL-borne cholesterol into the liver [6, mechanism_review].

Beyond RCT, HDL exerts a set of **pleiotropic, atheroprotective actions** that are again properties of the particle rather than of its cholesterol mass: it is **anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antiapoptotic, and endothelial-protective** — it limits the oxidation of LDL, supports endothelial nitric-oxide signaling, and dampens proinflammatory signaling in the vessel wall [6, mechanism_review].

Critically, these functions are properties of the *particle*, not of the cholesterol mass it carries. Under inflammatory or oxidative conditions HDL becomes **dysfunctional**: myeloperoxidase-mediated oxidation of apoA-I residues (oxidized tryptophan, chlorinated tyrosine-192, oxidized methionine-148) produces particles that lose efflux capacity and turn proinflammatory, and such dysfunctional HDL is associated with increased cardiovascular events *even when the HDL-C concentration is preserved* [6, mechanism_review]. This is the physiological seed of every paradox that follows in this report — a person can carry a "good" HDL-C number on a panel while the underlying particles are functionally impaired.

The functional readout bears this out empirically. In the Dallas Heart Study, HDL **cholesterol efflux capacity** in the highest versus the lowest quartile carried a hazard ratio of **0.33 (95% CI 0.19–0.55)** for incident cardiovascular events — and this held *after* adjustment for traditional risk factors, HDL-C level, AND HDL particle concentration [7, cohort]. In other words, HDL function predicts risk independently of, and beyond, HDL-C mass: the cholesterol number on the panel is a pale proxy for the biology that actually matters.

## Causal Evidence

HDL-C is one of the strongest and most reproducible *epidemiologic* inverse risk markers in cardiovascular medicine. In the pooled prospective cohorts assembled for the landmark Mendelian-randomization study, a 1-SD higher usual HDL-C was associated with a myocardial-infarction odds ratio of **0.62 (95% CI 0.58–0.66)** [1, meta_analysis]. This observational strength is precisely why HDL-C is embedded as an input in the Pooled Cohort Equations for 10-year ASCVD risk in the 2018 AHA/ACC/Multisociety cholesterol guideline — a low HDL-C raises the estimated risk [8, mechanism_review]. But the very same guideline assigns **no HDL-C-raising pharmacotherapy for prevention**: HDL-C is a risk-assessment input, not a treatment target [8, mechanism_review]. The reason the guideline draws that line is the body of causal evidence below.

### Mendelian randomization is null

If the inverse HDL-C–MI association were causal, genetic variants that raise HDL-C should lower MI risk in proportion to their lipid effect. They do not. The *LIPG* Asn396Ser variant raises HDL-C by about 0.14 mmol/L (~5.4 mg/dL) with no effect on LDL-C or triglycerides; the *observational* association predicted that this increment should carry an MI OR of **0.87 (95% CI 0.84–0.91)**, yet the *observed* genetic effect was **0.99 (95% CI 0.88–1.11, p=0.85)** across 116,320 participants — i.e., no effect [1, meta_analysis]. Aggregating the genetic instruments, a 14-SNP HDL genetic score gave an MI OR per 1-SD of **0.93 (95% CI 0.68–1.26, p=0.63)** — null [1, meta_analysis].

The strength of this null rests on an internal positive control. In the same study, a 13-SNP LDL genetic score *did* track its observational effect (OR 2.13, 95% CI 1.69–2.69 per 1-SD), confirming that the Mendelian-randomization method works as designed and isolating HDL-C as the genuine non-causal exception rather than a methodological failure [1, meta_analysis]. The genetic verdict is unambiguous: a lifelong, genetically conferred higher HDL-C does not protect against MI.

### HDL-C-raising interventions failed in randomized trials

Every major pharmacologic attempt to lower cardiovascular events by raising HDL-C has failed — and in one case caused net harm:

- **Torcetrapib (ILLUMINATE, n=15,067):** the CETP inhibitor raised HDL-C ~72% and lowered LDL-C ~25%, yet it *increased* death (HR 1.58, 95% CI 1.14–2.19, p=0.006) and major cardiovascular events (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.09–1.44, p=0.001); the trial was terminated early for harm [10, rct]. (Some of the harm was later attributed to off-target blood-pressure and aldosterone effects, but the trial nonetheless demonstrated that a large HDL-C rise did not translate to benefit.)
- **Evacetrapib (ACCELERATE, n=12,092):** this CETP inhibitor raised HDL-C ~132% and lowered LDL-C ~37% more than placebo, yet produced NO reduction in the primary composite cardiovascular endpoint; the trial was stopped for futility [5, rct].
- **Niacin — AIM-HIGH (n=3,414):** extended-release niacin raised HDL-C ~25% on a statin background, with no reduction in cardiovascular events; the trial was stopped early for futility [4, rct].
- **Niacin/laropiprant — HPS2-THRIVE (n=25,673):** despite favorable lipid changes, the primary major-vascular-event risk ratio was **0.96 (95% CI 0.90–1.03, p=0.29)** — no benefit — accompanied by a net excess of serious adverse events [3, rct].

The dalcetrapib CETP trial (dal-OUTCOMES) was likewise null. Across multiple drug classes (two CETP-inhibitor mechanisms, niacin) and tens of thousands of randomized patients, pharmacologically raising HDL-C mass did not reduce — and in the torcetrapib case worsened — clinical outcomes [3, rct][4, rct][5, rct][10, rct]. This is the converse of the LDL-C story: where lowering LDL-C reproducibly lowers events, raising HDL-C reproducibly does not.

### The U-shaped / paradoxical association

The "higher is always better" intuition is wrong even at the epidemiologic level. The HDL-C–mortality relationship is **U-shaped**: very high HDL-C is associated with *increased*, not decreased, mortality [18, mechanism_review]. In two large Danish cohorts (the Copenhagen City Heart Study plus the Copenhagen General Population Study; 52,268 men and 64,240 women, 745,452 person-years), all-cause mortality was lowest — the nadir — at **1.9 mmol/L (~73 mg/dL) in men** and **2.4 mmol/L (~93 mg/dL) in women** [2, cohort]. Above the nadir, mortality rose steeply: in men, HDL-C 2.5–2.99 mmol/L carried HR **1.36 (95% CI 1.09–1.70)** and HDL-C ≥3.0 mmol/L (~116 mg/dL) carried HR **2.06 (95% CI 1.44–2.95)**; in women, HDL-C ≥3.5 mmol/L (~135 mg/dL) carried HR **1.68 (95% CI 1.09–2.58)** [2, cohort].

The independent Canadian **CANHEART** cohort (≈631,762 Ontarians without prior cardiovascular disease, mean HDL-C 55.2 mg/dL, mean follow-up 4.9 years) reproduced the U-shape for both cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular mortality, found that high HDL-C conferred no cardioprotection, and noted that the excess mortality at high HDL-C was largely **non-cardiovascular** — which led the authors to question whether HDL-C should even be regarded as a cardiovascular-specific risk factor [9, cohort]. The high-HDL mortality inflection is reported most consistently around **≥80–90 mg/dL and is stronger in men**; supporting figures collated in a curated review include a coronary-heart-disease cohort in which HDL-C ≥80 mg/dL carried 96% higher all-cause and 71% higher ASCVD mortality versus a 40–60 mg/dL reference, a pooled cohort (~43,000) in which men with HDL-C ≥90 mg/dL had a 2.6-fold higher ASCVD mortality, and UK Biobank men with very-high HDL-C showing ~80% higher all-cause and ASCVD mortality (with no significant effect in women) [18, mechanism_review].

### Honest framing for downstream use

The three evidence streams converge on a single verdict. HDL-C is a useful, well-validated **inverse risk marker** and a legitimate **risk-calculator input** (it sits inside the Pooled Cohort Equations) [1, meta_analysis][8, mechanism_review]. It is NOT a validated causal or treatment target: genetics say raising it does not lower MI [1, meta_analysis], interventions confirm that raising it does not (and can harm) [3, rct][4, rct][5, rct][10, rct], and very high HDL-C tracks higher mortality [2, cohort][9, cohort]. The mechanistic resolution is that HDL *function* — efflux capacity, particle quality — carries whatever protective signal exists, not HDL-C *mass* [6, mechanism_review][7, cohort]. HDL-C should be interpreted as a barometer of underlying metabolic and cardiovascular health, never as a number to be pharmacologically maximized.

## Reference Ranges & Units

The threshold vocabulary in routine use comes from the NCEP Adult Treatment Panel III (ATP III). ATP III defines **low HDL-C as < 40 mg/dL**, treated as a categorical major cardiovascular risk factor, and **high HDL-C as ≥ 60 mg/dL**, which was historically counted as a "negative" (protective) risk factor that subtracts one factor from the risk-factor tally [8, regulatory]. The sex-specific metabolic-syndrome HDL criterion is **< 40 mg/dL in men and < 50 mg/dL in women** — these are the canonical "low HDL" thresholds used in metabolic-syndrome diagnosis and reproduced across the U-shaped-curve and mortality literature [8, regulatory][18, mechanism_review]. Low HDL-C (< 40 mg/dL in men, < 50 mg/dL in women) is one of the five NCEP ATP III metabolic-syndrome criteria; three of five confer the diagnosis [8, regulatory].

The **≥ 60 mg/dL "protective" designation is being de-emphasized**, because population data show a U-shaped rather than a monotonic relationship between HDL-C and mortality [18, mechanism_review]. As detailed in the Causal Evidence section, the Copenhagen cohorts placed the all-cause-mortality nadir at **1.9 mmol/L (73 mg/dL) in men and 2.4 mmol/L (93 mg/dL) in women** — not at the highest HDL values — with mortality rising above the nadir (men ≥3.0 mmol/L: HR 2.06, 95% CI 1.44–2.95; women ≥3.5 mmol/L: HR 1.68, 95% CI 1.09–2.58) [2, cohort]. The high-HDL mortality inflection is most consistently reported around **≥ 80–90 mg/dL and is stronger in men** [18, mechanism_review]. The practical reference picture is therefore: low HDL-C (< 40 mg/dL men, < 50 mg/dL women) flags elevated risk and feeds the risk calculator, a "normal" band sits in roughly the 40–60 mg/dL region, and **very high HDL-C (≳ 80–90 mg/dL, especially in men) is a flag for increased mortality — not a target to be maximized** [18, mechanism_review][2, cohort].

### Units and conversion

HDL-C in mg/dL is converted to mmol/L by **dividing by 38.67** (equivalently multiplying by ~0.0259); the factor derives from cholesterol's molecular weight (~386.65 g/mol) and is identical for total, HDL, and LDL cholesterol [16, regulatory]. Worked anchors: 40 mg/dL ≈ 1.03 mmol/L; 50 mg/dL ≈ 1.29 mmol/L; 60 mg/dL ≈ 1.55 mmol/L; 1.9 mmol/L ≈ 73 mg/dL [2, cohort][16, regulatory].

## Measurement

Modern clinical laboratories measure HDL-C with **homogeneous direct assays**, which replaced the older **lipoprotein-precipitation** methods. Precipitation methods cannot completely precipitate apoB-containing lipoproteins in hypertriglyceridemic samples, introducing interference; homogeneous assays show better precision (one validation reported within-run CV < 1.87% and total CV < 3.08%) [16, mechanism_review]. The **CDC reference method** for HDL-C is a three-step procedure — ultracentrifugation, heparin-manganese precipitation, then the Abell-Kendall reference method — and field methods are harmonized to it through the **CDC/NHLBI Cholesterol Reference Method Laboratory Network (CRMLN)** by fresh-sample comparison [16, regulatory]. The NCEP analytical goals for HDL-C are **bias ≤ 5.0%, precision CV ≤ 4.0% at concentrations ≥ 42 mg/dL (SD ≤ 1.7 mg/dL below 42 mg/dL), and allowable total error ≤ 13%** for a single measurement [16, regulatory].

Biological variability — not analytical error — dominates the interpretive picture for serial readings. HDL-C has a **within-subject biological variation (CVI) of 7.3%** and a between-subject variation (CVG) of 21.2%, with a desirable analytical imprecision of 3.65%, desirable bias 5.61%, and desirable total error 11.63% [17, mechanism_review]. The 7.3% within-subject CV means that a serial change must exceed the reference-change value before it can be read as a real biological shift rather than measurement-plus-biological noise. The practical implication is that small panel-to-panel swings in HDL-C (a few mg/dL) should not be over-interpreted, and — given the marker-not-target framing — should not in any case be chased pharmacologically.

## Determinants

### Genetics

Extreme HDL-C, at both tails, is strongly heritable. Rare large-effect variants in **CETP, LIPC (hepatic lipase), SCARB1, and LIPG (endothelial lipase)** drive very-high HDL-C, while **ABCA1 (Tangier disease), APOA1, and LCAT** drive very-low HDL-C [12, cohort]. In a clinical extremes cohort, **18.7% of low-HDL-C and 10.9% of high-HDL-C patients carried a rare heterozygous large-effect variant**; adding a polygenic trait score, roughly **30% of extreme-HDL individuals** had either a rare large-effect variant or a bundle of common small-effect variants [12, cohort]. The extreme thresholds used in that work were ≤ 0.8 mmol/L (men) / ≤ 1.0 mmol/L (women) for low HDL-C, and ≥ 1.4 mmol/L (men) / ≥ 1.8 mmol/L (women) for high HDL-C [12, cohort]. The genetic architecture matters for interpretation: the same *CETP* and *LIPG* loci that raise HDL-C are the ones whose Mendelian-randomization signal is null — a large genetically driven HDL-C number is not evidence of protection.

### Lifestyle determinants — raised by

- **Aerobic exercise.** A meta-analysis of 35 RCTs (1,404 subjects) found that aerobic training raised HDL-C by a mean of **2.53 mg/dL**; the minimum effective dose was approximately 900 kcal/week of energy expenditure or ~120 min/week of exercise, and **each additional 10 min per session added ~1.4 mg/dL** [13, meta_analysis].
- **Moderate alcohol.** A meta-analysis of interventional studies (33 pooled, 796 participants) found that moderate alcohol raised HDL-C by **0.094 mmol/L (95% CI 0.064–0.123; ~3.6 mg/dL)**, with a dose-response: +0.072 mmol/L at 1–2 drinks/day, +0.103 at 2–4, and +0.141 at ≥ 5 (P-trend = 0.013) [14, meta_analysis]. (That HDL-C rises with alcohol while alcohol does not reduce cardiovascular events is itself a clean illustration of the marker-not-target dissociation.)
- **Weight loss and estrogen** also raise HDL-C.
- **Smoking cessation.** A meta-analysis of 27 studies found that HDL-C rose **0.100 mmol/L (95% CI 0.074–0.127; ~3.9 mg/dL) after smoking cessation**, while LDL-C, total cholesterol, and triglycerides were unchanged — i.e., smoking lowers HDL-C and quitting reverses it [15, meta_analysis].

### Lifestyle determinants — lowered by

- **Smoking, obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes.** Low HDL-C co-clusters with obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes as the metabolic-syndrome phenotype [8, regulatory][18, mechanism_review].
- **Very-low-fat / high-carbohydrate diets.** In a randomized crossover trial, a low-fat high-carbohydrate diet lowered HDL-C from 1.21 to 1.07 mmol/L (~47 → ~41 mg/dL; P = 0.057) versus a Western diet [11, rct] — directional, from a single small trial, and flagged approximate.
- **Anabolic-androgenic steroids.** Oral 17-α-alkylated anabolic steroids profoundly suppress HDL-C, with reported reductions of **~60–80% (case reports > 90%)** alongside an LDL-C rise of ~50%; the mechanism is a rise in hepatic triglyceride lipase preceding the HDL2 decline, and the effect is reversible roughly 2.5–4 months after discontinuation [11, cohort] — pooled across heterogeneous sources and flagged approximate.

A unifying observation across the lifestyle determinants is that most levers that raise HDL-C (exercise, weight loss, smoking cessation) are independently cardioprotective for *other* reasons, while at least one (alcohol) raises HDL-C without conferring net benefit — which again warns against treating the HDL-C number itself as the goal of any intervention.

## Clinical Significance

HDL-C's correct clinical role is as a **risk-stratification input, not a treatment target**. It belongs in cardiovascular risk assessment because the inverse association with events is strong and reproducible: a 1-SD higher HDL-C carries an MI OR of 0.62 (95% CI 0.58–0.66) [1, meta_analysis], and the 2018 AHA/ACC/Multisociety guideline therefore embeds HDL-C as an input to the Pooled Cohort Equations, where a low value raises the estimated 10-year ASCVD risk [8, mechanism_review]. A low HDL-C (< 40 mg/dL in men, < 50 mg/dL in women) also flags the metabolic-syndrome phenotype and its cluster of obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes [8, regulatory][18, mechanism_review].

What HDL-C is *not* is a number to raise therapeutically. The same guideline assigns no HDL-C-raising pharmacotherapy for prevention [8, mechanism_review], a position grounded in the null Mendelian randomization [1, meta_analysis] and the uniform failure (and, with torcetrapib, net harm) of HDL-C-raising drug trials [3, rct][4, rct][5, rct][10, rct]. The corollary is clinically important and counterintuitive: because the HDL-C–mortality relationship is U-shaped, **very high HDL-C is not reassuring** — values around ≥ 80–90 mg/dL, especially in men, are associated with increased all-cause mortality and warrant attention rather than congratulation [2, cohort][9, cohort][18, mechanism_review].

The deeper clinical lesson is the mass-versus-function distinction. The protective biology of HDL lives in the particle — its cholesterol efflux capacity and broader functional quality — and that function predicts events independently of HDL-C concentration and HDL particle number (highest- vs lowest-quartile efflux capacity: HR 0.33, 95% CI 0.19–0.55) [7, cohort]. Dysfunctional HDL can coexist with a normal or even high HDL-C [6, mechanism_review]. For the operator and clinician, then, HDL-C is best read as one barometer of overall metabolic and cardiovascular health, interpreted alongside ApoB, LDL-C, and triglycerides — and explicitly *not* as a lever to be pulled. The single operative rule is: **do not raise HDL-C in order to lower cardiovascular risk.**
