# Serum Sodium: Canonical Research Report

## Summary

Sodium (Na⁺) is the principal cation of extracellular fluid and the dominant determinant of plasma osmolality — accounting for approximately 90% of extracellular solute and roughly 270 of the normal 280–295 mOsm/kg plasma osmolality [2, mechanism_review; 9, mechanism_review]. The normal adult reference interval is 135–145 mmol/L [1, regulatory]; the unit is millimoles per litre (mmol/L), numerically identical to milliequivalents per litre (mEq/L) because sodium is monovalent.

The single most important conceptual point about serum sodium — and the one most frequently misunderstood — is that **serum [Na⁺] reflects water balance, not sodium intake or total-body sodium content** [2, mechanism_review]. Formally, it approximates the ratio of total-body exchangeable sodium (plus potassium) to total body water (the Edelman identity). A patient can be simultaneously hypervolemic (expanded extracellular fluid, elevated total-body sodium) and hyponatremic if free water retention outpaces sodium accumulation — the defining paradox of heart failure, cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome. Conversely, the most common cause of hypernatremia (Na > 145 mmol/L) is a net free-water deficit, not sodium loading [3, mechanism_review].

Regulation operates through two distinct axes. The hypothalamic osmoreceptor–arginine vasopressin (AVP)–aquaporin-2 (AQP2) axis controls plasma tonicity with ±1–2% precision, adjusting renal free-water reabsorption minute-to-minute [2, mechanism_review; 5, mechanism_review; 6, mechanism_review]. The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) governs total-body sodium retention and effective circulating volume — a parallel but distinct axis that can conflict with osmotic regulation under conditions of severe volume depletion, driving non-osmotic AVP release and hyponatremia even when osmolality is low [9, mechanism_review].

From a measurement standpoint, virtually all modern clinical laboratories measure sodium by ion-selective electrode (ISE) potentiometry, which exists in two fundamentally different modes [15, mechanism_review]. **Indirect ISE** — used by the large automated chemistry analyzers that process the vast majority of hospital specimens — dilutes the sample before measurement and assumes that plasma water constitutes 93% of plasma volume. When that assumption is violated by severe hyperlipidemia or hyperproteinemia, the reported sodium is falsely depressed: this is **pseudohyponatremia**, the electrolyte exclusion effect. **Direct ISE** — used by point-of-care blood gas analyzers — measures sodium in the undiluted plasma water phase and is immune to this artifact. Historical flame photometry shares indirect ISE's vulnerability to pseudohyponatremia because it also dilutes the sample [15, mechanism_review]. Recognizing whether a laboratory uses direct or indirect ISE, and whether the patient has conditions predisposing to pseudohyponatremia (severe hypertriglyceridemia, paraproteinemia), is essential before interpreting a low sodium result.

Dysnatremia in both directions carries independent prognostic weight across multiple disease contexts, consistently associating with increased short- and long-term mortality — primarily as a severity marker of underlying neurohormonal dysregulation rather than as a direct cause of death [28, cohort; 26, cohort; 25, cohort; 30, cohort]. Values are reported in mmol/L throughout.

## Physiology & What Serum Sodium Measures

### The Principal Extracellular Cation

Sodium (Na⁺) is the principal cation of extracellular fluid (ECF). Serum sodium concentration in healthy adults is maintained within a narrow range of approximately 135–145 mmol/L [1, regulatory]. Within cells, however, sodium concentration is only ~10–15 mmol/L — a steep inward electrochemical gradient actively sustained by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump [7, mechanism_review]. This enzyme, present in virtually every mammalian cell membrane, continuously hydrolyzes one intracellular ATP molecule to export three Na⁺ ions out of the cell while importing two K⁺ ions, consuming roughly 20–40% of resting cellular energy expenditure to maintain these gradients [7, mechanism_review]. The resulting 10:1 or greater ratio of extracellular to intracellular sodium is not incidental: it drives secondary transport of nutrients, neurotransmitters, and amino acids; sets the resting membrane potential essential for nerve conduction and muscle contraction; and underpins the entire architecture of epithelial transport throughout the gut, kidney, and brain [7, mechanism_review].

### Sodium as the Dominant Determinant of Plasma Osmolality

Because Na⁺ — together with its balancing anions (principally chloride and bicarbonate) — constitutes ~90% of ECF solute, it is the principal determinant of plasma osmolality [9, mechanism_review]. Plasma osmolality can be estimated at the bedside with the Edelman-derived formula:

> **Plasma osmolality ≈ 2 × [Na⁺] + glucose (mg/dL)/18 + BUN (mg/dL)/2.8**   (conventional units)

where the coefficient of 2 accounts for the paired anions, and the glucose and BUN terms correct for other measurable osmoles [8, mechanism_review]. In healthy individuals, plasma osmolality is normally 280–295 mOsm/kg H₂O; sodium alone accounts for ~270 mOsm/kg of that total. Because sodium and its anions dominate the numerator, any clinically meaningful change in serum [Na⁺] directly shifts plasma tonicity — the effective osmolality that drives water movement across cell membranes [2, mechanism_review].

### The Load-Bearing Concept: Serum Sodium Reflects Water Balance, Not Sodium Intake

The most important conceptual point about serum [Na⁺] is frequently misunderstood: **serum sodium concentration is a measure of water balance, not of total-body sodium content or dietary salt intake** [2, mechanism_review]. Formally, serum [Na⁺] approximates the ratio of total-body exchangeable sodium (plus potassium) to total body water:

> [Na⁺]serum ≈ (Na⁺exchangeable + K⁺exchangeable) / Total Body Water

This identity, established by Edelman and colleagues, means that the serum sodium can fall even when total-body sodium is elevated — the defining paradox of heart failure, hepatic cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome — because free water retention by the kidney outpaces any sodium accumulation, diluting the extracellular compartment [2, mechanism_review]. Conversely, hypernatremia most commonly reflects a net water deficit rather than sodium loading [3, mechanism_review]. A patient can therefore be simultaneously hypervolemic (expanded ECF volume, pitting edema, elevated total-body sodium) and hyponatremic (diluted serum sodium due to proportionally greater water retention). This dissociation between volume status and sodium concentration is the conceptual foundation of dysnatremia evaluation.

### Osmoreceptor–Vasopressin–Aquaporin-2 Axis: Regulating Tonicity

The body defends serum osmolality — and therefore [Na⁺] — with remarkable precision (±1–2%) via an integrated hypothalamic–renal axis [2, mechanism_review]. Specialized osmoreceptor neurons in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus detect changes in effective plasma osmolality; threshold sensitivity is exquisite, with even a 1% rise in osmolality triggering measurable arginine vasopressin (AVP, also called antidiuretic hormone, ADH) release from the posterior pituitary [6, mechanism_review]. AVP then binds to V2 receptors on principal cells of the renal collecting duct, activating a cAMP–protein kinase A cascade that phosphorylates aquaporin-2 (AQP2) water channels and drives their rapid insertion into the apical membrane within minutes [5, mechanism_review]. Water crosses the tubular lumen through AQP2 and exits the cell basally via AQP3 and AQP4, producing concentrated urine and retaining free water in the circulation — restoring osmolality toward set point [6, mechanism_review]. With sustained AVP stimulation (hours to days), AQP2 gene transcription is upregulated, amplifying overall water-reabsorptive capacity [6, mechanism_review]. Thirst operates in parallel: the same hypothalamic osmoreceptors that trigger AVP release also stimulate thirst above ~290 mOsm/kg, providing the behavioral limb of water retention. Together, AVP secretion and thirst constrain osmolality fluctuations to within approximately 2–3 mOsm/kg of the individual's set point under physiological conditions.

### Volume Regulation: A Separate but Interacting Axis

Critically, the regulation of ECF **volume** is governed by a distinct axis that is not primarily concerned with [Na⁺] at all. Volume and pressure sensors — baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch, as well as renal juxtaglomerular cells detecting renal perfusion pressure — activate the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) when effective circulating volume falls [9, mechanism_review]. Aldosterone acts on the collecting duct to increase apical sodium influx via epithelial sodium channels (ENaCs), enhancing Na⁺ reabsorption and expanding ECF volume. This RAAS/aldosterone axis regulates how much **total-body sodium** is retained or excreted, not the serum concentration. The two axes can conflict: in severe volume depletion, baroreceptors override osmoreceptors and drive non-osmotic AVP release, causing water retention even at subnormal osmolality — a clinically important scenario that produces hypovolemic hyponatremia [4, mechanism_review].

### Tonicity, Cell Volume, and Neurological Consequences

Because the blood–brain barrier is freely permeable to water but relatively impermeable to sodium, acute changes in plasma tonicity drive rapid water shifts into or out of brain cells. Acute hyponatremia — a fall in [Na⁺] producing hypotonic plasma — causes water influx into neurons, with brain cell swelling and raised intracranial pressure; this is the mechanism of hyponatremic encephalopathy, manifesting as headache, confusion, seizures, and, in severe cases, herniation and death [4, mechanism_review]. Acute hypernatremia — a rise in [Na⁺] producing hypertonic plasma — extracts water from neurons, causing brain shrinkage, stretching of bridging veins, and risk of subdural hemorrhage [3, mechanism_review]. The brain's adaptive response — accumulation of intracellular idiogenic osmoles (organic osmolytes) over hours to days — partially restores cell volume during sustained hypernatremia, but this same adaptation creates the danger of over-rapid correction: correcting hypernatremia too quickly reverses the osmolar gradient, floods previously adapted cells, and can cause cerebral edema [3, mechanism_review]. Analogously, over-rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia strips neurons of osmolytes faster than they can be resynthesized, risking osmotic demyelination syndrome (formerly central pontine myelinolysis) [2, mechanism_review]. The kinetics of adaptation thus dictate the rate of safe correction for both disorders.

## Reference Ranges, Units & Interpretation

### Reference Interval and Units

Serum sodium is reported in millimoles per litre (mmol/L), which is numerically identical to milliequivalents per litre (mEq/L) because sodium is monovalent — no conversion factor is needed between the two unit expressions.

The adult reference interval is approximately **135–145 mmol/L** [1, regulatory]. Dysnatremia is classified at both poles:

| Condition | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Mild hyponatremia | 130–134 mmol/L |
| Moderate hyponatremia | 125–129 mmol/L |
| Severe / profound hyponatremia | < 125 mmol/L |
| Hypernatremia | > 145 mmol/L |
| Severe hypernatremia | > 155 mmol/L |

These thresholds are adopted from the 2014 European Society of Intensive Care Medicine / European Society of Endocrinology / European Renal Association clinical practice guideline [1, regulatory] and corroborated by the North American expert panel consensus [10, regulatory].

### The Hyponatremia Interpretation Framework

Because hyponatremia (serum Na < 135 mmol/L) arises from mechanistically distinct processes, a stepwise algorithm is required before any clinical or nutritional interpretation is made [1, regulatory; 10, regulatory; 11, mechanism_review].

**Step 1 — Measure Serum Osmolality / Tonicity.** The first question is whether the low sodium reflects a genuinely hypotonic state.

- **Normal or elevated serum osmolality (≥ 275 mOsm/kg)** in the setting of a low measured sodium indicates a non-hypotonic cause — either pseudohyponatremia (see Measurement section) or a translocational / hyperosmolar state (see glucose correction below). Treatment directed at water restriction would be inappropriate and potentially harmful.
- **Low serum osmolality (< 275 mOsm/kg)** confirms true hypotonic hyponatremia and initiates Step 2.

**Step 2 — Assess Volume Status.** Clinical assessment divides hypotonic hyponatremia into three hemodynamic categories:

1. **Hypovolemic hyponatremia** — reduced ECF volume; causes include gastrointestinal losses, excessive diuresis, third-spacing. Urine sodium < 20–30 mmol/L (unless the kidney is the primary sodium-losing organ).
2. **Euvolemic hyponatremia** — normal ECF; the dominant cause is the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuresis (SIAD/SIADH). Characterized by urine osmolality persistently > 100 mOsm/kg (typically > 300 mOsm/kg) and urine sodium ≥ 20–30 mmol/L despite hyponatremia. Other euvolemic causes: hypothyroidism, glucocorticoid deficiency, certain drugs.
3. **Hypervolemic hyponatremia** — expanded ECF (edema states: heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome). Urine sodium typically < 20 mmol/L due to avid renal sodium retention; effective circulating volume is perceived as low despite total body sodium excess.

**Step 3 — Urine Sodium and Urine Osmolality as Decision Branches:**

| Urine Na | Urine Osm | Likely etiology |
|---|---|---|
| < 20–30 mmol/L | variable | Hypovolemia (extrarenal loss) or hypervolemic states (HF, cirrhosis) |
| ≥ 20–30 mmol/L | > 100 mOsm/kg (often > 300) | SIADH; also diuretic use, renal salt-wasting |
| ≥ 20–30 mmol/L | < 100 mOsm/kg | Primary polydipsia; reset osmostat |

This three-step framework is embedded in both the European guideline [1, regulatory] and the Hoorn & Zietse compilation [11, mechanism_review].

### Pseudohyponatremia: A Laboratory Artifact

Pseudohyponatremia is a **measurement artifact**, not a physiological state, and must be excluded before treating an apparently low sodium result. The mechanism — the electrolyte exclusion effect — is covered in detail in the Measurement section; in brief: indirect ISE (and historical flame photometry) both dilute the sample before measurement and assume 93% plasma water content. When severe hyperlipidemia or hyperproteinemia displaces that water fraction downward, both methods systematically underreport true sodium. Direct ISE, which measures the undiluted plasma water phase, is immune. Recognition: normal or elevated measured osmolality (≥ 275 mOsm/kg) in the presence of low indirect-ISE sodium; confirmed by reflexing to a blood-gas analyzer using direct ISE [12, mechanism_review].

### Translocational / Hyperosmolar Hyponatremia: The Glucose Correction

When plasma glucose is markedly elevated — as in diabetic ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar hyperglycemic states — osmotic water shifts from cells into the extracellular compartment dilute sodium. Serum osmolality is elevated (not low), indicating the hyponatremia is dilutional and secondary, not reflective of true sodium deficit.

**Glucose correction formula.** The measured sodium must be adjusted before any interpretation of sodium homeostasis. Two correction factors are in clinical use:

- **Katz (1973) factor: + 1.6 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) glucose above 100 mg/dL.** This historically taught value is derived from in vitro calculations.
- **Hillier (1999) factor: + 2.4 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL glucose above 100 mg/dL.** Hillier et al. measured actual serum sodium in diabetic patients across a range of glucose concentrations and found the mean correction required was 2.4 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL increment — 50% larger than the Katz estimate, particularly at glucose levels > 400 mg/dL where the dilutional effect becomes nonlinear [13, cohort]. The Hillier factor is now generally preferred in clinical practice; both factors are typically quoted together [10, regulatory; 13, cohort].

Corrected Na (Hillier) = Measured Na + 2.4 × [(glucose in mg/dL − 100) ÷ 100]

### Over-Rapid Correction Danger

**Chronic Hyponatremia → Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome (ODS).** When hyponatremia has been present for more than 24–48 hours, brain cells adapt by exporting organic osmolytes (taurine, myoinositol, glutamate) to protect against cerebral edema. Rapid sodium correction reverses this adaptation abruptly, causing osmotic shrinkage of myelin-bearing cells and demyelination — classically of the pons (central pontine myelinolysis) but also extrapontine structures.

Guideline correction rate limits [1, regulatory; 11, mechanism_review]:
- **General recommendation:** do not increase serum sodium by more than **10–12 mmol/L in the first 24 hours** and **18 mmol/L in the first 48 hours**.
- **High-risk patients** (serum Na ≤ 105 mmol/L, hypokalemia, alcoholism, malnutrition, chronic liver disease, or pre-menopausal women): limit correction to **4–6 mmol/L per 24 hours; not to exceed 8 mmol/L per 24 hours** [14, mechanism_review].

A 2023 systematic review of 96 documented ODS cases found demyelination occurred in 10.4% of cases even with correction < 10 mmol/L per 24 hours, reinforcing the stricter 6–8 mmol/L target for high-risk groups [14, mechanism_review].

If overcorrection occurs (sodium rising too fast), desmopressin (dDAVP) plus electrolyte-free water can be used to re-lower sodium — so-called "rescue re-lowering."

**Hypernatremia → Cerebral Edema.** The converse danger exists in chronic hypernatremia: brain cells accumulate osmolytes (idiogenic osmoles) to defend against cell shrinkage. Correcting hypernatremia faster than approximately **10–12 mmol/L per 24 hours** risks cerebral edema, seizures, and brainstem herniation.

### Hypernatremia (Na > 145 mmol/L): Free-Water Deficit

Hypernatremia almost always reflects a free-water deficit relative to total body solute — either inadequate intake, excessive insensible loss (fever, burns), diabetes insipidus, or, rarely, sodium excess. The free-water deficit is estimated as [1, regulatory; 10, regulatory]:

**FWD (L) = TBW × [(current Na / target Na) − 1]**

Where TBW ≈ 0.6 × body weight in kg (young males), 0.5 × weight (young females / elderly males), or 0.45 × weight (elderly females).

Example: 70 kg male, measured Na = 158 mmol/L, target = 145 mmol/L: TBW = 42 L; FWD = 42 × [(158/145) − 1] = 42 × 0.090 = **3.8 L**. This estimate covers the deficit only; ongoing losses must be replaced in addition.

## Measurement & Standardization

### Ion-Selective Electrode (ISE): The Modern Standard

Serum sodium is measured in virtually all contemporary clinical laboratories using ion-selective electrode (ISE) potentiometry. The ISE consists of a sodium-selective membrane — typically incorporating a neutral carrier or glass electrode — that generates a potential difference proportional to the logarithm of the sodium ion (Na⁺) activity in the sample (the Nernst equation). That activity is then converted to concentration using a calibration derived from reference standards. ISE has displaced flame photometry as the dominant platform because it requires no combustible gas, is fully automatable on high-throughput chemistry analyzers, and operates with small sample volumes [15, mechanism_review].

### The Load-Bearing Distinction: Direct vs. Indirect ISE

ISE analyzers operate in one of two fundamentally different modes, and the difference has major clinical consequences.

**Direct ISE** presents the sample to the electrode membrane **without prior dilution**. This is the method used by point-of-care devices and blood-gas analyzers (e.g., Radiometer ABL series, Siemens RAPIDPoint). Because no dilution step occurs, the electrode responds to Na⁺ activity **in the plasma water phase itself** — the phase where sodium actually exists. Plasma normally contains approximately 93% water by mass (0.93 kg/L), with the remaining ~7% occupied by proteins and lipids. Direct ISE measures sodium in that aqueous phase, giving a result that reflects the physiologically meaningful quantity: sodium activity in plasma water [15, mechanism_review; 16, cohort].

**Indirect ISE** — used by the large automated chemistry analyzers that process the majority of hospital specimens (e.g., Roche Cobas, Abbott Architect, Siemens Dimension) — **dilutes the sample** (typically 1:20 to 1:34 with an ionic-strength adjustment buffer) before presenting it to the electrode. The calculation that converts the measured activity back to a reported plasma sodium concentration **assumes that plasma water constitutes exactly 0.93 kg/L** (i.e., the normal 93% water fraction). In a healthy person, this assumption is essentially correct, and indirect ISE gives a result closely comparable to direct ISE and to the historical flame photometry reference [15, mechanism_review; 19, mechanism_review].

### Pseudohyponatremia: The Electrolyte Exclusion Effect

The critical failure mode of indirect ISE arises when the 0.93 kg/L plasma-water assumption breaks down — specifically in **severe hyperlipidemia** (hypertriglyceridemia, hypercholesterolemia) or **severe hyperproteinemia** (paraproteinemia from multiple myeloma, IVIG infusion, polyclonal immunoglobulin disorders). In these states, the displaced water fraction falls substantially — toward 0.80–0.85 kg/L or below — while the volume occupied by lipid or protein rises correspondingly. The indirect ISE method, still assuming 0.93 kg/L of water per liter of plasma, systematically underestimates the sodium concentration. This artifact is called **pseudohyponatremia**, and its mechanism is the **electrolyte exclusion effect**: sodium (which exists only in the water phase) is excluded from the solid-phase volume, but the dilution-based method attributes that volume to the aqueous compartment [15, mechanism_review; 17, mechanism_review].

The magnitude is clinically significant. Studies comparing indirect ISE against direct ISE in patients with elevated total protein (> 7.9 g/dL) found an average sodium underestimation of **–6.1 mmol/L**, with 70% of hyperproteinemic specimens showing a clinically meaningful discrepancy [16, cohort]. One case series found that **43% of ICU patients** exceeded the 4 mmol/L threshold for clinically relevant disagreement between methods [15, mechanism_review]. In extreme cases — for example, severe hypertriglyceridemia (triglycerides > 15 mmol/L) — the sodium artifact can be quantified as approximately **1 mmol/L per 10 mmol/L increment in total lipids** [20, cohort]. In IVIG-treated patients, where exogenous immunoglobulin sharply raises total protein (documented cases: 8.0 → 9.6 g/dL), indirect ISE can underestimate true plasma sodium by **6 mmol/L or more**, with published corrective formulas proving unreliable in these settings [21, cohort]. Hypercholesterolemia — less commonly recognized than hypertriglyceridemia — can also drive pseudohyponatremia; routine lipemia indices may fail to flag cholesterol-mediated interference, since those indices are calibrated for triglyceride-based turbidity [22, cohort].

**Direct ISE is immune to pseudohyponatremia.** Because no dilution step is applied, the plasma solid fraction does not enter the calculation. Direct ISE measures what is actually present in the water phase, regardless of how elevated lipid or protein may be [16, cohort; 18, cohort]. This is why confirmation of a suspected pseudohyponatremia requires reflexing the specimen to a blood-gas analyzer using the direct ISE mode — or using plasma (rather than whole blood) validated for that instrument [18, cohort].

**Historical flame photometry shared this vulnerability.** Before ISE became universal, flame emission photometry was the reference method for serum sodium. Because flame photometry **also dilutes the sample** before measurement, it is **equally susceptible to the electrolyte exclusion effect** — pseudohyponatremia was first characterized as a flame photometry artifact before indirect ISE was understood to share the same vulnerability [15, mechanism_review]. Flame photometry is now largely replaced in clinical chemistry by ISE, though it remains the historical anchor for traceability.

### Standardization and Metrological Traceability

Sodium measurement traceability in clinical laboratories runs through the **NIST Standard Reference Material (SRM) 956 series** — frozen human serum certified for sodium, potassium, chloride, and several other electrolytes. The most recent version, SRM 956e (issued December 2024), provides three concentration levels spanning the clinically relevant range, with certified sodium values traceable to primary methods. The SRM 956 series was developed in conjunction with the **Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) documentary standard C29-A2**, which codifies the standardization of ISE systems to the flame photometric reference method. This traceability chain grounds both direct and indirect ISE calibration against a common metrological anchor [23, mechanism_review].

One measurement-mode consequence of this traceability architecture is that **direct ISE and indirect ISE can report systematically different values in normal specimens**. Direct ISE measures Na⁺ activity in plasma water (molal scale), which — after accounting for the molal activity coefficient of sodium in plasma (~0.747) and the normal plasma water fraction — tends to read **2–7 mmol/L higher** than indirect ISE in method-comparison studies under normal physiological conditions [15, mechanism_review; 19, mechanism_review]. Because of this systematic offset, laboratories using direct ISE must apply **method-specific reference intervals** rather than assuming the canonical 135–145 mmol/L range (which was established with indirect ISE / flame photometry platforms).

### Pre-analytic Artifacts

Two pre-analytic errors are particularly important for sodium:

**IV-line contamination (spuriously HIGH sodium).** Drawing blood from a line above an infusing normal saline (0.9% NaCl, ~154 mmol/L Na) introduces saline into the sample, producing a falsely elevated sodium result. This spurious hypernatremia is recognized by an implausibly high sodium combined with dilution of other analytes (low creatinine, low albumin, abnormal glucose) in the same specimen. The solution is to discard an adequate volume before collection or to redraw from a distal site [15, mechanism_review].

**Serum vs. plasma vs. whole blood.** Small systematic differences exist between matrices. In head-to-head comparison of whole blood (direct ISE on blood-gas analyzer) and plasma (same direct ISE), the average difference is modest (~1.3 mmol/L), well within clinical tolerance in most settings [18, cohort]. Serum and lithium-heparin plasma perform equivalently for sodium under standard conditions.

## Determinants & Clinical Significance

Serum sodium ([Na⁺]) is a **concentration** — it reflects the ratio of sodium to total body water, not total-body sodium stores. Interpreting a result in isolation is insufficient; volume status, serum osmolality, blood glucose, and the clinical context must all be integrated [1, regulatory]. Two directions of derangement dominate clinical practice: hyponatremia (< 135 mmol/L) and hypernatremia (> 145 mmol/L). Both carry independent prognostic weight that extends well beyond their immediate biochemical danger.

### Hyponatremia (< 135 mmol/L): The Most Common Electrolyte Disorder

Hyponatremia affects approximately 14–30% of hospitalized adults and is the most frequent electrolyte abnormality encountered in clinical practice [28, cohort]. The European clinical practice guideline published by Spasovski et al. in 2014, developed jointly by three European medical societies (ESICM, ESE, ERA-EDTA), defines the threshold at < 135 mmol/L and classifies severity as mild (130–134), moderate (125–129), and profound (< 125) [1, regulatory]. Understanding the mechanism requires classifying by volume status, because treatment differs fundamentally across subtypes.

#### Hypovolemic Hyponatremia

The patient has lost both sodium and water, but has replaced more of the water than the sodium — often through thirst and hypotonic fluid intake. Causes include:

- **Thiazide diuretics** (particularly thiazides, not loop diuretics, because thiazides blunt dilution in the distal tubule while loop diuretics spare this mechanism)
- Vomiting and diarrhea (sodium-rich GI fluid loss)
- Third-spacing (pancreatitis, burns, peritonitis)
- **Adrenal insufficiency** (aldosterone deficiency → renal salt wasting)
- Salt-wasting nephropathy (tubulointerstitial disease, post-obstructive diuresis)
- **Cerebral salt wasting** (natriuretic peptide–driven sodium loss from CNS injury — distinguished from SIADH by volume depletion, not euvolemia; treatment is volume and sodium replacement, not fluid restriction)

#### Euvolemic Hyponatremia

Clinically, the patient appears euvolemic; there is water retention without overt edema. The dominant mechanism is non-osmotic ADH (vasopressin) secretion driving excessive renal water retention.

**SIADH (Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion)** is the most common cause of euvolemic hyponatremia. Diagnostic criteria require hypotonic hyponatremia with an inappropriately concentrated urine (> 100 mOsm/kg), elevated urine [Na⁺] (typically > 40 mmol/L), clinical euvolemia, and exclusion of hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, and diuretic use. Major precipitants include [1, regulatory]:

- **Drugs**: SSRIs, carbamazepine, antipsychotics (particularly haloperidol), vincristine, cyclophosphamide, NSAIDs
- **CNS disease**: stroke, subarachnoid hemorrhage, traumatic brain injury, meningitis, encephalitis
- **Pulmonary disease**: pneumonia, TB, ARDS, positive-pressure ventilation
- **Malignancy**: small-cell lung cancer (the classic culprit — ectopic ADH production), lymphoma, pancreatic carcinoma
- **Post-operative/procedural**: pain and nausea are potent non-osmotic ADH stimuli; a major source of hospital-acquired hyponatremia
- **Primary polydipsia and beer potomania / low-solute intake**: excessive water ingestion overwhelms renal diluting capacity (primary polydipsia) or insufficient solute substrate limits the kidney's ability to excrete free water even without elevated ADH (beer potomania)

Other euvolemic causes include **hypothyroidism** (reduced GFR and ADH release) and **glucocorticoid deficiency** (cortisol deficiency stimulates ADH and impairs cardiac output, reducing free-water excretion).

#### Hypervolemic Hyponatremia

Edematous states in which effective arterial blood volume (EABV) is perceived as low by baroreceptors, triggering non-osmotic ADH release and avid renal sodium retention — yet total-body water and sodium are both expanded, with the dilutional effect on sodium predominating:

- **Heart failure**: reduced cardiac output → neurohormonal activation → ADH + aldosterone + sympathetic nervous system → free-water and sodium retention
- **Cirrhosis**: splanchnic vasodilation → reduced EABV → same neurohormonal cascade; hyponatremia is so prognostically relevant that serum sodium was incorporated into the MELD-Na score [25, cohort]
- **Nephrotic syndrome**: oncotic pressure loss → edema with reduced effective intravascular volume
- **Advanced renal failure**: diminished capacity to excrete free water

#### Exercise-Associated Hyponatremia (EAH)

EAH is a distinct and potentially fatal variant relevant to endurance athletes. The primary mechanism is over-drinking hypotonic fluid in excess of free-water losses, often compounded by non-osmotic ADH release (pain, nausea, exertion). Almond et al. (NEJM, 2005) prospectively studied 488 Boston Marathon runners at finish-line blood draw and found 13% had hyponatremia (≤ 135 mmol/L), with 0.6% reaching critical hyponatremia (≤ 120 mmol/L) [24, cohort]. On multivariate analysis, weight gain during the race (OR 4.2, 95% CI 2.2–8.2) and race time > 4 hours (OR 7.4 vs. < 3:30 hours, 95% CI 2.9–23.1) were the strongest independent predictors [24, cohort]. Severe EAH has caused deaths from cerebral herniation; the prevention strategy is drinking to thirst — not to a schedule — and avoiding hypotonic fluids.

### Hypernatremia (> 145 mmol/L): A Free-Water Deficit

Hypernatremia almost always represents a deficit of water relative to sodium — pure free-water loss or insufficient intake, occasionally combined with hypertonic sodium gain. Because the thirst mechanism is a powerful defense, sustained hypernatremia usually requires impaired access to water or impaired thirst perception, so it disproportionately affects elderly patients, infants, the unconscious, and those with neurological disease.

Principal causes include:

- **Inadequate water intake**: impaired consciousness, physical disability, dementia, extreme age, iatrogenic fluid restriction
- **Central diabetes insipidus**: ADH deficiency from posterior pituitary or hypothalamic damage (neurosurgery, traumatic brain injury, tumor, infiltrative disease, ischemia) — characterized by dilute urine with persistent hypernatremia; responds to desmopressin
- **Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus**: renal ADH resistance — most commonly acquired from **lithium** (downregulation of aquaporin-2 channels), **hypercalcemia** (impairs ADH signaling), chronic hypokalemia; also hereditary (AVPR2 or AQP2 mutations)
- **Osmotic diuresis**: hyperglycemia (the osmotic load drives obligatory water loss exceeding sodium loss); mannitol therapy; urea; post-contrast nephropathy
- **Gastrointestinal and skin losses**: severe watery diarrhea (rotavirus, cholera), profuse sweating, burns — particularly when free-water replacement is inadequate

Arzhan et al. (Kidney360, 2022) analyzed 1.9 million hospitalized patients and found 3% had hypernatremia (Na > 145 mEq/L) at admission, with in-hospital mortality of 12% versus 2% in normonatremic patients; severe hypernatremia (Na > 155 mEq/L) carried an adjusted in-hospital mortality OR of 34.41 (95% CI 30.59–38.71) compared with normonatremia [30, cohort].

### Prognostic Significance: Both Poles Predict Mortality

#### Hyponatremia and Mortality

The prognostic weight of hyponatremia is well-established across multiple disease contexts, with the consistent caveat that it largely acts as a **severity or illness marker** — it indexes the neurohormonal activation and hemodynamic compromise of underlying disease — rather than as the direct cause of death in most chronic cases.

**Heart failure**: Gheorghiade et al. (Eur Heart J, 2007) analyzed 48,612 patients from the OPTIMIZE-HF registry across 259 US hospitals [26, cohort]. For every 3 mmol/L decrement in admission sodium below 140 mmol/L, in-hospital mortality risk increased by 19.5% and follow-up mortality risk increased by 10%. A complementary meta-analysis of eight cohort studies (Wang et al., Heart Fail Rev, 2019; N = 5,714 acute decompensated HF patients) demonstrated that hyponatremia improvement during hospitalization reduced all-cause mortality risk (RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.53–0.80, P < 0.001), with short-term mortality reduction most marked (RR 0.54) [27, meta_analysis].

**Cirrhosis**: Kim et al. (NEJM, 2008) studied 6,769 adult candidates for liver transplantation registered with the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) in 2005–2006 [25, cohort]. Each 1 unit decrease in serum sodium between 125–140 mmol/L was associated with a hazard ratio of 1.05 for 90-day waitlist mortality (P < 0.001). Incorporating serum sodium into MELD (to create MELDNa) was estimated to have prevented 7% of 90-day waitlist deaths in the 2006 validation cohort. This finding was influential enough to drive adoption of MELDNa into organ allocation policy.

**General hospitalized patients**: Waikar, Mount, and Curhan (Am J Med, 2009) conducted a prospective cohort study of 98,411 hospitalized adults at two Boston teaching hospitals (2000–2003) and found hyponatremia in 14.5% on initial measurement [28, cohort]. After multivariable adjustment, hyponatremia was associated with increased in-hospital mortality (OR 1.47, 95% CI 1.33–1.62), 1-year mortality (HR 1.38, 95% CI 1.32–1.46), and 5-year mortality (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.21–1.30). Even mild hyponatremia (130–134 mEq/L) carried an in-hospital mortality OR of 1.37 (95% CI 1.23–1.52). Critically, resolution of hyponatremia during hospitalization attenuated this risk — consistent with the marker-vs.-cause framing [28, cohort].

**Community-acquired pneumonia**: Krüger et al. (Respir Med, 2014) in the multicentre prospective CAPNETZ cohort (N = 2,138) found a U-shaped association between serum sodium and 28-day mortality, with both hyponatremia (31.8% prevalence) and hypernatremia independently predicting 28-day mortality in multivariate Cox analysis [29, cohort].

#### Hypernatremia and Mortality

Hypernatremia carries even higher crude mortality than hyponatremia, though again much of this reflects severity of background illness [30, cohort]. In the large Arzhan et al. population-based analysis, an admission sodium > 145 mEq/L was associated with an unadjusted in-hospital mortality rate six times that of normonatremic patients (12% vs. 2%) [30, cohort].

### Symptoms and the Correction-Rate Danger

#### Acute Hyponatremia

The brain adapts to hypo-osmolality via solute extrusion over 48–72 hours (the adaptation window). When sodium falls acutely (within < 48 hours), this adaptation has not yet occurred, and the brain takes on water — producing cerebral edema with a characteristic clinical progression: headache → nausea → confusion → seizures → herniation. This is the setting where acute symptomatic hyponatremia is directly dangerous rather than merely a severity marker, and where urgent correction with hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) is warranted.

Even chronic "asymptomatic" hyponatremia is not truly benign. Renneboog et al. (Am J Med, 2006) documented in a case-control study of 122 hyponatremic patients (mean Na 126 mEq/L, mean age 72 years) that 21.3% were admitted for falls versus 5.3% of controls (adjusted OR 67, P < .001), with objective gait instability (centre-of-pressure displacement 1,336 vs. 1,047 mm, P = .003) and attention deficits (58 ms increase in reaction time, P < .001) [31, cohort].

#### Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome

Over-rapid correction of **chronic** hyponatremia is the signature iatrogenic danger. Once the brain has adapted (extruded osmolytes), a sudden osmotic gradient causes rapid water egress from brain cells — oligodendrocytes are particularly vulnerable — producing **osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)**, historically called central pontine myelinolysis. Sterns, Riggs, and Schochet (NEJM, 1986) described osmotic demyelination in a series of severely hyponatremic patients and identified that rapid correction (> 12 mmol/L/day) was associated with neurological sequelae [32, cohort]. Current consensus guidelines cap correction more conservatively at ≤ 6–8 mmol/L per 24 hours (the 2014 European guideline uses 8 mmol/L/24h as the ceiling) [1, regulatory], reflecting subsequent evidence that even 12 mmol/L/day can be harmful in high-risk patients (chronic alcoholism, malnutrition, hypokalemia, liver disease). When accidental overcorrection occurs, re-lowering serum sodium with hypotonic fluids ± DDAVP is the rescue strategy.

#### Hypernatremia Correction

Over-rapid correction of hypernatremia carries the symmetric danger: the chronically dehydrated brain (having accumulated idiogenic osmoles to prevent shrinkage) is at risk of cerebral edema if sodium is corrected too fast. Correction should not exceed 10–12 mmol/L per 24 hours.

### Interpretive Limitations

Serum [Na⁺] is a concentration — the ratio of sodium to water in the sampled fluid, not a measure of total-body sodium stores. Two critical artifacts must be excluded:

1. **Pseudohyponatremia**: Extreme hyperlipidemia or hyperproteinemia (e.g., multiple myeloma, severe hypertriglyceridemia) displaces plasma water when sodium is measured by indirect ISE or flame photometry — yielding a falsely low sodium despite normal plasma osmolality. Only direct ISE is immune, because it measures the undiluted plasma water phase. Modern chemistry analyzers predominantly use indirect ISE, so this artifact remains clinically relevant when lipids or protein are grossly elevated [15, mechanism_review; 16, cohort].

2. **Translocational hyponatremia**: Osmotically active solutes that cannot freely cross cell membranes — most importantly severe hyperglycemia, but also mannitol — draw water from the intracellular compartment into plasma, diluting [Na⁺] without a true excess of free water. Two correction factors are in clinical use: the Katz 1973 factor (1.6 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL glucose above normal) and the Hillier 1999 factor (2.4 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL, now generally preferred as it better captures the nonlinear dilutional effect at high glucose) [13, cohort]. This is not hypotonic hyponatremia and requires glucose correction, not sodium supplementation.

The obligatory framework for interpretation therefore layers serum osmolality over the measured sodium, then integrates volume status (edema, skin turgor, mucous membranes, orthostatic vitals, urine output, urine sodium and osmolality) to classify hypo-, eu-, or hypervolemic etiology before any treatment is initiated.
