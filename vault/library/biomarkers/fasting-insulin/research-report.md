# Fasting Insulin: Canonical Research Report

## Summary

Fasting insulin is a peripheral venous blood measurement taken after a minimum 8-hour overnight fast, reported in µIU/mL (equivalently µU/mL or mIU/L; convert to pmol/L by multiplying by 6.0). A result of 2.52–13.14 µIU/mL (15–79 pmol/L) falls within the reference interval derived from a large, metabolically screened Brazilian cohort using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay [13, cohort], and postprandial concentrations in healthy adults rise from this baseline to roughly 250–300 pmol/L at 30 minutes after oral glucose [2, mechanism_review].

**The most important single fact about fasting insulin: there is no universally validated diagnostic threshold.** Unlike fasting glucose or HbA1c, fasting insulin has no harmonized reference measurement procedure and no universal cutpoint adopted by the ADA, IFCC, or AACE. The between-assay coefficient of variation across 12 commercial platforms is 12–66%, with a median of 24% [12, cohort]. The same blood sample measured on two different immunoassay platforms can yield results differing by a quarter of the measured value under routine conditions. Reference intervals are assay-specific, population-specific, and should not be transferred across labs. Interpreting fasting insulin as an absolute number without knowing the assay is a misuse of the biomarker.

Within those limits, fasting insulin is clinically valuable precisely because it is the earliest detectable signal of insulin resistance — rising years or decades before fasting glucose leaves the normal range [26, mechanism_review]. A cross-sectional study of 1,313 young Indian adults found that 30.5% displayed hyperinsulinemia with completely normal glucose and HbA1c [26, mechanism_review]. The prognostic literature consistently associates elevated fasting insulin and HOMA-IR with incident type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and hepatic steatosis. Fasting insulin is also a bidirectional signal: a low or normal value can represent either genuine insulin sensitivity or late β-cell failure with exhausted compensatory capacity — clinical context is required to differentiate the two [27, mechanism_review].

Measurement quality is fragile. Hemolysis is the dominant pre-analytical hazard: insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE) released from lysed erythrocytes degrades insulin at a rate that renders even mildly hemolyzed samples unreportable [22, mechanism_review]. Heparin plasma yields values approximately 15% lower than serum [24, cohort]. Within-subject biological variation is approximately 25% CV day-to-day [25, cohort], meaning a serial change of ~71% or more is required to confidently attribute a difference to a true biological change rather than physiological fluctuation.

## Physiology & What Fasting Insulin Measures

### Insulin Biosynthesis in the Pancreatic β-Cell

Insulin originates in the pancreatic β-cell as a 110-amino-acid precursor, preproinsulin. Within seconds of ribosomal translation, the N-terminal signal peptide directs co-translational translocation across the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane, where signal peptidase removes the peptide and generates proinsulin — an 86-amino-acid single-chain molecule consisting of the B chain, connecting peptide (C-peptide), and A chain in tandem [1, mechanism_review]. Proinsulin undergoes oxidative folding in the ER lumen, forming three conserved disulfide bonds with assistance from ER chaperones, before trafficking through the Golgi apparatus to the trans-Golgi network, where it is packaged into immature secretory granules [1, mechanism_review].

Within the maturing granule, the endoproteases PC1/3 and PC2 cleave proinsulin at two dibasic sites, excising the C-peptide and yielding mature two-chain insulin in equimolar amounts. At least 99% of intragranular proinsulin undergoes this conversion, storing insulin and C-peptide in a 1:1 molar ratio alongside small quantities of incompletely processed split-proinsulin intermediates [1, mechanism_review]. Under normal physiological conditions, these split products and intact proinsulin represent a minor fraction of total β-cell secretory output, though they rise disproportionately when β-cell secretory capacity is stressed [2, mechanism_review].

### Fasting (Basal) Insulin Secretion and Its Determinants

In the overnight-fasted state, circulating glucose concentrations are low and stable, and insulin secretion is accordingly minimal compared with the postprandial period. Basal insulin secretory tone is set by the intersection of ambient nutrient sensing, autonomic input, and incretin background, sustaining enough portal insulin delivery to suppress hepatic glucose production while avoiding hypoglycaemia [2, mechanism_review].

Critically, basal insulin secretion is not tonically flat. Healthy β-cells exhibit rhythmic, pulsatile secretory bursts with a principal oscillatory period of approximately 10–14 minutes. Lang et al. (1982) measured plasma insulin at one-minute intervals in 28 fasted normal subjects and found synchronous oscillations with a mean period of 10.7 minutes and an amplitude of approximately 1.1 mU/L [3, cohort]. This pulsatility is driven in part by glycolytic oscillations within the β-cell, linking mitochondrial metabolism to rhythmic Ca²⁺ influx and exocytosis. Superimposed on these ~10-minute ultradian cycles are faster, ~5-minute oscillations observed across multiple species. Juhl et al. (2002), in a within-subject study of 7 healthy humans, demonstrated that during prolonged fasting the frequency of insulin secretory bursts is preserved (6.3 ± 0.4 minutes per pulse versus 6.1 ± 0.4 in the fed state; P = NS), while burst amplitude falls significantly (10.1 ± 1.7 vs. 16.0 ± 3.2 pmol/L per pulse, P < 0.05) [4, cohort]. Fasting insulin secretion is therefore modulated primarily through changes in secretory burst amplitude, not frequency — a distinction with clinical relevance, since loss of oscillatory amplitude is an early marker of β-cell dysfunction in type 2 diabetes and obesity.

### Co-Secretion with C-Peptide

Because C-peptide and insulin are cleaved from a single proinsulin precursor and stored together in the same secretory granule, they are released in equimolar amounts into the portal circulation on every secretory event [5, mechanism_review]. This stoichiometric co-secretion is the basis for using C-peptide as a surrogate of β-cell secretory output. However, the two molecules diverge sharply once they enter the hepatic portal vein.

Insulin undergoes substantial first-pass hepatic extraction on its initial transit through the liver. Direct measurements in dogs using paired portal/peripheral infusion protocols found a mean hepatic first-pass extraction fraction of 50.4% (SD ±19.1%; range 22.4–77.2%) (canine study) [6, animal]. This extraction is tightly coupled to hepatic insulin sensitivity: subjects with lower insulin sensitivity extract a smaller fraction on first pass, resulting in higher peripheral insulin concentrations for any given secretion rate (correlation rs = −0.64, P = 0.03) [6, animal]. C-peptide, in contrast, is not extracted by hepatocytes in any appreciable amount and passes through the liver essentially intact, conferring a longer and more stable plasma half-life [7, mechanism_review]. Polonsky and Rubenstein (1984) formalized the practical consequence: peripheral C-peptide concentrations reflect β-cell secretory output far more faithfully than peripheral insulin concentrations do, because insulin levels are confounded by variable hepatic clearance [7, mechanism_review].

### Portal-vs-Peripheral Insulin Concentrations

Because the liver extracts approximately 40–60% of portal insulin on first pass, the concentration of insulin in the portal vein substantially exceeds that measured in peripheral venous or arterial blood. Under normal physiology, portal (hepatic sinusoidal) insulin concentrations are 2- to 3-fold greater than arterial concentrations (canine model) — a gradient that is physiologically critical: the liver is exquisitely sensitive to insulin's suppression of hepatic glucose output at portal concentrations that are well above what peripheral muscle and adipose tissue encounter [8, animal]. This gradient is lost when insulin is delivered subcutaneously (as in treated diabetes), producing relative hepatic insulin deficiency alongside peripheral hyperinsulinaemia.

### What a Peripheral Fasting Insulin Measurement Does and Does Not Index

A fasting peripheral venous insulin value is the net product of two upstream variables: (i) basal β-cell secretory output and (ii) hepatic first-pass extraction efficiency. It is therefore a composite biomarker.

In practical terms, a fasting peripheral insulin that is elevated can reflect either excess secretion (β-cell hypersecretion in response to insulin resistance) or reduced hepatic clearance (lower extraction fraction in insulin-resistant individuals) — and typically reflects both simultaneously, since hepatic extraction varies inversely with insulin resistance [6, animal].

When fasting insulin is combined with fasting glucose in the HOMA-IR formula [(fasting insulin µIU/mL × fasting glucose mg/dL) / 405], the resulting index correlates primarily with hepatic insulin resistance — specifically, with the liver's failure to adequately suppress glucose production in response to prevailing insulin concentrations [9, mechanism_review]. Hoffman (2008) confirmed in adolescents that HOMA-IR and QUICKI correlate significantly with hepatic insulin resistance (HIR) but not with peripheral (muscle) insulin sensitivity (SI*) [10, cohort]. Importantly, HOMA-IR does not directly quantify whole-body or peripheral (skeletal muscle) insulin sensitivity; that domain requires dynamic testing such as the hyperinsulinaemic-euglycaemic clamp or insulin suppression test.

A peripheral fasting insulin therefore encodes:
- The integral of basal β-cell secretory tone over the preceding hours
- The degree of hepatic insulin clearance efficiency
- An inverse signal of hepatic insulin resistance (higher fasting insulin → reduced hepatic suppression of glucose output)

It does **not** directly index skeletal muscle or adipose tissue insulin sensitivity, which require glucose infusion/clamp methods to isolate.

## Reference Ranges, Units & Indices

### The Fundamental Caveat: No Harmonized Diagnostic Cutpoint

Fasting insulin has no universally agreed diagnostic threshold — a fact that is load-bearing for every number in this section. Unlike HbA1c or fasting glucose, which benefit from decades of assay-harmonization efforts anchored to clinical outcome data, fasting insulin values are both **assay-dependent** and **population-dependent**. A result of 10 µIU/mL on one platform does not mean the same thing as 10 µIU/mL on another, and a reference interval derived from a healthy Brazilian cohort does not automatically apply to a South Asian population at matched BMI. Treating any single number from the ranges below as a validated diagnostic cutpoint is a misuse of this biomarker.

### Units and Conversion

Clinical reports use two unit systems:

- **µIU/mL** (also written mU/L or µU/mL — numerically equivalent) is the dominant convention in North American laboratories.
- **pmol/L** is preferred in many European and international contexts.

**Conversion factor:** The WHO-recommended conversion is **1 µIU/mL = 6.00 pmol/L** [11, mechanism_review]. An older international standard uses **6.945**, derived from the specific activity of an earlier insulin reference preparation; this factor is still encountered in some published equations and older assay calibrations [12, cohort]. The difference is ~15% — enough to shift a borderline value across an interpretive category — so when comparing values across publications or platforms, confirm which conversion factor the source used.

Throughout this report, insulin is expressed in **µIU/mL with pmol/L equivalents in parentheses** using the 6.00 factor unless otherwise noted.

### Adult Fasting Insulin Reference Ranges

Laboratory reference ranges for fasting insulin are wide and assay-specific. A commonly cited broad range in adult clinical practice is approximately **2–25 µIU/mL (12–150 pmol/L)**, though the upper bound varies substantially across platforms.

A large Brazilian database study (n = 21,684 healthy adults, BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m², glucose < 100 mg/dL, HbA1c < 5.7%) using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA, Roche Cobas) derived the following intervals:

- **Overall: 2.52–13.14 µIU/mL (15.1–78.8 pmol/L)**
- Women: 2.54–13.30 µIU/mL (15.2–79.8 pmol/L)
- Men: 2.43–11.89 µIU/mL (14.6–71.3 pmol/L)

These intervals were notably narrower than manufacturer-supplied ranges, illustrating how reference values anchored to a genuinely healthy, metabolically screened cohort differ from population averages [13, cohort].

A Nepalese study (n = 135 healthy adults, BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m², chemiluminescence immunoassay) derived a reference interval of **2.63–14.56 µIU/mL** (median 7.69 µIU/mL), higher than the Brazilian data despite similar BMI screening — an instance of the "South Asian phenotype," where greater insulin secretion accompanies comparable glucose levels [14, cohort].

In NHANES samples of US adults (non-diabetic), mean fasting insulin has been reported at approximately **9.3 µIU/mL** (55.8 pmol/L), with the distribution right-skewed and typically log-transformed for statistical analyses [15, cohort]. The NHANES program itself applied cycle-to-cycle assay adjustment via regression equations to account for platform changes — a concrete illustration that raw insulin values are not directly comparable across assay generations [15, cohort].

**The "optimal" or "insulin-sensitive" framing:** A lower fasting insulin in the context of normal glucose is a physiological marker of higher insulin sensitivity — a framing commonly used in functional medicine and metabolic health contexts. No specific numeric cutpoint for this "optimal" range has been validated or adopted by any major regulatory body, in part because of the assay standardization problem.

### Assay Dependency: Why Numbers Don't Transfer Across Labs

The American Diabetes Association Workgroup on Insulin Immunoassay Standardization evaluated 12 commercial insulin methods from 9 manufacturers and found:

- Within-assay CV: 3.7–39.0% (7 of 10 assays ≤ 10.6%)
- **Between-assay CV: 12–66%, median 24%**
- Only 6 of 10 assays met clinical acceptability (total error ≤ 32% based on biological variability criteria)
- A common reference preparation failed to reduce between-assay disagreement [12, cohort]

A median between-assay CV of 24% means that two laboratories running the same sample could produce results differing by roughly one-quarter of the measured value under typical conditions. The primary sources of discordance include differing antibody specificity, variable cross-reactivity with proinsulin split forms — des(64,65) proinsulin cross-reacted at > 40% in 9 of 10 assays — and platform-specific calibration [12, cohort]. This is the mechanistic reason no universal reference interval exists: the number a laboratory reports is inseparable from the assay it uses.

### Computed Indices

Because a single fasting insulin value is hard to interpret without knowing the concurrent glucose level, several computed indices integrate both measurements to estimate insulin sensitivity or β-cell function.

**HOMA-IR (Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance)**

$$\text{HOMA-IR} = \frac{\text{Fasting insulin (µIU/mL)} \times \text{Fasting glucose (mg/dL)}}{405}$$

Equivalently, with glucose in mmol/L:

$$\text{HOMA-IR} = \frac{\text{Fasting insulin (µIU/mL)} \times \text{Fasting glucose (mmol/L)}}{22.5}$$

The denominator normalizes the result so that a healthy lean individual with insulin ~5 µIU/mL and glucose ~81 mg/dL (4.5 mmol/L) yields HOMA-IR ≈ 1.0 [16, mechanism_review]. HOMA-IR < 1.0 is widely referenced as "optimal" insulin sensitivity. Values > 1.9 suggest early insulin resistance; values > 2.9 indicate significant insulin resistance in most frameworks [16, mechanism_review]. The Brazilian reference interval (screened healthy adults, ECLIA assay) was **0.39–2.86** overall; the Nepalese study derived **0.56–3.50** [13, cohort; B4, cohort]. The span across these two populations underscores population-dependence of HOMA-IR cutpoints.

No HOMA-IR value has been universally validated as a clinical diagnostic threshold. The ADA's "Use and Abuse of HOMA Modeling" article explicitly cautions that threshold cutpoints depend on the assay platform used to measure insulin [16, mechanism_review].

**QUICKI (Quantitative Insulin Sensitivity Check Index)**

$$\text{QUICKI} = \frac{1}{\log(\text{Fasting insulin, µIU/mL}) + \log(\text{Fasting glucose, mg/dL})}$$

A higher QUICKI indicates greater insulin sensitivity. Validated against the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, QUICKI demonstrated a correlation of r = 0.78 with directly measured insulin sensitivity — superior to the minimal model (r = 0.57) in the original validation study [17, cohort]. The Nepalese reference interval was **0.32–0.42** [14, cohort]. Values ≤ 0.30–0.33 are associated with insulin resistance in most published series, though no universal cutpoint is recognized.

**HOMA-β (β-Cell Function)**

$$\text{HOMA-β (\%)} = \frac{20 \times \text{Fasting insulin (µIU/mL)}}{\text{Fasting glucose (mmol/L)} - 3.5}$$

HOMA-β estimates β-cell secretory capacity relative to a healthy young adult reference (100%) [16, mechanism_review]. A value ≥ 100% is considered normal function. HOMA-β is most useful longitudinally or in relative comparison, not as a standalone diagnostic number.

**Fasting Glucose-to-Insulin Ratio (FGIR)**

$$\text{FGIR} = \frac{\text{Fasting glucose (mg/dL)}}{\text{Fasting insulin (µIU/mL)}}$$

A higher ratio reflects better insulin sensitivity (glucose is controlled with less insulin). In girls with premature adrenarche, FGIR < 7 achieved 87% sensitivity and 89% specificity for insulin resistance [18, cohort]. In women with PCOS, FGIR < 4.5 performed better than HOMA-IR in one cohort [18, cohort]. Population-specific cutpoints vary; no universal adult threshold exists.

### Population Distribution Notes

South Asian adults demonstrate systematically higher fasting insulin levels than matched Caucasian controls at equivalent BMI and glucose, reflecting ethnic differences in insulin secretion and peripheral sensitivity that require ethnicity-specific reference intervals [14, cohort]. NHANES fasting insulin values should not be used to validate absolute thresholds on other platforms; the published NHANES insulin values are regression-adjusted estimates, not raw assay outputs [15, cohort].

## Measurement & Standardization

### Assay Non-Standardization: The Central Problem

Fasting insulin occupies an unusual position in clinical laboratory medicine: it is routinely ordered, widely interpreted, and yet its measurement remains among the least harmonized of all common analytes. Unlike glucose or HbA1c — both of which have well-established reference measurement procedures (RMPs) and enforced traceability chains — insulin immunoassays differ substantially from one another in calibration, antibody specificity, and reportable range, making numeric results from different platforms functionally non-interchangeable.

The American Diabetes Association convened a formal Insulin Standardization Workgroup to characterize the scope of this problem. In a landmark evaluation of 12 commercial insulin methods from 9 manufacturers — testing a panel of 39 single-donor serum specimens — the Workgroup found among-assay CVs ranging from 12% to 66%, with a median inter-assay CV of 24% [12, cohort]. Within-assay imprecision was more acceptable (CV 3.7%–39.0%, with 7 of 10 assays ≤ 10.6%), but the cross-platform disagreement dwarfed analytical imprecision as the dominant source of measurement error [12, cohort]. Critically, supplying a common insulin reference preparation to recalibrate assays did not meaningfully reduce the inter-assay scatter, indicating that calibration alone cannot correct for fundamental antibody-specificity and matrix-interaction differences between platforms [12, cohort].

A subsequent study by Miller and colleagues (the Insulin Standardization Work Group, 2009) confirmed the degree of bias relative to a higher-order reference. Using isotope dilution-liquid chromatography/tandem mass spectrometry (ID-LC-MS/MS) calibrated against purified recombinant insulin as the comparator, 7 of 10 commercial assays showed bias exceeding 15.5% in 36%–100% of individual samples [19, cohort]. Calibration using a panel of native human sera was identified as the most effective strategy for reducing inter-method disagreement across the full measuring range [19, cohort].

### The IFCC Working Group and Reference Measurement Infrastructure

The IFCC Working Group on Standardization of Insulin Assays (WG-SIA) and the ADA-affiliated Insulin Standardization Work Group have jointly pursued a traceability architecture modeled on the ISO metrological hierarchy: a primary certified reference material (high-purity recombinant human insulin with assigned mass-unit values) → a trueness-based RMP → secondary calibrators for commercial use [20, regulatory]. Both groups developed ID-MS candidate RMPs and negotiated with the World Health Organization to obtain the required certified primary calibrators [20, regulatory]. As of the most recent status reports, harmonization of commercial immunoassays to the reference system remains incomplete: even after recalibration exercises, mean inter-method CVs decreased from approximately 22% to approximately 17% — an improvement, but still far above the ≤5% threshold considered acceptable for clinical interchangeability [20, regulatory].

A 2025 publication described a validated candidate RMP for human insulin in serum: immunoaffinity extraction followed by ID-LC-MS/MS, providing method-independent accuracy traceable to SI units [21, mechanism_review]. The JCTLM (Joint Committee for Traceability in Laboratory Medicine) database lists these LC-MS/MS procedures as the higher-order reference methods, though they remain research tools rather than routine clinical platforms [20, regulatory].

### Assay Methods: From RIA to Mass Spectrometry

**Radioimmunoassay (RIA).** RIA was the original clinical method, using a polyclonal anti-insulin antibody in a competitive displacement format. RIA antibodies are typically less specific than two-site immunometric antibodies and show greater cross-reactivity with proinsulin and its processing intermediates. Many reference-range values and early HOMA-IR derivations were established using RIA, making direct comparison with contemporary assays methodologically problematic.

**Two-site immunometric assays (ELISA, chemiluminescent immunoassay).** Modern platforms predominantly use sandwich immunometric designs — one capture antibody and one detection antibody binding distinct epitopes on the insulin molecule. Two-site formats generally confer higher specificity for intact insulin than RIA because recognition of both epitopes is required for signal generation. Chemiluminescent immunoassays (CLIAs) on automated platforms (e.g., Abbott ARCHITECT, Roche Elecsys, Beckman Coulter Access) are the dominant contemporary clinical format.

**LC-MS/MS.** Isotope dilution LC-MS/MS represents the current reference-measurement standard. After immunoaffinity capture to enrich insulin from the complex serum matrix, the analyte is quantified by tandem mass spectrometry using a stable-isotope-labeled internal standard, providing absolute molar specificity without antibody cross-reactivity [21, mechanism_review]. This method defines what insulin "really is" in a reference material but is not currently deployed in clinical routine.

### Cross-Reactivity with Proinsulin and Split Products

Insulin biosynthesis passes through proinsulin, which is cleaved to mature insulin plus C-peptide, generating partially processed intermediates — split (32,33) proinsulin and des-(64,65) proinsulin — along the way. These precursors share structural epitopes with mature insulin and can cross-react with assay antibodies. The ADA Workgroup characterized specificity systematically across 10 methods [12, cohort]:

- **Intact proinsulin:** < 2% cross-reactivity in 9 of 10 assays — acceptably low across nearly all platforms.
- **Split (32,33) proinsulin:** < 3% cross-reactivity in 8 of 10 assays — similarly well-discriminated.
- **Des-(64,65) proinsulin:** exceeded 40% cross-reactivity in 9 of 10 assays — a near-universal failure mode [12, cohort].

Des-(64,65) proinsulin retains the C-terminal B-chain sequence that many antibodies use for insulin recognition, explaining the high and consistent cross-reactivity. In normal fasting physiology, des-(64,65) proinsulin concentrations are low enough that this artifact is minor. In subjects with β-cell dysfunction or insulin resistance — precisely the population where fasting insulin testing is most clinically informative — elevated proinsulin intermediates can produce spuriously elevated apparent insulin values. The degree of inflation is assay-specific and unpredictable in magnitude.

### Pre-Analytical Requirements

**Hemolysis.** The single most clinically impactful pre-analytical hazard for insulin measurement is hemolysis. Erythrocytes contain insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE, also called insulysin), a zinc metallopeptidase that cleaves insulin at multiple sites [22, mechanism_review]. When red cells lyse — whether from difficult venipuncture, rough handling, delay in separation, or temperature excursion — IDE is released into the sample and degrades insulin at a rate proportional to hemolysis severity, lysate concentration, and time at ambient temperature. Even slight hemolysis (hemoglobin ~0.5 g/L) measurably lowers insulin, while massive hemolysis (hemoglobin ~6 g/L) can destroy > 90% of insulin immunoreactivity within one hour at 37 °C [22, mechanism_review]. Hemolysis renders a sample unreportable; no correction algorithm is reliable across assays.

**Prompt centrifugation and separation.** Because IDE activity is ongoing in whole blood, prompt centrifugation and plasma/serum separation are essential. EDTA-anticoagulated plasma tubes provide acceptable stability at room temperature for at least 24 hours when centrifuged and kept separated [23, cohort]. Serum yields comparable results to EDTA plasma when processed promptly. Samples should be stored at −20 °C or lower if analysis is delayed beyond several hours.

**Fasting state.** A minimum 8-hour overnight fast is standard; shorter fasts introduce noise that overwhelms the signal at low fasting concentrations.

**Tube type.** Heparin plasma yields insulin values approximately 15% lower than serum due to heparin's interaction with insulin binding or assay antibodies [24, cohort]. Serum or EDTA plasma are preferred; heparin plasma should be avoided or, if used, results should not be compared to serum-derived reference ranges.

### Variability: Analytical, Biological, and Inter-Assay

**Analytical CV.** Within-assay imprecision is acceptable for most modern automated platforms (typically 3%–10% CV at mid-range concentrations) but rises substantially at concentrations < 5 µIU/mL — precisely where most clinically interesting fasting samples fall.

**Within-subject biological CV.** The European Biological Variation Study (EuBIVAS), the largest and most methodologically rigorous dataset of its kind, estimated within-subject biological CV (CVI) for serum insulin at 25.3% (95% CI 24.0%–26.6%) in healthy adults [25, cohort]. A serial change of ~71% or more is required to confidently attribute a difference to a true biological change rather than physiological noise (at 95% probability, assuming comparable analytical CV ~5%).

**The cutpoint-transferability problem.** The compound effect of (a) inter-assay bias of up to 66% CV, (b) within-subject biological CV ~25%, and (c) non-specific cross-reactivity with des-(64,65) proinsulin means that a fasting insulin cutpoint established on one platform in one population cannot be applied to results from a different platform. Clinical decision thresholds for insulin resistance (e.g., > 10 µIU/mL or > 25 pmol/L) that appear in the literature were derived on specific assays — frequently RIA or early ELISA platforms — and are not automatically valid for contemporary chemiluminescent immunoassays.

## Determinants & Clinical Significance

### Hyperinsulinemia as the Earliest Detectable Signal of Insulin Resistance

Fasting hyperinsulinemia is now understood to precede glucose dysregulation by years, sometimes decades, in the progression toward type 2 diabetes (T2D). During the early phase of insulin resistance (IR), pancreatic β-cells mount a compensatory response — upregulating both insulin secretory capacity and, in animal models, β-cell mass — to maintain euglycemia in the face of peripheral resistance [26, mechanism_review]. This compensatory hyperinsulinemia can sustain normal fasting glucose for an extended window, meaning a clinician reviewing only glucose or HbA1c may see a completely "normal" metabolic panel in a patient whose fasting insulin is already substantially elevated [26, mechanism_review].

The temporal priority of insulin over glucose elevation was demonstrated in a cross-sectional study of 1,313 young Indian adults (ages 16–25): 30.5% displayed hyperinsulinemia despite normal blood glucose and HbA1c (< 5.7%), and only 4.2% met ADA prediabetes criteria by glucose thresholds — yet 19.7% showed HbA1c in the prediabetic range, illustrating that conventional glucose-based screening systematically misses this early metabolic disturbance [26, mechanism_review].

The mechanistic basis of the β-cell–driven trajectory has been characterized in the JCI: β-cells initially mount an adaptive secretory surge (Phase 1 — compensatory hyperinsulinemia), which restores euglycemia in the prediabetic state; chronic exposure to excess glucose and lipids then leads to β-cell dysfunction (Phase 2 — plateau); progressive loss of β-cell mass and secretory capacity follows, producing the frank hyperglycemia of overt T2D (Phase 3 — decline) [27, mechanism_review]. A critically important corollary: a "normal" fasting insulin level is therefore ambiguous. It can represent either genuine insulin sensitivity or late-stage β-cell exhaustion in which the pancreas can no longer maintain compensatory output. Clinical context — particularly glucose trajectory, HbA1c, and c-peptide — is required to differentiate the two.

### Prognostic Associations

**Incident Type 2 Diabetes.** The METSIM (Metabolic Syndrome in Men) Study, a Finnish prospective cohort of 10,197 men (8,749 non-diabetic at baseline) followed for 5.9 years and yielding 558 incident T2D cases, found fasting insulin to be a significant independent predictor of T2D with a hazard ratio of **1.37 (95% CI: 1.32–1.42)** per unit increase [28, cohort]. Liver insulin resistance index was the single strongest predictor (HR 1.83, 95% CI: 1.68–1.98) in the same analysis, underscoring that fasting insulin captures a real but partial signal of the full IR spectrum [28, cohort].

The Hoorn Study (Dutch prospective cohort of 1,349 adults aged 50–75 without diabetes at baseline) confirmed that both fasting insulin and HOMA-IR provide meaningful predictive discrimination across the range of values, not only above clinical thresholds [29, cohort].

**Metabolic Syndrome.** A Korean health examination cohort of 2,350 participants followed for 5 years found that subjects in the highest quartile of fasting insulin levels (≥ 8.98 µIU/mL) had **OR 5.1 (95% CI: 3.1–8.2)** for developing metabolic syndrome compared with those in the lowest quartile (≤ 6.01 µIU/mL), after adjustment for age, sex, smoking, exercise, alcohol, and blood pressure [30, cohort]. In the subgroup free of any metabolic syndrome components at baseline, the effect was larger still: **OR 10.7 (95% CI: 2.4–47.9)** [30, cohort]. This dose-response relationship places elevated fasting insulin at the upstream causal position in the metabolic syndrome constellation.

**Cardiovascular Disease.** A 2012 meta-analysis of 65 prospective studies (N = 516,325 non-diabetic adults) found HOMA-IR to be significantly associated with incident coronary heart disease, with the highest vs. lowest category showing **RR 1.64 (95% CI: 1.35–2.00; I² = 0%)** and a per-SD estimate of **RR 1.46 (95% CI: 1.26–1.69)** [31, meta_analysis]. Fasting insulin alone was less discriminating (CHD RR 1.12, 95% CI: 0.92–1.37 for highest vs. lowest; per-SD 1.04, 95% CI: 0.96–1.12) and did not reach statistical significance when examined independently [31, meta_analysis]. This contrast is mechanistically coherent: HOMA-IR integrates insulin and glucose signals, capturing β-cell compensatory dynamics more completely than insulin alone.

A subsequent meta-analysis (7 studies; 26,976 non-diabetic adults) focused on mortality endpoints found pooled adjusted risk ratios for all-cause mortality of **1.13** for fasting insulin and **1.34** for HOMA-IR (highest vs. lowest category), with cardiovascular mortality risk ratios reaching 1.40 (fasting insulin) and 2.11 (HOMA-IR) [32, meta_analysis].

**MASLD / NAFLD.** Insulin resistance is the dominant pathophysiological driver of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, formerly NAFLD). A large NHANES-derived analysis found risk of metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) to be substantially higher in the highest HOMA-IR quartile vs. the lowest (**OR 5.94, 95% CI: 2.12–16.68**) [33, cohort]. Elevated fasting insulin functions as a leading indicator of hepatic fat accumulation — itself partly driven by insulin's direct promotion of de novo lipogenesis and suppression of hepatic fatty acid oxidation.

### Determinants That Raise Fasting Insulin

**Obesity and visceral adiposity** are the dominant modifiable drivers. Expansion of intra-abdominal fat depots elevates circulating free fatty acids (FFA) through insulin-resistant lipolysis; the eBioMedicine POEM cohort (499 Uppsala adults, age 50) and paired mechanistic case-control data (n = 27) support the hypothesis that elevated FFA — rather than glucose — is the primary metabolic derangement initiating fasting hyperinsulinemia in non-diabetic obese individuals [34, cohort]. The pancreas responds to FFA-mediated lipotoxicity and reduced peripheral glucose uptake by upregulating insulin output [34, cohort].

Additional modifiable determinants:
- **Refined-carbohydrate / high-glycaemic-load diet:** repeated postprandial glucose excursions drive sustained elevations in mean insulin secretion, shifting fasting levels upward over time.
- **Physical inactivity:** skeletal muscle is the primary site of insulin-stimulated glucose disposal; sedentary behavior reduces GLUT4 expression and insulin receptor signaling, increasing secretory demand.
- **Sleep deprivation / restriction:** A randomized trial restricting sleep to ≤ 6.2 h/night over 6 weeks found significantly impaired insulin sensitivity (HOMA-IR β = 0.30 ± 0.12; P = 0.016), independent of adiposity changes; effects were observed in both pre- and postmenopausal women [35, rct].
- **Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS):** IR is present in 35–80% of women with PCOS; hyperinsulinemia is both a consequence of IR and an amplifier of ovarian androgen synthesis (insulin stimulates thecal testosterone secretion and suppresses sex hormone-binding globulin), creating a self-reinforcing pathophysiological loop [36, mechanism_review].
- **Certain drugs:** glucocorticoids, atypical antipsychotics, and thiazide diuretics at high doses promote IR and elevate fasting insulin. Exogenous insulin and insulin secretagogues (sulfonylureas, meglitinides) directly raise measured fasting insulin and render the fasting value uninterpretable as a measure of endogenous secretory status.

### Determinants That Lower Fasting Insulin

- **Weight loss:** a 5–10% reduction in body weight significantly reduces fasting insulin and HOMA-IR, proportional to the degree of visceral fat lost.
- **Aerobic and resistance exercise:** combined exercise training programs reduce fasting insulin and HOMA-IR in sedentary adults without diabetes [37, meta_analysis]. Resistance training independently improves skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity through GLUT4 upregulation, while aerobic training reduces hepatic fat and improves hepatic insulin sensitivity.
- **Carbohydrate restriction:** low-carbohydrate diets reduce postprandial insulin excursions and lower mean fasting insulin, with degree of reduction proportional to extent of carbohydrate restriction.
- **Metformin:** improves hepatic insulin sensitivity (reducing hepatic glucose output), thereby lowering the secretory burden on β-cells and reducing fasting insulin, though effects on fasting insulin per se are modest and secondary to IR improvement.
- **Bariatric surgery:** among the most potent interventions; a meta-analysis of 39 studies (3,855 participants) found fasting insulin reductions with WMD −0.62 (95% CI: −0.88 to −0.36) on standardized scales, with weight-loss-independent effects observed early post-surgery, likely mediated by changes in gut hormones and hepatic insulin clearance [38, meta_analysis].

**Drug confounders:** exogenous insulin therapy and insulin secretagogues raise measured fasting insulin and render the fasting value uninterpretable as a measure of endogenous secretory status. Patients on these agents should be assessed with c-peptide instead.

### Limitations of Fasting Insulin as a Clinical Marker

**Assay non-standardization** is the most serious analytical limitation. Unlike glucose or HbA1c, insulin immunoassays are not harmonized across manufacturers. Within-assay CVs range from 3.7% to 39%, and between-assay CVs from 12% to 66% [39, regulatory]. The ADA has called for a sustainable insulin assay standardization program to enable population-level risk thresholds [39, regulatory].

**Single fasting time-point:** insulin is secreted pulsatilely; a single morning draw introduces within-subject biological variability of ~25% CV. Repeated measures or combined indices (HOMA-IR, FGIR) reduce but do not eliminate this noise.

**The bidirectional "normal" problem:** as discussed above, a fasting insulin in the normal range may represent either genuine insulin sensitivity or late β-cell failure with loss of compensatory capacity. A low or normal fasting insulin in a patient with established T2D does not provide reassurance about the underlying metabolic state — and may signal advanced disease.

**Population specificity:** reference ranges established in predominantly European or North American cohorts may not apply universally; South and East Asian populations tend to develop significant IR and β-cell dysfunction at lower BMI values, making population-specific thresholds important for clinical interpretation.
