## TL;DR

Sermorelin is the minimal fully active fragment of human GHRH — GRF(1-29)NH2 — and the prototype of the GRF(1-29) family of pituitary GH secretagogues. Mechanistically it is clean and well-understood: it binds the somatotroph GHRH receptor (a class-B Gs-coupled GPCR), drives the canonical cAMP → PKA → Ca²⁺ cascade, and evokes **release of stored GH plus sustained GH synthesis** — but because it acts one step upstream at the pituitary (rather than supplying hormone directly, as recombinant GH does), it **augments the body's own physiologic, pulsatile GH secretion under intact feedback.** Its defining pharmacokinetic limitation is a **very short half-life (~11–12 minutes)** driven by DPP-IV degradation, which is exactly what the stabilized analogues (CJC-1295 / Mod GRF 1-29) and the related (1-44)-based tesamorelin were engineered to overcome, and which underlies the practice of stacking sermorelin with a mechanistically distinct GHRP/ghrelin-receptor agonist.

The honest evidence state has a sharp seam. Sermorelin **was a real FDA-approved drug (Geref)**, with genuine **pediatric-GHD growth-velocity efficacy** (the pivotal Thorner 1996 trial raised height velocity from ~4.1 to ~8.0 cm/yr at 6 months, though less than recombinant somatropin achieves) and a **validated diagnostic role** in GHRH-stimulation testing. That approved/evidenced lineage is **pediatric and diagnostic.** The popular **adult** use — anti-aging, recomposition, sleep, recovery, athletic enhancement — is entirely **off-label**, and the adult evidence is a small cluster of **four old, small trials in aging adults with low baseline IGF-1**, showing biomarker (GH/IGF-1) change plus, in some, modest skin-thickness, sex-restricted lean-mass, and cognition signals — **no large modern RCT in healthy adults exists** for any of those goals. The most common mis-credit is folding tesamorelin (Baker 2012) and full-length GHRH-1,44 results into sermorelin's résumé; those are different molecules and are explicitly excluded here. The market reality: Geref is gone, but sermorelin is one of the most widely **§503A-compounded** GH-axis peptides (via its prior-approval pathway), it was **not** swept up in the April 2026 FDA peptide actions, and it is **WADA-prohibited at all times.** Net: a **formerly-approved, mechanistically physiologic GH secretagogue with real pediatric-GHD + diagnostic evidence but only biomarker-level adult evidence** — promising and monitorable, but doctor-required and unproven for the adult goals it is actually marketed for.

## 1. Identity, Structure & Pharmacokinetics

### 1.1 What sermorelin actually is

Sermorelin is the acetate salt of an amidated synthetic 29-amino-acid peptide designated **GRF(1-29)NH2** (equivalently **GHRH(1-29)NH2**). It reproduces exactly the first 29 residues, counting from the N-terminus, of endogenous human growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH), with a C-terminal amide [1, regulatory]. The single defining structural fact is that sermorelin is the **shortest synthetic GHRH fragment that retains the full biological activity of the parent hormone**: Prakash and Goa describe it as "a 29 amino acid analogue of human growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH)... the shortest synthetic peptide with full biological activity of GHRH" [2, mechanism_review]. Because it is the minimal fully active fragment, sermorelin functions as the **prototype of the GRF(1-29) class** of GHRH-receptor agonists; the later, longer-acting members of this class (§1.6) are stabilized derivatives built on the same 29-residue scaffold.

### 1.2 Relationship to full-length GHRH(1-44) — and the molecules it is NOT

Endogenous human GHRH circulates principally as a 44-residue amidated peptide, GHRH(1-44)NH2, secreted by the hypothalamus into the hypophyseal portal system. Structure–activity work established that biological potency resides almost entirely in the N-terminal portion: the first ~29 residues are sufficient for high-affinity receptor engagement and full GH-releasing activity, while residues 30–44 are largely dispensable for intrinsic activity. Sermorelin is the pharmaceutical embodiment of that finding — GHRH(1-44) truncated to its minimal active core and C-terminally amidated [2, mechanism_review]. The N-terminal tyrosine (Tyr1) and adjacent residues are both essential for receptor activation and the site of the body's primary inactivation step (§1.5).

Sermorelin must **not** be conflated with two other named GHRH analogues that are structurally distinct [1, regulatory][2, mechanism_review][3, rct]:

1. **Tesamorelin** is a synthetic analogue of the **full-length 44-residue** GHRH(1-44) carrying a trans-3-hexenoyl (hexenoyl, C6) acyl group on the N-terminal tyrosine; that N-terminal cap confers serum stability versus native GHRH [3, rct]. Tesamorelin is therefore a **(1-44)-based, N-acylated** molecule — *not* a (1-29) fragment.
2. **CJC-1295 (modified GRF(1-29), "Mod GRF 1-29")** is, by contrast, a member of the **same GRF(1-29) family** as sermorelin — a stabilized sermorelin analogue carrying amino-acid substitutions (and, in the DAC variant, an albumin-binding maleimide) on the 29-residue backbone (§1.6).

This distinction is load-bearing for the evidence demarcation in §3 and §5: clinical data on tesamorelin and on recombinant full-length GHRH-1,44 are **not** sermorelin data.

### 1.3 Mechanism of action — receptor and signal transduction

Sermorelin acts on the somatotroph the same way native GHRH does, by engaging the **growth hormone-releasing hormone receptor (GHRH-R)** — a **class B (secretin-family) G-protein-coupled receptor** "physiologically mainly expressed on the growth-stimulating somatotropic cells in the pituitary gland"; the human receptor is a 423-amino-acid protein encoded on chromosome 7p14-15 [4, mechanism_review]. A second independent review concurs that "the GHRHR is a member of the class II B GPCR family, which couples predominantly to the Gs-adenylate cyclase-cAMP signaling pathway" [5, mechanism_review].

The downstream cascade is a canonical Gs/cAMP/PKA pathway. On agonist binding, "a stimulatory G protein... activates adenylyl cyclase to produce cAMP, leading to activation of protein kinase A (PKA)" [5, mechanism_review]. Halmos et al. describe the terminal exocytotic step: "the activation of adenylate cyclase increases cAMP concentration, resulting in an increase in Ca2+ ions, and consequent GH release into the systemic circulation," with PKA activation also "determining cell proliferation, and GH and GHRH-R synthesis"; mechanistically the rise in cAMP leads to "the opening of a sodium channel... a voltage-dependent Ca2+ channel will open, allowing calcium influx, which directly causes the release of GH stored in secretory granules" [4, mechanism_review]. Thus sermorelin drives **both** acute release of pre-formed GH (cAMP → Ca²⁺ → exocytosis) **and** sustained GH gene transcription/synthesis (via PKA) [4, mechanism_review].

The clinical-pharmacology endpoint is straightforward: "Sermorelin acetate for injection increases plasma growth hormone (GH) concentration by stimulating the pituitary gland to release GH" [1, regulatory]. Released GH then drives the second tier of the somatotropic axis — hepatic production of insulin-like growth factor I: GH "stimulates the production of insulin-like growth factor I (IGF-I) in the liver," which mediates many of GH's peripheral anabolic and growth effects [4, mechanism_review].

A mechanistically important corollary distinguishes sermorelin from exogenous recombinant GH. Because sermorelin acts one step upstream — at the pituitary, rather than directly supplying hormone — it **augments the organism's own physiologic, pulsatile GH secretion** rather than imposing a non-physiologic flat exposure; it depends on, and works through, an intact pituitary somatotroph population and the normal feedback architecture of the GH/IGF-1 axis [1, regulatory][2, mechanism_review]. This pituitary-dependence is also why sermorelin historically functioned as a **diagnostic** probe of pituitary GH reserve (an IV 1 µg/kg challenge) in addition to its therapeutic use [2, mechanism_review][1, regulatory].

### 1.4 Pharmacokinetics

Sermorelin is administered **parenterally (subcutaneous or intravenous)**; it is not orally bioavailable as a peptide [1, regulatory]. Its defining PK feature is a **very short circulating half-life.** The Geref labeling states that "the half-life of Sermorelin is short, 11-12 minutes after either intravenous or subcutaneous administration," and that after a 2 mg subcutaneous dose "peak concentrations of sermorelin were reached in 5-20 minutes" [1, regulatory]. This ~11–20-minute window is the figure that should anchor any claim about sermorelin's duration; the broader "~10–20 min" range commonly quoted is consistent with this label value [1, regulatory].

### 1.5 Degradation — DPP-IV

The short half-life is a direct consequence of rapid enzymatic degradation. The principal inactivation route for the GHRH/GRF(1-29) scaffold is cleavage by **dipeptidyl peptidase-IV (DPP-IV)** at the N-terminus: "Dipeptidylpeptidase IV inactivates the active form of GHRH in blood to its more stable inactive metabolite GHRH(3-44)-NH2" [5, mechanism_review]. The analogous Tyr1-Ala2 cleavage applies to the (1-29) fragment, and the resulting truncated metabolites have essentially no residual GH-releasing activity; general serum peptidase activity contributes additional clearance. This degradation biology is precisely what the stabilized GRF(1-29) analogues (§1.6) and tesamorelin (via N-terminal acylation) [3, rct] were engineered to overcome. A frequently noted functional point is that the GH-releasing *effect* outlasts the parent peptide's measurable presence, because a brief pituitary stimulus triggers a self-limited endogenous GH pulse; this preserved pulsatility, together with rapid clearance, is the pharmacodynamic rationale for sermorelin's physiologic profile [1, regulatory][2, mechanism_review]. (Quantitative duration-of-effect figures from non-Tier sources are not asserted here.)

### 1.6 The GRF(1-29) family and the GHRP-synergy rationale

**The GRF(1-29) family.** Sermorelin's minutes-long half-life motivated a family of stabilized analogues on the same 29-residue backbone. The best known is **CJC-1295 without DAC ("Modified GRF 1-29" / Mod GRF 1-29)**, a sermorelin analogue carrying point substitutions (classically at the DPP-IV cleavage site and other peptidase-sensitive positions) that markedly slow enzymatic degradation while preserving GHRH-R agonism. The **CJC-1295 with DAC** variant adds a Drug Affinity Complex (an albumin-binding moiety) that extends the half-life into the multi-day range by tethering the peptide to serum albumin. All remain GHRH-R agonists of the GRF(1-29) lineage and share sermorelin's receptor and signaling mechanism (§1.3); they differ from it essentially in **pharmacokinetic stability, not receptor target.** (Stabilization is the same conceptual strategy as tesamorelin's N-terminal hexenoyl cap, applied to the (1-44) scaffold [3, rct].)

**GHRP-synergy rationale.** In practice GRF(1-29) agonists are commonly co-administered with a **growth hormone-releasing peptide / secretagogue (GHRP-2, GHRP-6, hexarelin, or ipamorelin).** The mechanistic basis is that these GHRPs act on a **different receptor** — the **ghrelin receptor GHS-R1a** — rather than the GHRH-R [7, mechanism_review]. Because the two ligand classes engage distinct receptors on (and around) the somatotroph, their effects on GH output are more than additive: GHRH and ghrelin/GHS show "a synergistic relationship... in the release of GH," and GHRH can act as "an allosteric modulator of the ghrelin receptor GHS-R1a" [7, mechanism_review]. The dual-pathway rationale — a GHRH-R agonist raising the cAMP-driven secretory drive while a GHS-R1a agonist amplifies pulse amplitude — is therefore grounded in two genuinely separate somatotroph receptor systems converging on GH exocytosis [7, mechanism_review][4, mechanism_review].

### 1.7 Coverage gaps (Section 1)
- **No human adult PK study** — the ~11–12-min half-life and 5–20-min Tmax are from the Geref label (single-dose IV/SC), not from a modern adult dose-response PK study; multi-dose accumulation and dose-proportionality at off-label doses are uncharacterized.
- **Duration-of-effect numbers** — the "effect outlasts the peptide" point is qualitative; no Tier-anchored quantitative duration-of-GH-pulse figure is asserted.
- **Family analogue PK** — CJC-1295/Mod GRF 1-29 half-life specifics are described mechanistically; per-analogue PK belongs to those compounds' own entries, not sermorelin's.

## 2. Approval History + Pediatric-GHD / Diagnostic Efficacy

> **Population integrity (carry throughout):** every efficacy figure in this section is in sermorelin's **approved/evidenced populations — pediatric idiopathic GHD growth failure, and diagnostic GH-secretory-capacity testing.** None of it is evidence for adult anti-aging use (that is §3). One enumerated trial (Kirk 1994) was conducted in **idiopathic short stature, NOT GHD,** and is flagged accordingly.

### 2.1 Regulatory history (Geref / Serono)

Two distinct Geref products were FDA-approved, both held by Serono (later EMD Serono) [8, regulatory][9, regulatory]:

1. **Geref Diagnostic, sermorelin acetate 0.05 mg base/ampule — NDA 19-863, initially approved December 28, 1990.** Indicated **for evaluating the ability of the somatotroph of the pituitary gland to secrete growth hormone** — i.e., a single-IV-injection GHRH-stimulation diagnostic agent (1 µg/kg) [8, regulatory][9, regulatory].
2. **Geref, sermorelin acetate 0.5 mg and 1.0 mg base/vial — NDA 20-443, initially approved September 26, 1997.** Indicated **for the treatment of idiopathic growth hormone deficiency in children with growth failure** (pediatric-GHD growth therapy) [8, regulatory][9, regulatory].

So the approved label scope was, accurately: **a diagnostic GHRH-stimulation product (1990)** and a **pediatric idiopathic-GHD growth-treatment product (1997)** — both from Serono. **There was no approved adult anti-aging or adult body-composition indication for Geref** [8, regulatory][9, regulatory].

### 2.2 Withdrawal (~2008) — commercial, NOT safety/efficacy

Both products were discontinued by EMD Serono in 2008: the diagnostic 0.05 mg/amp product was reported discontinued in a letter dated **July 11, 2008**, and the pediatric-treatment 0.5/1.0 mg/vial product in a letter dated **December 2, 2008**, with a request to withdraw NDA 20-443; the FDA subsequently withdrew approval of the applications [8, regulatory]. Crucially, the FDA formally determined — published in the **Federal Register, March 4, 2013** (Doc. 2013-04827) — that **Geref (all three presentations: 0.5 mg/vial, 1.0 mg/vial, and 0.05 mg/amp) was NOT withdrawn from sale for reasons of safety or effectiveness** [8, regulatory]. This is the load-bearing integrity fact: the disappearance of Geref from the US market was a **commercial/business decision,** not a regulatory safety action. "Withdrawn for commercial reasons" ≠ "banned" and ≠ "found unsafe or ineffective" [8, regulatory]. Contributing market context (sponsor-side, from secondary regulatory-summary sources rather than the primary FDA determination): by the late 2000s pediatric GHD was overwhelmingly treated with daily recombinant somatropin — which, as the trials below show, produced larger height-velocity gains — eroding the commercial case for a GHRH product [9, regulatory].

### 2.3 Diagnostic efficacy — GHRH-stimulation testing

The diagnostic rationale is that a single IV dose of sermorelin (1 µg/kg) provokes a GH surge from a structurally intact pituitary; a subnormal response flags impaired GH secretory capacity.

- **Ranke et al., 1986** tested GRF(1-29)NH2 at **1 µg/kg IV** in **131 children and adolescents (45 with idiopathic GHD)** plus young-adult volunteers, measuring maximal GH over 120 min alongside basal somatomedin-C (IGF-1). A **maximal GH >10 ng/mL was defined as normal,** and the authors concluded that GRF testing combined with basal somatomedin-C measurement offers a safe, convenient way to diagnose GHD in clinically suspected cases [10, cohort]. Population: children/adolescents with suspected/idiopathic GHD.
- The review literature concurs that **IV sermorelin 1 µg/kg is a rapid and relatively specific provocative test** for GHD in children, producing **fewer false-positive (falsely subnormal) GH responses than other provocative tests** [2, mechanism_review].

**Important diagnostic limitation (annotated):** a *normal* GH response to IV sermorelin **cannot exclude GHD of hypothalamic origin,** because sermorelin acts at the pituitary downstream of any hypothalamic defect; a subnormal response to a separate provocative test is still needed to confirm such cases [2, mechanism_review]. This is why the diagnostic product assessed *pituitary somatotroph capacity* specifically, rather than serving as a standalone GHD rule-out [1, regulatory].

### 2.4 Treatment efficacy — pediatric idiopathic GHD

**Pivotal trial — Thorner et al. (Geref International Study Group), 1996** [11, open_label].
- *Population:* **110 previously untreated prepubertal children with GH deficiency** (86 evaluable for efficacy). **(Pediatric GHD.)**
- *Intervention:* GHRH-(1-29) **30 µg/kg SC once daily at bedtime, up to 12 months,** multicenter open-label.
- *Outcome:* mean height velocity rose from **4.1 ± 0.9 cm/yr at baseline to 8.0 ± 1.5 cm/yr at 6 months and 7.2 ± 1.3 cm/yr at 12 months;** **74% of children had a "good response" at 6 months.** Bone-age/height-age ratio at 12 months (1.04 ± 0.58) was not significantly different from unity — i.e., no disproportionate bone-age advancement.
- *Safety/tolerability:* well tolerated; no adverse biochemical/hormonal changes, no change in fasting glucose, no excessive IGF-1 generation [11, open_label].

**Supporting evidence — Prakash & Goa review, 1999** (BioDrugs), the principal published review of Geref's clinical dossier [2, mechanism_review].
- For **treatment** it states that **once-daily SC sermorelin 30 µg/kg at bedtime is effective in some prepubertal children with idiopathic GHD,** with **significant height-velocity increases sustained over 12 months** and data in a few children suggesting maintenance through **36 months. (Pediatric idiopathic GHD.)**
- It explicitly notes these height-velocity increases were **less than those achieved with once-daily SC somatropin 30 µg/kg** — i.e., GHRH therapy was efficacious but **inferior to direct recombinant GH replacement,** consistent with sermorelin's mechanism (it requires a responsive pituitary).
- The review notes the **effect on final adult height was undetermined** at publication — long-term/adult-height outcome data were incomplete [2, mechanism_review].

**Non-GHD comparator trial — Kirk et al., 1994 (FLAGGED: NOT GHD)** [12, open_label].
- *Population:* **18 prepubertal children with IDIOPATHIC SHORT STATURE — NOT GH-deficient** (height <3rd centile but peak GH >20 mU/L on provocation; 17 evaluable). Included for completeness/contrast and **must not be read as pediatric-GHD efficacy.**
- *Intervention:* GHRH(1-29)NH2 **20 µg/kg SC twice daily for 12 months.**
- *Outcome:* mean height velocity rose from **4.8 ± 0.9 cm/yr pre-treatment to 7.2 ± 1.6 cm/yr at 12 months (P = 0.001);** predicted final height improved by a mean of 3.4 ± 2.6 cm; slow-growing children responded more. Glucose, insulin, and IGF-1 rose during therapy, and after stopping, growth velocity returned toward pre-treatment values within 12 months [12, open_label].

### 2.5 Population-integrity summary

Geref's evidenced efficacy is in **(a) prepubertal/pediatric idiopathic GHD growth failure** (Thorner 1996; reviewed by Prakash & Goa 1999) and **(b) diagnostic pituitary GH-secretory-capacity testing** (Ranke 1986; reviewed Prakash & Goa 1999). The single idiopathic-short-stature trial (Kirk 1994) is **not** GHD evidence and is labeled as such. **No source in this section supports adult anti-aging, body-composition, or longevity efficacy** — none of the approved indications or cited trials addressed adults for therapeutic benefit (the only adult use was diagnostic GH-axis testing). Adult claims belong to §3 [8, regulatory][2, mechanism_review][10, cohort][11, open_label].

### 2.6 Coverage gaps (Section 2)
- **No final-adult-height outcome** — the pivotal pediatric evidence is first-year height velocity; the effect on *final adult height* was undetermined at the time of the Prakash & Goa review.
- **Pediatric efficacy is inferior to recombinant GH** — the comparator data establish sermorelin worked but less than somatropin; head-to-head magnitude detail beyond the review summary is paywalled.
- **One enumerated trial is non-GHD** — Kirk 1994 (idiopathic short stature) is carried only as contrast and is not GHD evidence.

## 3. Adult / Off-Label Evidence (INVESTIGATIONAL)

> **Demarcation up front.** The popular *adult* uses of sermorelin — anti-aging, body recomposition, sleep, recovery, "GH optimization" / athletic enhancement — are **OFF-LABEL.** The only approval sermorelin (Geref) ever held in the US was pediatric (diagnostic GH-reserve test + treatment of childhood idiopathic GHD; §2). The US-approved product was discontinued in 2008 and the approval withdrawn (not for safety/efficacy; §2.2); adult sermorelin today is supplied only through compounding. **Every adult finding below is INVESTIGATIONAL,** and most derives from a small cluster of old (1992–2006), small (n = 9–89) trials in **aging adults** (typically 55–76 y with low baseline IGF-1) — *not* healthy young athletes. Throughout, "raises GH / IGF-1" is a **biomarker,** not a demonstrated clinical benefit [13, regulatory].

### 3.1 Aging-adult GHRH(1-29) / sermorelin trials

**Corpas et al. 1992 — biomarker reversal, no body-composition endpoint.** Healthy old men (n = 10, mean 68 y) vs young men (n = 9), GHRH(1-29) 0.5 mg and 1 mg SC twice daily for 14 days. Dose-related increases in 24-h GH and IGF-1; at the high dose the old-vs-young GH/IGF-1 gap was abolished. The authors framed this as biomarker normalization and *hypothesized* (did not test) downstream body-composition benefit [14, rct]. This is a 2-week pharmacodynamic study, not an efficacy trial.

**Vittone et al. 1997 — single nightly dose; biomarkers up, body composition flat.** Eleven healthy non-obese men, 64–76 y, low baseline IGF-1; GHRH(1-29) 2 mg nightly SC for 6 weeks. Nocturnal GH release and peak amplitude rose (P < .02), but **IGF-1, IGFBP-3, and GHBP did not change,** and there was **no change in weight, BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, or DEXA muscle/fat.** Two of six strength measures and one endurance measure improved. The authors explicitly concluded single nightly dosing is **less effective than multiple daily doses** [15, rct]. *Caution: vendor blogs frequently attribute "improved body composition / walking speed" to "Vittone 1997"; the actual paper found no DEXA body-composition change — those functional claims come from a different, non-sermorelin study (see §3.2).*

**Khorram, Laughlin & Yen 1997 (JCEM) — the most-cited adult body-composition signal.** Single-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled; 19 age-advanced adults (10 women, 9 men, 55–71 y); **[Nle27]GHRH(1-29)-NH2** 10 µg/kg nightly SC for 16 weeks (4-week placebo lead-in). IGF-1 rose within 2 weeks (P < .05); nocturnal GH was sustained in both sexes; skin thickness increased in both sexes (P < .05). But the anabolic endpoints were **sex-restricted:** lean body mass, insulin sensitivity, and libido improved in **men only** (P < .05–.01), not women [16, rct]. Note this used the **[Nle27] norleucine-substituted** analog — a stabilized GHRH(1-29) variant closely related to but not identical to clinical sermorelin acetate; n = 19 is small and the design single-blind.

**Vitiello, Moe, Merriam et al. 2006 (Neurobiol Aging) — aging-adult cognition.** Randomized placebo-controlled; 89 healthy older adults (mean 68 y); 6 months of daily GHRH(1-29)/sermorelin acetate (the UW Geref program). Significant gains in fluid-intelligence domains — WAIS-R performance IQ (P < .01), picture arrangement (P < .01), psychomotor speed ("finding A's", P < .01), working memory; no change in crystallized intelligence [17, rct]. This is the **legitimate sermorelin cognition RCT** — and it is in *aging* adults, not a general anti-aging claim.

**Sleep.** Older-adult GHRH(1-29) work reports modest increases in slow-wave / NREM sleep and fewer nocturnal awakenings per review aggregation [18, mechanism_review] — small polysomnography-marker studies, not clinical insomnia trials.

### 3.2 Critical mis-credit flags (do NOT attribute to sermorelin)

- **Baker et al. 2012 (Arch Neurol)** — the well-known 152-adult cognition + body-composition RCT (executive function ↑; body fat −7.4%, lean mass +3.7%) used **tesamorelin (stabilized GHRH analog, Egrifta),** *not* sermorelin [19, rct]. **Do not credit to sermorelin.**
- **Friedman et al. 2013** brain-GABA cognition study — also **tesamorelin.** (Carried as a mis-credit guard; covered under the same tesamorelin demarcation.)
- The widely circulated "shortened time to walk 30 m / climb four flights of stairs + reduced abdominal visceral fat" results (Veldhuis/Taaffe-type postmenopausal & older-men work) were obtained with **recombinant human GHRH-1,44-amide,** the full-length 44-residue peptide — **not GHRH(1-29)/sermorelin** [18, mechanism_review]. The vendor-blog conflation of these into sermorelin's résumé is unsupported.

### 3.3 What is ABSENT

There are **no large, modern, double-blind RCTs of sermorelin in healthy adults** for anti-aging, muscle hypertrophy, fat loss, or athletic performance. The adult evidence is entirely (a) old (1992–2006), (b) small (n = 9–89), (c) in **aging adults with low baseline IGF-1,** and (d) often single-blind or pharmacodynamic. Specifically, these adult domains have **ZERO robust sermorelin data:**
- **Healthy-adult athletic/strength performance** — the only strength signal is 2/6 measures in 11 elderly men, with no DEXA change.
- **Healthy-adult (non-aging) anti-aging / longevity** — no trial enrolled healthy young/middle-aged adults.
- **Fat loss in healthy adults** — the fat-loss data are tesamorelin or GHRH-1,44, not sermorelin.

Demonstrated effects are confined to **biomarker change (GH/IGF-1)** plus, in aging adults only, modest skin-thickness, sex-restricted lean-mass, and cognition signals.

### 3.4 Coverage gaps (Section 3)
- **No healthy-adult RCT** — every adult primary is in aging adults with low baseline IGF-1; the marketed healthy-adult use is uns­tudied.
- **Biomarker ≠ outcome** — the consistent finding is GH/IGF-1 elevation; clinical body-composition/performance outcomes are absent or null in the sermorelin-specific set.
- **Analog/full-length conflation risk** — Khorram used a [Nle27] variant; the strongest "adult GHRH" outcomes in circulation are tesamorelin/GHRH-1,44, explicitly excluded here.

- **UC San Diego (Yen lab):** 1 of 4 (Khorram 1997).

### 4.5 Coverage gaps (Section 4)
- **Independence ≠ robustness** — three groups, but only four small old trials total.

## 5. Safety & Adverse Effects

**Defining-fact framing.** Sermorelin is a GHRH analog that acts on the pituitary somatotroph to augment *endogenous, pulsatile* GH secretion rather than supplying exogenous GH [2, mechanism_review]. This mechanism is the basis of its generally favorable short-term safety profile: GH output remains subject to negative feedback from somatostatin and IGF-1, and the pharmacodynamic effects reverse on discontinuation [2, mechanism_review]. The principal honest caveat throughout this section is that the *grounded* adverse-event (AE) data derive almost entirely from **pediatric idiopathic-GHD trials and from diagnostic IV use** — the only populations and doses formally studied and labeled. Contemporary adult anti-aging / body-composition / wellness use is **off-label, at non-label doses in non-label populations,** and **no comparable long-term controlled adult sermorelin safety dataset exists** [dose/population-extrapolation].

### 5.1 Labeled and trial-derived adverse effects (pediatric GHD + diagnostic)

The most consistently reported AE in the Geref program is a **local injection-site reaction** (pain, swelling, or redness), occurring in **about 1 patient in 6** among children treated with daily SC sermorelin. Across the program, **350 patients** were exposed; **3 of 350 discontinued** therapy specifically because of injection-site reactions [20, regulatory]. This local-reaction predominance is corroborated by Prakash & Goa, who concluded that "transient facial flushing and pain at injection site were the most commonly reported adverse events" and that single IV doses and repeated once-daily SC doses are well tolerated [2, mechanism_review].

Other treatment-related systemic AEs in the chronic pediatric setting each occurred at **individual rates of less than 1%** and included **headache, flushing, dysphagia, dizziness, hyperactivity, somnolence, and urticaria;** the **hyperactivity and somnolence** signals are noted specifically in the pediatric population [20, regulatory].

When sermorelin is given **intravenously for diagnostic GH-stimulation testing** (a single high-dose bolus rather than chronic low-dose SC therapy), the labeled AE set is distinct and reflects the acute vasoactive response: **flushing of the face, injection-site pain/redness/swelling, nausea, headache, vomiting, dysgeusia (transient altered taste), pallor, and tightness in the chest** — characteristically transient [20, regulatory].

**Hypothyroidism.** The Geref labeling records an incidence of hypothyroidism during therapy of **6.5%** [20, regulatory]. This is consistent with the known phenomenon that augmenting the GH axis can unmask or precipitate central/relative hypothyroidism (GH and IGF-1 increase peripheral T4→T3 conversion and can lower free T4), and is the rationale for thyroid monitoring; untreated hypothyroidism also blunts the GH response [20, regulatory].

**Anti-GRF antibody formation.** A large proportion of treated patients develop **anti-GRF (anti-sermorelin) antibodies at least once during treatment** [20, regulatory]. The clinical significance is unclear: a positive test frequently reverts to negative by the next assessment, antibody presence **did not appear to affect growth,** and no specific adverse-reaction pattern was attributable to it; no generalized allergic/anaphylactic reactions were reported in the program [20, regulatory].

### 5.2 Hypersensitivity

Beyond the local urticaria signal, hypersensitivity to sermorelin itself or to excipients is the formal allergy concern and the basis of the contraindication (§5.4) [20, regulatory]. For class perspective, the labeled GHRH analog tesamorelin reports hypersensitivity reactions in **4%** of treated patients in controlled trials — a useful order-of-magnitude class reference, though tesamorelin is a different molecule studied in HIV-associated lipodystrophy [dose/population-extrapolation] [21, regulatory].

### 5.3 GH-axis class considerations (theoretical / dose-related — flagged class-extrapolation)

The following are **class-level, dose-dependent** risks inherent to raising GH and IGF-1. They are **not** prominent in the pediatric sermorelin label (where physiologic pulsatile augmentation keeps IGF-1 in range) but become relevant at higher exposures or in adults, and are flagged as theoretical/extrapolated for sermorelin specifically.

- **Fluid retention** (peripheral edema, arthralgia, carpal tunnel syndrome, paresthesias) — the classic dose-related GH-axis AEs. The tesamorelin label carries an explicit fluid-retention warning ("edema, arthralgia, and carpal tunnel syndrome") [21, regulatory]. In a large long-term registry of adult GHD patients on GH replacement, arthralgia occurred in **4.6%,** peripheral edema in **3.8%,** and carpal tunnel syndrome in **1.2%** — low absolute rates responsive to dose reduction [22, cohort]. These are exposure-driven and expected only at supraphysiologic sermorelin dosing [dose/population-extrapolation].
- **Glucose intolerance / reduced insulin sensitivity.** GH is a counter-regulatory, insulin-antagonist hormone; the tesamorelin label carries a glucose-intolerance warning [21, regulatory]. The magnitude appears modest when IGF-1 stays in range: a randomized, placebo-controlled trial of tesamorelin **in type 2 diabetics** found **no significant between-group difference in fasting glucose or HbA1c,** only a transient fasting-glucose rise at weeks 4–8 in the 2 mg arm that resolved by week 12, and **no discontinuations for loss of diabetes control;** IGF-1 rose dose-dependently but **remained within normal age-adjusted ranges** [23, rct]. Long-term adult GHD registry data likewise showed a clinically non-meaningful median glucose change (+0.28 mmol/L) [22, cohort].
- **IGF-1 elevation and neoplasm precaution.** Because the whole point of a secretagogue is to raise GH and hence IGF-1 (a growth factor), persistent supraphysiologic IGF-1 is the central theoretical concern; the tesamorelin label instructs monitoring IGF-1 and considering discontinuation for persistent elevations [21, regulatory]. Reassuringly, large long-term adult GH-replacement data show an all-cancer standardized incidence ratio of **0.92** (no excess), with no increased prostate, colon, or breast cancer when IGF-1 is kept in the physiologic range [22, cohort]. The practical safety lever for sermorelin is therefore **keeping IGF-1 within the age/sex reference range rather than above it.**

### 5.4 Contraindications

- **Known hypersensitivity** to sermorelin or any excipient — the formal sermorelin contraindication [20, regulatory].
- **Concomitant drugs that affect GH release** — a diagnostic-use caution that also bears on therapy: glucocorticoids can inhibit the GH response to sermorelin, and other agents (somatostatin/analogs, dopamine agents, antithyroid drugs, cyclooxygenase inhibitors) can confound testing and blunt efficacy [20, regulatory].
- **Pregnancy and lactation** — sermorelin is Pregnancy Category C with no adequate human data, and it is unknown whether it is excreted in human milk; use only if clearly needed [20, regulatory]. For the class, the tesamorelin label is outright **contraindicated in pregnancy** [21, regulatory].
- **Active malignancy** — not a formal sermorelin-label contraindication, but a strong class precaution given GH/IGF-1 trophic effects: the tesamorelin label **contraindicates active malignancy** (requiring any preexisting malignancy to be inactive and its treatment complete before initiating) and also contraindicates use where the hypothalamic-pituitary axis is disrupted (hypophysectomy, hypopituitarism, pituitary tumor/surgery, head irradiation/trauma) [21, regulatory]. Applying these to off-label adult sermorelin use is prudent extrapolation [dose/population-extrapolation].
- **Pediatric vs adult.** Sermorelin's only labeled therapeutic indication was pediatric idiopathic GHD (plus adult diagnostic use); there is no labeled adult *therapeutic* indication, so all adult treatment is off-label [20, regulatory].

### 5.5 Monitoring and stopping criteria

- **IGF-1 (and IGFBP-3)** — measure at baseline and periodically; the operative target is to keep IGF-1 **within the age/sex reference range,** not above it. Consider dose reduction or discontinuation for persistent IGF-1 elevation [21, regulatory]. (`[[biomarkers/igf-1]]` where a wiki entry exists.)
- **Fasting glucose / HbA1c** — assess glucose status before initiation and monitor periodically, especially in pre-diabetes, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome [21, regulatory].
- **Thyroid function** — given the 6.5% hypothyroidism incidence and that untreated hypothyroidism blunts the GH response, check thyroid status before and during therapy [20, regulatory].
- **Local tolerability** — track injection-site reactions (the dominant AE); rotate sites; discontinuation for this reason was rare (3/350) [20, regulatory].
- **Stopping criteria** — discontinue for hypersensitivity, persistent supraphysiologic IGF-1, emergent active malignancy, or intolerable fluid-retention/glucose effects; benefits reverse on cessation given the physiologic mechanism [2, mechanism_review].

### 5.6 Honest overall framing

The labeled and trial-grounded **short-term** safety of sermorelin is **favorable:** as a secretagogue augmenting endogenous pulsatile GH under intact feedback, its dominant AE is a minor injection-site reaction, systemic AEs are <1%, no anaphylaxis was reported, and antibody formation appears clinically inconsequential [20, regulatory][2, mechanism_review]. The serious limitation is the **absence of long-term controlled adult safety data:** every quantified figure above comes from pediatric GHD therapy, diagnostic IV use, or the related GHRH analog tesamorelin / GH-replacement literature — **not** from chronic adult sermorelin use at the doses now marketed off-label [dose/population-extrapolation]. The reassuring class-level malignancy and metabolic data hold specifically *when IGF-1 is kept in the physiologic range;* pushing IGF-1 supraphysiologic re-introduces the acromegaly-spectrum risks (edema, arthralgia, carpal tunnel, glucose intolerance) that the labeled physiologic-dose data do not capture [21, regulatory][22, cohort].

### 5.7 Coverage gaps (Section 5)
- **No long-term adult AE dataset** — the dominant risk-floor input; all quantified AEs are pediatric/diagnostic or class-extrapolated.
- **Class AEs are tesamorelin/GH-replacement-derived** — fluid retention, glucose, IGF-1/neoplasm figures are class-extrapolation, explicitly flagged, not sermorelin-labeled.
- **Anti-GRF antibody long-term significance** — characterized as clinically inconsequential in the pediatric program, but long-term adult immunogenicity is unstudied.

## 6. Regulatory & Sport Status

### 6.1 FDA history — approved, then commercially withdrawn (not for safety/efficacy)

Sermorelin acetate reached the US market as **GEREF,** held by Serono (later EMD Serono), in two FDA-approved presentations with two distinct uses [8, regulatory][9, regulatory]:
1. **GEREF (treatment)** — NDA 20-443, 0.5 mg and 1.0 mg base/vial, **approved September 26, 1997,** for **treatment of idiopathic GHD in children with growth failure.**
2. **Geref Diagnostic** — 0.05 mg base/amp (NDA 19-863, approved December 28, 1990), for **evaluating pituitary GH secretory capacity.**

Both were voluntarily discontinued by the manufacturer (discontinuation letters July 11, 2008 and December 2, 2008; the treatment NDA's withdrawal was requested December 2008) [8, regulatory]. **The load-bearing point — the withdrawal was a commercial/business decision, NOT a safety or efficacy action — is anchored in a primary FDA document.** On **March 4, 2013, FDA published a determination in the Federal Register** (Doc. 2013-04827) titled, verbatim, *"Determination That GEREF (Sermorelin Acetate) Injection... Were Not Withdrawn From Sale for Reasons of Safety or Effectiveness"* [8, regulatory]. This is the formal mechanism by which FDA records that a discontinued drug was pulled for business reasons; its express purpose is to **permit FDA to approve abbreviated new drug applications (ANDAs)** — generics — for the product, which the agency may only do once it has affirmatively found the original product was not removed for safety or effectiveness [8, regulatory]. In short: **"withdrawn" means the sponsor stopped selling it; it does NOT mean "banned" or "deemed unsafe."** (Dating note: the *discontinuation/withdrawal-request* event sits in **2008**; the *Federal Register determination* documenting the non-safety basis was published in **2013** — both dates are correct for their respective events and should not be conflated.)

### 6.2 Current status — no marketed product, but §503A-compoundable via prior-approval

There is **no currently marketed FDA-approved sermorelin product** in the US — the branded Geref lines were discontinued and no generic ANDA has been brought to market despite the 2013 determination opening that pathway [8, regulatory][9, regulatory]. Despite the absence of a finished approved drug, **sermorelin is one of the most widely available compounded GH-axis peptides,** dispensed by §503A compounding pharmacies under prescription [24, regulatory].

Its compounding standing is materially different from the never-approved "research" peptides, on a point of statutory law. Under **section 503A(b)(1)(A) of the FD&C Act (21 U.S.C. 353a),** a pharmacy may compound from a bulk drug substance that satisfies a **tiered (cascade) test**, applied in order: (i) it **complies with an applicable USP/NF monograph;** (ii) **if no such monograph exists,** it is a **component of a drug approved by FDA;** or (iii) **if neither applies,** it appears on **FDA's §503A bulk-drug-substances ("bulks") list** [25, regulatory]. The tiers are sequential, not free-standing alternatives — but the practical effect for sermorelin is the same: absent a USP/NF sermorelin monograph, the **component-of-an-approved-drug tier controls** (sermorelin was the active ingredient of FDA-approved Geref), so **sermorelin need not appear on the bulks list at all** to be §503A-eligible.

**Sermorelin qualifies through the component-of-an-approved-drug pathway** — it was the active ingredient of FDA-approved Geref (NDA 020443), a fact anchored to the 2013 Federal Register determination and the NDA record [8, regulatory][9, regulatory]. This prior-approval pedigree is the verifiable, primary-anchored basis for its compounding eligibility and is what distinguishes sermorelin from the never-approved peptides whose only possible route is the bulks list.

**Honest hedge on the bulks-list category.** FDA's interim policy sorts *nominated* bulk substances into **Category 1** (nominated with sufficient supporting information, not flagged for safety, no enforcement action pending a final decision) and **Category 2** (substances FDA would consider taking enforcement action against) [24, regulatory]. Some secondary legal analyses describe sermorelin as a "Category 1" interim-bulks-list substance [26, regulatory]. **That specific Category-1 placement could not be independently confirmed from a primary FDA bulks-list document as of mid-2026,** and it is in any event **not** the load-bearing basis for sermorelin's compounding standing — the component-of-a-formerly-approved-drug pathway above is sufficient on its own and is primary-anchored. Stated accurately: **sermorelin is compoundable under §503A via a qualifying statutory pathway (component of the formerly FDA-approved Geref, and/or — per secondary analyses — interim Category-1 treatment); the precise interim-category designation as of mid-2026 is not confirmable from a primary FDA source and should not be asserted as fact** [25, regulatory][8, regulatory][26, regulatory].

### 6.3 Sermorelin was NOT in the April 2026 peptide actions

**Crucially, sermorelin is NOT among the peptides swept up in the April 2026 FDA peptide actions.** Two related events occurred in mid-April 2026. First, FDA acted, through its §503A bulks-list process, to remove a set of peptide bulk drug substances from **Category 2** and refer them for Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) review; the affected substances are the never-FDA-approved "research" peptides described in the secondary legal coverage [27, regulatory][28, regulatory]. Second, FDA published the **PCAC notice of meeting — Federal Register Doc. 2026-07361,** "Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee; Notice of Meeting...," published April 16, 2026 [29, regulatory]. **FR Doc. 2026-07361 is NOT itself the "removal of 12" enumeration — it is a meeting notice** that lists the **seven** substances set for the upcoming PCAC meeting [29, regulatory]:

1. BPC-157 (free base and acetate)
2. KPV (free base and acetate)
3. TB-500 / thymosin beta-4 fragment (free base and acetate)
4. MOTS-c (free base and acetate)
5. Emideltide / DSIP (free base and acetate)
6. Semax (free base and acetate)
7. Epitalon (free base and acetate)

Per the secondary legal coverage, those seven were set for the **July 23–24, 2026 PCAC meeting,** while the broader Category-2 action affected a total of **twelve** peptides — adding GHK-Cu, Melanotan-II, Dihexa (acetate), PEG-MGF, and Cathelicidin/LL-37 — with the remaining five slated for review before the end of February 2027 [27, regulatory][28, regulatory]. (The "twelve" figure traces to FDA's Category-2 action as reported in the secondary analyses; the primary FR meeting notice [29] enumerates only the seven on the agenda.)

**Sermorelin appears in NEITHER the seven-substance PCAC meeting agenda (primary-confirmed: it is not named anywhere in FR Doc. 2026-07361) NOR the twelve-peptide Category-2 removal set (per the secondary coverage)** [29, regulatory][27, regulatory]. Every one of those affected peptides is a **never-FDA-approved** substance whose only §503A foothold was a bulks-list nomination; sermorelin's situation is categorically different because it qualifies under the component-of-an-approved-drug pathway. **The April 2026 peptide actions therefore did not restrict sermorelin compounding.** (A January 7, 2025 FDA policy change ended categorization of *newly nominated* bulk substances into the interim Category 1/2 framework [24, regulatory]; that governs interim-list mechanics and does not disturb sermorelin's independent eligibility. Any eventual *final* §503A bulks-list determination, where relevant, is not yet decided.)

### 6.4 WADA — prohibited at all times (S2.2.4)

Sermorelin is **prohibited in sport at all times — both in- and out-of-competition** — under **Section S2 (Peptide Hormones, Growth Factors, Related Substances and Mimetics)** of the WADA Prohibited List [30, regulatory]. Within S2, the relevant subsection is **growth-hormone-releasing factors (S2.2 / S2.2.4),** which expressly covers GHRH and its analogues. **Sermorelin is named by name,** alongside the other GHRH analogues CJC-1293, CJC-1295, and tesamorelin (and, in the broader subsection, GH secretagogues such as MK-677/ibutamoren and ipamorelin, and the GHRPs). As an S2 substance it is a **non-threshold prohibited substance banned at all times;** there is no in-competition-only carve-out [30, regulatory].

### 6.5 EU status (brief) and gray-market reality

Sermorelin has **no current centrally authorized (EMA) marketed product** in the EU; as in the US, the historical branded products are off the market and there is no widely marketed approved sermorelin drug. It is not a controlled/scheduled substance, but absent an authorized product it has no routine licensed clinical channel and is encountered chiefly through compounding/prescriber routes where national rules permit [regulatory]. **Gray-market reality:** practical supply runs through two channels — (1) **legitimate §503A physician-prescribed compounding** in the US (the dominant lawful route), and (2) an extensive **"research chemical" / not-for-human-use online market** selling sermorelin vials labeled "not for human consumption" to bypass prescription requirements [regulatory][anecdote_aggregate]. The latter carries the usual gray-market hazards (no identity/purity/sterility assurance) and is legally distinct from compounded sermorelin dispensed against a valid prescription. The repeated lay claim that sermorelin is "FDA-approved" is inaccurate — there is no approved product on the market — but the equally common claim that it was "banned" or "pulled for safety" is also inaccurate, as the 2013 Federal Register determination expressly establishes [8, regulatory].

### 6.6 Coverage gaps (Section 6)
- **Interim bulks-list category not primary-confirmable** — sermorelin's compounding standing rests on the component-of-an-approved-drug pathway; the secondary "Category 1" claim is not load-bearing and is not primary-anchored.
- **No generic ANDA marketed** despite the 2013 determination opening that pathway.
- **EU/EMA status** is "no approval found," not affirmatively characterized.
- **Final §503A determination** for the broader peptide class is not yet decided; sermorelin's independent eligibility is unaffected as of mid-2026.

## 7. Why this lands at evidence_tier B / risk_tier medium

### 7.1 evidence_tier B

Sermorelin lands at **B** — above the unapproved C-tier peptides but below S/A — for converging reasons:

1. **It was a genuinely FDA-approved drug.** Geref held two NDAs (diagnostic 1990; pediatric-GHD treatment 1997). A real approval history, with a primary-anchored FDA record, is the single strongest reason it clears C (§2, §6).
2. **It has real efficacy evidence — in pediatric GHD + diagnostic use.** The pivotal Thorner 1996 trial showed genuine height-velocity acceleration in GH-deficient children, and the diagnostic use is validated (Ranke 1986; Prakash & Goa 1999). This is controlled human efficacy data — which the C-tier peptides lack (§2).
3. **But the approval is WITHDRAWN, the efficacy population is pediatric, and the popular adult use is biomarker-only.** It cannot reach S/A because (i) Geref is off-market; (ii) the evidenced population is pediatric GHD, not the adults who actually use it; and (iii) the adult anti-aging case rests on only four small old aging-adult trials showing GH/IGF-1 biomarker change (plus modest, sometimes sex-restricted, secondary signals) — not robust clinical outcomes, and with zero healthy-adult RCTs (§3).
4. **The single-lineage audit does not flag** for the correctly-scoped adult set (50%, three groups) — so the B grade is not undermined by concentration, but neither is the adult base robust (four small trials) (§4).

**B = formerly-approved with real pediatric-GHD + diagnostic evidence, but withdrawn and only biomarker-level for the adult goals it is marketed for.**

### 7.2 risk_tier medium

1. **Favorable short-term safety, mechanistically.** As a secretagogue augmenting endogenous pulsatile GH under intact feedback, the dominant AE is a minor injection-site reaction; systemic AEs <1%; no anaphylaxis; antibody formation clinically inconsequential in the pediatric program (§5.1, §5.6).
2. **Formerly approved + monitorable.** It has a real label, defined contraindications, and a clear monitoring lever (keep IGF-1 in the age/sex range; track glucose and thyroid) (§5.4–§5.5).
3. **But GH-axis/IGF-1 class considerations + no long-term adult safety data keep it above "low."** Pushing IGF-1 supraphysiologic re-introduces acromegaly-spectrum risks (edema, arthralgia, carpal tunnel, glucose intolerance), and there is no long-term controlled adult sermorelin safety dataset at the off-label doses now used (§5.3, §5.6). Hence **doctor_discussion_required: true.**

**medium = favorable, monitorable short-term profile, held above low by GH-axis class risk and the long-term-adult-data vacuum.**

### 8.1 Prescribing-practice layer (brief)

- **Pediatric label dose (kept distinct):** the approved Geref pediatric-GHD dose was **30 µg/kg/day SC at bedtime** (§2) — this is the labeled therapeutic dose and is **not** an adult convention.

Reconstituted compounded sermorelin is conventionally refrigerated (2–8 °C) per each pharmacy's beyond-use date. ## 9. Bottom Line — Supported vs Marketed

**What is genuinely supported:**
- Sermorelin is **GRF(1-29)NH2,** the minimal fully active GHRH fragment and prototype of the GRF(1-29) class, with a clean, well-understood GHRH-R → cAMP/PKA mechanism that augments **physiologic, pulsatile** endogenous GH (and downstream IGF-1).
- It **was a real FDA-approved drug (Geref),** withdrawn ~2008 for **commercial — not safety/efficacy — reasons** (FDA's 2013 Federal Register determination).
- It has **genuine pediatric-GHD growth-velocity efficacy** (Thorner 1996) and a **validated diagnostic use** (Ranke 1986; Prakash & Goa 1999).
- Short-term safety is **favorable and monitorable;** it is **§503A-compoundable** via its prior-approval pathway and was **not** restricted by the April 2026 peptide actions.

**What is marketed but NOT supported:**
- **Adult anti-aging / body-composition / fat-loss / athletic efficacy** — the adult evidence is four small old aging-adult trials showing **biomarker (GH/IGF-1) change** plus modest, sometimes sex-restricted, secondary signals; **no healthy-adult RCT exists.**
- **The impressive body-composition and cognition numbers** (e.g., fat −7.4% / lean +3.7%, walking/stair-climb/visceral-fat gains) — those belong to **tesamorelin (Baker 2012) and recombinant GHRH-1,44,** different molecules, explicitly **not** credited to sermorelin.
- **"FDA-approved" framing** — there is no marketed approved product; equally, "banned / pulled for safety" is wrong.
- **Long-term adult safety reassurance** — no long-term controlled adult dataset exists; the reassuring class data hold only when IGF-1 is kept in the physiologic range.

**Net:** sermorelin is a **formerly-approved, mechanistically physiologic GH secretagogue with real pediatric-GHD + diagnostic evidence but only biomarker-level adult evidence.** It is promising, monitorable, and broadly available by compounding — but **unproven for the adult goals it is actually marketed for,** prohibited in sport, and doctor-required. Treat all adult human-use framing as off-label, and discuss with a clinician.
