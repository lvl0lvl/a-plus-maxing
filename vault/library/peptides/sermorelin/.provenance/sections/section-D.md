## Section D — Safety & adverse effects

**Defining-fact framing.** Sermorelin (GRF(1-29)NH2; trade name Geref / Geref Diagnostic) is a growth hormone–releasing hormone (GHRH) analog that acts on the pituitary somatotroph to augment *endogenous, pulsatile* GH secretion rather than supplying exogenous GH. [mechanism_review][1] This mechanism is the basis of its generally favorable short-term safety profile: GH output remains subject to negative feedback from somatostatin and IGF-1, and the pharmacodynamic effects reverse on discontinuation. [mechanism_review][1] The principal honest caveat throughout this section is that the *grounded* adverse-event (AE) data derive almost entirely from pediatric idiopathic GH-deficiency (GHD) trials and from diagnostic IV use — the only populations and doses that were formally studied and labeled. Contemporary adult "anti-aging," body-composition, and wellness use is **off-label, at non-label doses in non-label populations** [dose/population-extrapolation], and no comparable long-term controlled adult sermorelin safety dataset exists.

### Labeled and trial-derived adverse effects (pediatric GHD + diagnostic use)

The most consistently reported AE in the Geref clinical program is a **local injection-site reaction** (pain, swelling, or redness), occurring in **about 1 patient in 6** among children treated with daily subcutaneous sermorelin. [regulatory][2] Across the program, **350 patients** were exposed to sermorelin; **3 of 350 discontinued** therapy specifically because of injection-site reactions. [regulatory][2] This local-reaction predominance is corroborated by the Prakash & Goa review, which concluded that "transient facial flushing and pain at injection site were the most commonly reported adverse events" and that single IV doses and repeated once-daily subcutaneous doses are well tolerated. [cohort][1]

Other treatment-related systemic AEs in the chronic-treatment pediatric setting each occurred at **individual rates of less than 1%** and included **headache, flushing, dysphagia, dizziness, hyperactivity, somnolence, and urticaria.** [regulatory][2] The hyperactivity and somnolence signals are noted specifically in the pediatric population. [regulatory][2]

When sermorelin is given **intravenously for diagnostic GH-stimulation testing** (a single high-dose bolus rather than chronic low-dose SC therapy), the labeled AE set is distinct and reflects the acute vasoactive response: **flushing of the face, injection-site pain/redness/swelling, nausea, headache, vomiting, dysgeusia (transient altered taste), pallor, and tightness in the chest.** [regulatory][2] These are characteristically transient. The transient dysgeusia/altered-taste and the dysphagia noted in chronic use should be distinguished — both are minor and self-limited per the label. [regulatory][2]

**Hypothyroidism.** The Geref labeling records an incidence of hypothyroidism during sermorelin therapy of **6.5%**. [regulatory][2] This is consistent with the known phenomenon that augmenting the GH axis can unmask or precipitate central/relative hypothyroidism (GH and IGF-1 increase peripheral T4→T3 conversion and can lower free T4), and it is the mechanistic rationale for thyroid monitoring during therapy. Thyroid function should therefore be assessed before and periodically during treatment, since untreated hypothyroidism also blunts the GH response. [regulatory][2]

**Anti-GRF antibody formation.** A large proportion of treated patients develop **anti-GRF (anti-sermorelin) antibodies at least once during treatment.** [regulatory][2] The clinical significance is unclear: a positive test at one assessment frequently reverts to negative by the next, antibody presence **did not appear to affect growth**, and no specific adverse-reaction pattern was attributable to it. [regulatory][2] No generalized allergic/anaphylactic reactions to sermorelin were reported in the program. [regulatory][2]

### Hypersensitivity

Beyond the local urticaria signal noted above, hypersensitivity to sermorelin itself or to excipients is the formal allergy concern and the basis of the contraindication below. [regulatory][2] For perspective on the GHRH class, the labeled GHRH analog tesamorelin reports hypersensitivity reactions in **4%** of treated patients in controlled trials — a useful order-of-magnitude class reference, though tesamorelin is a different molecule studied in HIV-associated lipodystrophy [dose/population-extrapolation]. [regulatory][3]

### GH-axis class considerations (theoretical / dose-related, not sermorelin-labeled)

The following are **class-level, dose-dependent** risks inherent to raising GH and IGF-1. They are **not** prominent in the pediatric sermorelin label (where physiologic pulsatile augmentation keeps IGF-1 in range) but become relevant at higher exposures or in adults — and they are therefore flagged as theoretical/extrapolated for sermorelin specifically.

- **Fluid retention** (peripheral edema, arthralgia, carpal tunnel syndrome, paresthesias). These are the classic dose-related GH-axis AEs. In the GHRH-analog tesamorelin label, fluid retention manifesting as "edema, arthralgia, and carpal tunnel syndrome" is an explicit warning. [regulatory][3] In a large long-term registry of adult GHD patients on GH replacement, arthralgia occurred in **4.6%**, peripheral edema in **3.8%**, and carpal tunnel syndrome in **1.2%** — low absolute rates that respond to dose reduction. [cohort][4] These are exposure-driven and would be expected only at supraphysiologic sermorelin dosing [dose/population-extrapolation].

- **Glucose intolerance / reduced insulin sensitivity.** GH is a counter-regulatory, insulin-antagonist hormone. The tesamorelin label carries a glucose-intolerance warning and directs evaluating glucose status before initiation and monitoring periodically. [regulatory][3] However, the magnitude appears modest when IGF-1 stays in range: a randomized, placebo-controlled trial of tesamorelin **in type 2 diabetics** found **no significant between-group difference in fasting glucose or HbA1c**, with only a transient fasting-glucose rise at weeks 4–8 in the 2 mg arm that resolved by week 12, and **no discontinuations for loss of diabetes control**; IGF-1 rose dose-dependently but **remained within normal age-adjusted ranges**. [rct][5] Likewise, long-term adult GHD registry data showed a clinically non-meaningful median glucose change (+0.28 mmol/L) and diabetes incidence tracking classical risk factors (BMI, age) rather than treatment. [cohort][4]

- **IGF-1 elevation and neoplasm precaution.** Because the whole point of a secretagogue is to raise GH and hence IGF-1 (a growth factor), persistent supraphysiologic IGF-1 is the central theoretical concern; the tesamorelin label instructs monitoring IGF-1 and considering discontinuation for persistent elevations. [regulatory][3] Reassuringly, large long-term adult GH-replacement data show an all-cancer standardized incidence ratio of **0.92** (no excess), with no increased prostate, colon, or breast cancer risk when IGF-1 is kept in the physiologic range. [cohort][4] The practical safety lever for sermorelin is therefore keeping IGF-1 within the age/sex reference range rather than above it.

### Contraindications

- **Known hypersensitivity** to sermorelin or to any excipient — the formal sermorelin contraindication. [regulatory][2]
- **Concomitant drugs that affect GH release** are a diagnostic-use caution that also bears on therapy: glucocorticoids can inhibit the GH response to sermorelin, and other agents affecting somatotroph function (e.g., somatostatin/analogs, dopamine agents, antithyroid drugs, cyclooxygenase inhibitors) can confound testing and blunt efficacy. [regulatory][2]
- **Pregnancy and lactation** — sermorelin is Pregnancy Category C with no adequate human data, and it is not known whether it is excreted in human milk; use only if clearly needed. [regulatory][2] For the GHRH class, the tesamorelin label is outright **contraindicated in pregnancy.** [regulatory][3]
- **Active malignancy** — not a formal sermorelin-label contraindication, but a strong class precaution given GH/IGF-1 trophic effects: the tesamorelin label **contraindicates active malignancy** and requires any preexisting malignancy to be inactive and its treatment complete before initiating, and also contraindicates use where the hypothalamic-pituitary axis is disrupted (hypophysectomy, hypopituitarism, pituitary tumor/surgery, head irradiation/trauma — settings where a secretagogue cannot work and may be inappropriate). [regulatory][3] Applying these to off-label adult sermorelin use is prudent extrapolation [dose/population-extrapolation].
- **Pediatric vs adult.** Sermorelin's only labeled therapeutic indication was pediatric idiopathic GHD (and adult diagnostic use); there is no labeled adult *therapeutic* indication, so all adult treatment is off-label. [regulatory][2]

### Monitoring and stopping criteria

- **IGF-1 (and IGFBP-3)** — measure at baseline and periodically; the operative target is to keep IGF-1 **within the age/sex-specific reference range**, not above it. Consider dose reduction or discontinuation for persistent IGF-1 elevation. [regulatory][3]
- **Fasting glucose / HbA1c** — assess glucose status before initiation and monitor periodically, especially in pre-diabetes, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome. [regulatory][3]
- **Thyroid function** — given the 6.5% hypothyroidism incidence and that untreated hypothyroidism blunts the GH response, check thyroid status before and during therapy. [regulatory][2]
- **Local tolerability** — track injection-site reactions (the dominant AE); rotate sites; discontinuation for this reason was rare (3/350). [regulatory][2]
- **Stopping criteria** — discontinue for hypersensitivity, persistent supraphysiologic IGF-1, emergent active malignancy, or intolerable fluid-retention/glucose effects; benefits reverse on cessation given the physiologic mechanism. [mechanism_review][1]

### Honest overall framing

The labeled and trial-grounded short-term safety of sermorelin is **favorable**: as a secretagogue augmenting endogenous pulsatile GH under intact feedback, its dominant AE is a minor injection-site reaction, systemic AEs are <1%, no anaphylaxis was reported, and antibody formation appears clinically inconsequential. [regulatory][2][cohort][1] The serious limitation is the **absence of long-term controlled adult safety data**: every quantified figure above comes from pediatric GHD therapy, diagnostic IV use, or the related GHRH analog tesamorelin/GH-replacement literature, **not** from chronic adult sermorelin use at the doses now marketed off-label [dose/population-extrapolation]. The reassuring class-level malignancy and metabolic data hold specifically *when IGF-1 is kept in the physiologic range*; pushing IGF-1 supraphysiologic re-introduces the acromegaly-spectrum risks (edema, arthralgia, carpal tunnel, glucose intolerance) that the labeled physiologic-dose data do not capture. [regulatory][3][cohort][4]
