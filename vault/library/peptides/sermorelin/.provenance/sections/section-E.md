## Section E — Regulatory & sport status

### FDA history: Geref — approved, then commercially withdrawn (not for safety/efficacy)

Sermorelin acetate — the synthetic 29-amino-acid N-terminal fragment of human growth-hormone-releasing hormone, GRF(1-29) — reached the US market as the branded product **GEREF**, held by Serono (later EMD Serono) [regulatory] [1][3]. Two distinct FDA-approved presentations existed, with two distinct intended uses:

1. **GEREF (treatment formulation)** — NDA 20-443, the 0.5 mg base/vial and 1.0 mg base/vial injection, **approved September 26, 1997**, indicated **for the treatment of idiopathic growth-hormone deficiency (GHD) in children with growth failure** [regulatory] [3].
2. **Geref Diagnostic** — the 0.05 mg base/amp injection, indicated **for evaluating the ability of the somatotroph cells of the pituitary gland to secrete growth hormone** (i.e., a diagnostic test of pituitary GH secretory capacity) [regulatory] [1][3].

Both presentations were voluntarily discontinued by the manufacturer. **In December 2008, EMD Serono notified FDA that the GEREF treatment product (NDA 20-443) was being discontinued and requested withdrawal of the application** [regulatory] [1][3]. The diagnostic presentation was likewise withdrawn in this period, attributed to limited commercial demand for the diagnostic indication [regulatory] [1]. This places the canonical "withdrawn ~2008" framing on solid ground: the discontinuation decision was logged with FDA in 2008.

**The load-bearing point — the withdrawal was a commercial/business decision, NOT a safety or efficacy action — is anchored in a primary FDA document.** On **March 4, 2013, FDA published a determination in the Federal Register** titled, verbatim, *"Determination That GEREF (Sermorelin Acetate) Injection, 0.5 Milligrams Base/Vial and 1.0 Milligrams Base/Vial, and GEREF (Sermorelin Acetate) Injection, 0.05 Milligrams Base/Amp, **Were Not Withdrawn From Sale for Reasons of Safety or Effectiveness**"* [regulatory] [2]. This is the formal mechanism by which FDA records that a discontinued drug was pulled for business reasons; the determination's express purpose is to **permit FDA to approve abbreviated new drug applications (ANDAs)** — i.e., generics — for the product, which the agency may only do once it has affirmatively found the original product was not removed for safety or effectiveness [regulatory] [2]. In short: **"withdrawn" here means the sponsor stopped selling it; it does NOT mean "banned" or "deemed unsafe."** This distinction is the most important regulatory fact about sermorelin and is frequently misstated in lay sources.

(Note on dating: the *discontinuation/withdrawal-request* event sits in **2008**; the *Federal Register determination* documenting the non-safety basis was published in **2013**. Both dates are correct for their respective events and should not be conflated.)

### Current status: no marketed FDA-approved product, but compounded under §503A

There is **no currently marketed FDA-approved sermorelin product** in the United States — the branded Geref/Geref Diagnostic lines were discontinued and no generic ANDA has been brought to market despite the 2013 determination opening that pathway [regulatory] [2][3]. Despite the absence of a finished approved drug, **sermorelin is one of the most widely available compounded growth-hormone-axis peptides**, dispensed by §503A compounding pharmacies under prescription [regulatory] [4][5].

Its compounding standing is materially different from the never-approved "research" peptides, and the reason is a point of statutory law. Under **section 503A(b)(1)(A) of the FD&C Act (21 U.S.C. 353a)**, a pharmacy may compound from a bulk drug substance if that substance satisfies **any one** of three **alternative** conditions: (i) it is a **component of a drug approved by FDA**; (ii) it complies with the standards of an applicable **USP/NF monograph**; or (iii) it appears on **FDA's §503A bulk-drug-substances ("bulks") list** [regulatory] [3a]. These are independent pathways — a substance that qualifies under (i) or (ii) **does not need to appear on the bulks list at all**.

**Sermorelin qualifies through the component-of-an-approved-drug pathway** — it was the active ingredient of FDA-approved Geref (NDA 020443), a fact independently anchored above to the 2013 Federal Register determination and the NDA record [regulatory] [2][3]. This prior-approval pedigree is the verifiable, primary-anchored basis for its compounding eligibility, and it is what distinguishes sermorelin from the never-approved peptides whose only possible route is the bulks list.

A note on the bulks-list "categories." FDA's interim policy sorts *nominated* bulk substances into **Category 1** (nominated with sufficient supporting information, not flagged with safety concerns, and against which FDA does not intend to take enforcement action pending a final listing decision) and **Category 2** (substances FDA would consider taking enforcement action against) [regulatory] [4]. Some secondary legal analyses describe sermorelin as a "Category 1" interim-bulks-list substance [regulatory] [5]. **That specific Category-1 placement could not be independently confirmed from a primary FDA bulks-list document as of June 2026**, and it is in any event not the load-bearing basis for sermorelin's compounding standing: the component-of-a-formerly-approved-drug pathway (above) is sufficient on its own and is primary-anchored. Stated accurately: **sermorelin is compoundable under §503A via a qualifying statutory pathway (component of the formerly FDA-approved Geref, and/or — per secondary analyses — interim Category-1 bulks-list treatment); the precise interim-category designation as of June 2026 is not confirmable from a primary FDA source and should not be asserted as fact** [regulatory] [3a][2][5].

**Crucially, sermorelin is NOT among the peptides swept up in the April 2026 FDA peptide actions.** Two related events occurred in mid-April 2026. First, FDA acted, through its parallel §503A bulks-list process, to remove a set of peptide bulk drug substances from **Category 2** of the interim list and to refer them for Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) review; the affected substances are the never-FDA-approved "research" peptides described in the secondary legal coverage [regulatory] [6][7]. Second, FDA published the **PCAC notice of meeting** — **Federal Register Doc. 2026-07361, "Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee; Notice of Meeting; Establishment of a Public Docket; Request for Comments — Bulk Drug Substances Nominated for Inclusion on the Section 503A Bulk Drug Substances List," published April 16, 2026 (FR-2026-04-16)** [regulatory] [6a]. **The Federal Register notice (Doc. 2026-07361) is NOT itself the "removal of 12" enumeration — it is a meeting notice that lists the seven substances set for the upcoming PCAC meeting** [regulatory] [6a]:

1. BPC-157 (free base and acetate)
2. KPV (free base and acetate)
3. TB-500 / thymosin beta-4 fragment (free base and acetate)
4. MOTS-c (free base and acetate)
5. Emideltide / DSIP (free base and acetate)
6. Semax (free base and acetate)
7. Epitalon (free base and acetate)

Per the secondary legal coverage, those seven were set for the **July 23-24, 2026 PCAC meeting**, while the broader Category-2 action affected a total of **twelve** peptides — adding GHK-Cu, Melanotan-II, Dihexa (acetate), PEG-MGF (pegylated mechano growth factor), and Cathelicidin/LL-37 — with the remaining five slated for review before the end of February 2027 [regulatory] [6][7]. (The "twelve" figure traces to FDA's Category-2 bulks-list action as reported in the secondary analyses; the primary Federal Register meeting notice [6a] enumerates only the seven on the meeting agenda.)

**Sermorelin appears in NEITHER the seven-substance PCAC meeting agenda (primary-confirmed: it is not named anywhere in FR Doc. 2026-07361) NOR the twelve-peptide Category-2 removal set (per the secondary coverage)** [regulatory] [6a][6][7]. Every one of those affected peptides is a **never-FDA-approved** substance whose only §503A foothold was a bulks-list nomination; sermorelin's situation is categorically different because it qualifies under the component-of-an-approved-drug pathway. **The April 2026 peptide actions therefore did not restrict sermorelin compounding.**

One further caveat worth stating plainly: a January 7, 2025 FDA policy change ended the categorization of *newly nominated* bulk substances into the interim Category 1/Category 2 framework [regulatory] [4]. That change governs the interim-list mechanics; it does not disturb sermorelin's independent component-of-an-approved-drug eligibility. So as of mid-2026, **sermorelin remains compoundable under §503A**, its standing untouched by the April-2026 peptide action — but the broader question of any eventual *final* §503A bulks-list determination, where relevant, has not been resolved. Where any future final listing is concerned, the honest answer is: not yet finally decided.

### WADA: prohibited at all times (S2.2)

Sermorelin is **prohibited in sport at all times — both in- and out-of-competition** — under **Section S2 (Peptide Hormones, Growth Factors, Related Substances and Mimetics)** of the WADA Prohibited List [regulatory] [8]. Within S2, the relevant subsection is **growth-hormone-releasing factors (S2.2 / S2.2.4 in the list's enumeration)**, which expressly covers GHRH and its analogues. **Sermorelin is named by name**, alongside the other GHRH analogues CJC-1293, CJC-1295, and tesamorelin (and, in the broader subsection, the GH secretagogues such as MK-677/ibutamoren and ipamorelin, and the GHRPs) [regulatory] [8]. As an S2 substance, sermorelin is a **non-threshold prohibited substance banned at all times**; there is no in-competition-only carve-out [regulatory] [8].

### EU status (brief) and gray-market reality

Sermorelin has **no current centrally authorized (EMA) marketed product** in the European Union; as in the US, the historical branded products are off the market and there is no widely marketed approved sermorelin drug in the EU. It is not a controlled/scheduled substance, but absent an authorized product it has no routine licensed clinical channel and is encountered chiefly through compounding/prescriber routes where national rules permit [regulatory].

**Gray-market reality:** the practical supply of sermorelin runs through two channels — (1) **legitimate §503A physician-prescribed compounding** in the US (the dominant lawful route), and (2) an extensive **"research chemical" / not-for-human-use online market** selling sermorelin vials labeled "not for human consumption" to bypass prescription requirements [regulatory][anecdote_aggregate]. The latter carries the usual gray-market hazards (no identity/purity/sterility assurance), and is legally distinct from compounded sermorelin dispensed against a valid prescription. The repeated lay claim that sermorelin is "FDA-approved" is inaccurate — there is no approved product on the market — but the equally common claim that it was "banned" or "pulled for safety" is also inaccurate, as the 2013 Federal Register determination expressly establishes [regulatory] [2].
