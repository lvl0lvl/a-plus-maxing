# FOXO4-DRI (Proxofim)

## Metadata

- **class:** peptide (D-retro-inverso peptidomimetic senolytic)
- **evidence_tier:** D — All human evidence is absent. There is no published human clinical trial, no published human pharmacokinetics, and no human safety database for FOXO4-DRI or any Cleara Biotech next-generation analog. The entire efficacy base is preclinical: three mouse models in the founding 2017 Cell paper (Baar et al.), plus five to six independent rodent or cell-culture extensions. No lifespan endpoint exists even in mice. The compound sits materially behind both dasatinib+quercetin (human pilot, n=9 diabetic kidney disease) and fisetin (Phase 2 RCTs underway) in the senolytic translation pipeline.
- **risk_tier:** experimental — FDA-unapproved; no IND on public record; not on FDA 503A/503B bulk substance lists; not nominated to the PCAC; WADA-prohibited in- and out-of-competition (S0 — Non-Approved Substances); on-target p53-apoptosis activation risk in non-senescent tissues formally flagged in the literature; FOXO4 expressed in heart and testis with cardiotoxicity concern unresolved; grey-market product only; D-chirality unverifiable by standard HPLC CoA.
- **status:** researching
- **last_verified:** 2026-06-20

## Summary

FOXO4-DRI (also called Proxofim in some commercial contexts) is a synthetic **D-retro-inverso (DRI) senolytic peptidomimetic** derived from the forkhead (FH) domain of the transcription factor FOXO4 — specifically the N-terminal disordered region and first alpha-helix that mediate FOXO4's physical interaction with the tumor suppressor p53 [1]. Its molecular weight is approximately 5,382 Da at ~48 amino acids; it carries an appended HIV-TAT cell-permeability sequence that both enables intracellular delivery and, as 2025 structural work demonstrated, participates directly in the binding interface with p53 [2]. The peptide was designed and first reported by Marjolein P. Baar and Peter L. J. de Keizer at Erasmus University Medical Center and published in *Cell* in 2017 in what remains the foundational paper for the compound [1].

**The mechanism in three steps.** First, in senescent cells specifically, FOXO4 protein expression is markedly elevated relative to proliferating or quiescent counterparts; FOXO4 tethers p53 to nuclear DNA-damage foci (DNA-SCARs), preventing p53 from initiating the mitochondrial apoptosis program and thereby keeping the senescent cell alive in its characteristic "zombie" state despite an intracellular milieu primed for death [1]. Second, FOXO4-DRI enters the cell (via the TAT sequence), occupies the same binding surface on the p53 transactivation subdomain 2 (p53 TAD2) as the native FOXO4 forkhead domain, and displaces it — with approximately five-fold higher affinity than the native FOXO4-FH fragment (Kd ~400 ± 280 nM vs. ~2.5 µM) [2]. Third, freed from its nuclear tether, p53 is excluded from the nucleus, translocates to mitochondria, and there activates the intrinsic apoptosis pathway via BAX/BAK-dependent outer-membrane permeabilization, cytochrome c release, and downstream caspase-3 cleavage — selectively in the senescent population that elevated FOXO4 in the first place [1]. Because FOXO4 expression is low in non-senescent cells, non-senescent cells are largely spared at active concentrations, conferring the compound's fundamental selectivity.

**All efficacy evidence is preclinical.** Baar et al. 2017 demonstrated senescent-cell clearance and restoration of fur density, locomotor fitness, and renal function across three mouse models (naturally aged C57BL/6, XpdTTD progeroid, and doxorubicin-chemotoxicity mice; 5 mg/kg IP or IV, three doses every other day) [1]. Independent groups have since replicated or extended the senolytic effect in naturally aged Leydig cells (Zhang et al. 2020, Sun Yat-sen University, IP, 5 mg/kg × 3; restored testosterone in aged male mice) [3], human articular chondrocytes in vitro (Huang et al. 2021, University of Pittsburgh, 25 µM × 5 days; reduced senescent fraction >50% while sparing non-senescent cells) [4], bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in young mice (Han et al. 2022, Peking Union Medical College) [5], keloid fibroblast senescence in vitro (Kong et al. 2025, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences) [6], and vascular endothelial senescence in aged animals (Hu et al. 2026, Wenzhou Medical University) [7]. Structural mechanistic work in 2025 (Bourgeois et al., Nature Communications, PMID 40593617) characterized the FOXO4–p53 TAD2 interaction by NMR and confirmed that phosphorylation of p53 at Ser46 and Thr55 — senescent-state events — further enhances binding affinity for both the native interaction and its displacement by FOXO4-DRI [2]. There is no lifespan endpoint in any of these studies; Baar 2017 assessed healthspan-adjacent markers across a five-day dosing window. **No human clinical trial of FOXO4-DRI exists at any phase.**

**Position in the senolytic landscape.** FOXO4-DRI sits behind the leading small-molecule senolytics in clinical translation by a meaningful margin. Dasatinib plus quercetin (D+Q) is the most translationally advanced combination: a Mayo Clinic open-label pilot in nine adults with diabetic kidney disease (mean age 68.7 years) showed measurable reduction in senescent-cell burden in tissue biopsies and circulating SASP markers following three days of oral dosing [8]. Fisetin has entered Phase 2 randomized placebo-controlled trials (TROFFi in breast-cancer survivors, NCT05595499; STOP-Sepsis in elderly sepsis patients, NCT05758246) [9, 10], though neither has published efficacy readouts. Navitoclax has extensive human pharmacology data from oncology programs, though dose-limiting thrombocytopenia limits its senolytic application. FOXO4-DRI has none of these translational anchors.

**Load-bearing safety concerns.** FOXO4-DRI directly engages p53 — a master regulator of apoptosis, cell-cycle arrest, DNA damage response, hypoxia response, and wound healing — and activates the p53-centric mitochondrial apoptosis pathway. A systemic p53-apoptosis activator could in principle trigger apoptosis in any non-senescent cell that happens to have elevated p53 activity at the moment of exposure. The selectivity hypothesis rests on differential FOXO4 expression as the filter; if that expression filter is incomplete at any tissue, the p53 apoptotic signal will reach non-senescent cells. The published literature explicitly identifies this as a clinical-translation risk: "FOXO4-DRI targets the tumor suppressor p53, a crucial protein, which may lead to various side effects in clinical trials" [11]. Additionally, FOXO4 is expressed in normal human heart and testis tissue (in addition to senescent cells); the Zhang 2020 Leydig-cell study authors explicitly flagged that "special attention must be paid to muscle damage, especially cardiotoxicity," acknowledging an inability to fully evaluate this risk in their mouse system [3]. No published study has assessed cardiac tissue histopathology or troponin/CK at time points appropriate for detecting delayed myocardial injury. A separate concern was raised by the chondrocyte study: FOXO4-DRI treatment increased p21 levels even in senescent chondrocyte cultures, suggesting the compound may act as a cellular stressor beyond selectively executing apoptosis in target cells [4]. The entire published preclinical safety window is an acute profile (five days dosing at 5 mg/kg IP/IV in mice, three published groups); no repeat multi-cycle rodent toxicology study has been published, no immunogenicity study has been conducted, and no ADME characterization exists in any species.

**Research concentration: below the flag.** De Keizer's group holds lead authorship on 1 of 8 primary papers (12.5%), well below the 70% single-group concentration flag. Six of eight primary papers are fully independent, spanning Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou), University of Pittsburgh, INSERM/Hôpital Henri Mondor (Paris), Peking Union Medical College (Beijing), and Wenzhou Medical University — genuine multi-continental independent coverage. The caveat is that most independent papers use FOXO4-DRI as a pharmacological tool to confirm mechanism in rodent disease models rather than advancing clinical translation, and the translational science itself remains largely in de Keizer's orbit via **Cleara Biotech B.V.** (Utrecht, 2018), which holds the founding IP and is advancing next-generation analogs CL04177 and CL04183 toward IND-enabling studies, with no registered human trial as of mid-2026 [12, 13].

**Sourcing problem.** "FOXO4-DRI" purchased through grey-market peptide suppliers cannot be verified as correctly synthesized by standard certificate-of-analysis methods. The D-retro-inverso chirality — which is essential for both proteolytic stability and senolytic activity — is undetectable by standard reversed-phase HPLC or mass spectrometry. A peptide with even one L-amino acid substitution has the same molecular weight but profoundly different pharmacology. Consumer-grade CoAs confirm purity by HPLC but not D-amino acid identity; only chiral-specific HPLC or NMR can confirm the configuration. The purchased material is, in practical terms, of uncertain identity.

**The honest bottom line.** FOXO4-DRI is mechanistically elegant — a rationally designed peptidomimetic that exploits a senescent-cell-specific protein–protein interaction with structural rigor backed by two landmark papers (Baar 2017 Cell, Bourgeois 2025 Nature Communications). But the evidence remains entirely in mice and cell culture, there are no human data of any kind, the compound carries a formally flagged on-target p53/cardiotoxicity safety concern that preclinical short-course data have not resolved, it sits behind small-molecule senolytics in clinical translation, its grey-market form is of unverifiable identity, and the regulatory pathway to human use does not exist in any jurisdiction.

## 1. Identity, Design, and Molecular Mechanism

### 1.1 Identity and nomenclature

FOXO4-DRI takes its name from its molecular origin and design strategy: **FOXO4** names the protein whose interaction surface was mimicked (forkhead box O-class transcription factor 4), and **DRI** is the abbreviation for **D-retro-inverso** — the stereochemical and sequential transformation applied to the native FOXO4 sequence. The compound is sometimes listed in commercial contexts under the proprietary name Proxofim. It is approximately 48 amino acids with a calculated molecular weight of approximately 5,382 Da, placing it at the upper end of the molecular weight range typically called a "peptide" rather than a protein; its complexity and synthesis demands are correspondingly high [1, 2].

The specific FOXO4 segment from which the sequence is derived is the N-terminal disordered region and first alpha-helix of the FOXO4 forkhead domain — the precise region responsible for mediating FOXO4's protein–protein contact with the second transactivation subdomain of p53 (p53 TAD2). A cationic HIV-TAT cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) sequence is appended to enable intracellular delivery, and as the 2025 Bourgeois et al. NMR characterization revealed, the HIV-TAT sequence is not an inert cargo but participates directly in the binding interface with p53 TAD2, contributing to the approximately five-fold affinity advantage FOXO4-DRI holds over the native FOXO4 forkhead fragment [2].

The compound was designed by Marjolein P. Baar and Peter L. J. de Keizer at Erasmus University Medical Center (Rotterdam, Netherlands) and first reported as a published finding in *Cell* on March 23, 2017 [1]. De Keizer subsequently co-founded Cleara Biotech B.V. in Utrecht in 2018 to commercialize FOXO4-based senolytic assets.

### 1.2 The D-retro-inverso design strategy

The D-retro-inverso modification addresses two pharmacological liabilities that afflict native L-peptides designed to disrupt intracellular protein–protein interactions: rapid proteolytic degradation and poor membrane permeability. Each liability is addressed separately by one component of the DRI modification, and they combine synergistically.

Endogenous mammalian proteases — aminopeptidases, carboxypeptidases, and the major serine and cysteine endopeptidases — are stereospecific for L-amino acid substrates. A peptide composed entirely of D-amino acids presents a non-cognate backbone stereochemistry that these enzymes cannot productively engage, making the molecule essentially invisible to the cell's proteolytic machinery. Native L-peptides of FOXO4-DRI's size (~48 residues) are degraded within minutes in serum; the D-amino acid substitution extends functional persistence to an hours-to-days range in biological environments, though no formal pharmacokinetic study has been published for any species [14, 3]. This stability is the primary rationale for the DRI approach and is what distinguishes FOXO4-DRI from earlier, shorter-lived peptide approaches to disrupting protein–protein interactions.

The retro-inversion — reversing the amino-acid sequence — accomplishes something more subtle: it restores the spatial arrangement of side-chain pharmacophores. Because the backbone amide bonds are inverted relative to the retro direction, the side chains of the reversed D-peptide project in approximately the same orientations as in the native L-peptide, a principle termed "topological mimicry" [14]. Without the reversal, a pure D-amino acid version of the original sequence would have the correct stereochemistry at each residue but the wrong overall side-chain geometry to engage the target binding surface. With both modifications together, the DRI peptide presents a binding surface that is structurally similar to the native sequence — capable of occupying the same binding interface on p53 TAD2 — while being pharmacologically invisible to endogenous proteases. In some retro-inverso analogs, cell internalization exceeds the parent L-form by eight-fold or more, likely because the all-D backbone interacts with membrane phospholipids in a geometrically compatible way [14].

### 1.3 Molecular mechanism: the FOXO4–p53 axis in senescent cells

The three-step mechanism of FOXO4-DRI's senolytic action is one of the best-characterized protein–protein interaction disruption mechanisms in the senolytic field, with both functional evidence (Baar 2017 Cell) and high-resolution structural evidence (Bourgeois 2025 Nature Communications).

**Step 1: FOXO4 sequesters p53 in senescent cells.** Cellular senescence is a state in which damaged, stressed, or oncogene-activated cells permanently exit the cell cycle but resist the apoptosis that would normally follow such severe cellular damage. The Baar 2017 study demonstrated in ionizing-radiation-induced senescent IMR90 human lung fibroblasts and replicative-senescent WI-38 fibroblasts that FOXO4 protein expression is markedly elevated in the senescent population compared to proliferating or quiescent counterparts [1]. In the nucleus of these senescent cells, FOXO4 physically binds p53 and tethers it to DNA-damage foci known as DNA-SCARs (DNA Segments with Chromatin Alterations Reinforcing Senescence), preventing p53 from leaving the nucleus and activating the mitochondrial apoptosis cascade. This sequestration keeps senescent cells viable — the "zombie cell" state — despite an intracellular environment that is otherwise primed for apoptosis: elevated pro-apoptotic PUMA and BIM, reduced BCL-2 [1]. The key insight is that FOXO4 is selectively elevated in senescent cells; in non-senescent cells, FOXO4 expression is low and the FOXO4–p53 nuclear complex does not meaningfully form.

**Step 2: FOXO4-DRI competitively displaces FOXO4 from p53.** The 2025 Bourgeois et al. Nature Communications paper (NMR structural characterization in solution) resolved that both partners — the FOXO4 forkhead domain and p53 TAD2 — are intrinsically disordered in isolation and undergo synergistic coupled folding upon complex formation, adopting transient alpha-helical structure at the interface [2]. FOXO4-DRI binds the same p53 TAD2 surface as the native FOXO4 forkhead domain. Biophysical measurement places the binding affinity of FOXO4-DRI at approximately 400 ± 280 nM, compared to approximately 2.5 µM for the native FOXO4 forkhead fragment — a roughly five-fold affinity advantage that ensures the synthetic peptide can competitively displace the endogenous protein at achievable intracellular concentrations [2]. A further structural detail with mechanistic significance: p53 phosphorylation at Ser46 and Thr55 — phosphorylation events associated with the senescent cellular state — further enhances the affinity of both the native FOXO4-FH/p53 interaction and the displacement of FOXO4-FH by FOXO4-DRI. This means FOXO4-DRI is not merely selective because FOXO4 is differentially expressed in senescent cells; it is additionally selective because the p53 TAD2 surface it targets is post-translationally modified in the senescent state in a way that makes it a better substrate for both FOXO4 binding and FOXO4-DRI competition [2]. Both layers of selectivity compound.

**Step 3: nuclear p53 exclusion and mitochondrial apoptosis.** With FOXO4 displaced from its p53 TAD2 binding site, p53 can no longer be retained at nuclear DNA-damage foci. In the Baar 2017 study, immunofluorescence in ionizing-radiation-induced senescent IMR90 fibroblasts demonstrated that FOXO4-DRI treatment caused p53 to shift from a nuclear localization to a cytoplasmic and then mitochondrial localization [1]. Once at the mitochondrial outer membrane, p53 engages the intrinsic apoptosis machinery: the Baar study documented mitochondrial cytochrome c release, BAX/BAK-dependent outer-membrane permeabilization, and downstream caspase-3 cleavage, each occurring selectively in senescent cells and not in proliferating controls at the same peptide concentration [1]. Because non-senescent cells have low FOXO4, they do not form the nuclear FOXO4–p53 complex that FOXO4-DRI disrupts, and therefore p53 is not aberrantly re-localized by the peptide in those cells — the downstream apoptotic cascade is not activated [1].

Subsequent independent studies confirmed this pathway in additional cell types and animal contexts. In naturally aged testicular Leydig cells, IP FOXO4-DRI at 5 mg/kg for three doses produced nuclear p53 exclusion as the proximal mechanistic event, followed by reductions in senescent-cell burden markers and restoration of testosterone secretion [3]. In human umbilical vein endothelial cells treated at 25–50 µM, nuclear p53-Ser15 phosphoprotein exclusion was the key readout [7]. In senescence-enriched human chondrocytes (passage 9 cells, SA-β-gal-positive) treated at 25 µM for five days, the senescent fraction fell by more than 50% while non-senescent (minimally expanded, passage 3) chondrocytes were not significantly affected [4]. Together these independent replications confirm the mechanistic logic across relevant senolytic targets.

### 1.4 Mechanistic distinction from small-molecule senolytics

FOXO4-DRI is mechanistically distinct from the leading small-molecule senolytic strategies in ways that matter for translational reasoning. The BCL-2/BCL-xL inhibitors (navitoclax, ABT-263) operate as BH3 mimetics that directly compete with pro-apoptotic proteins for binding to anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family members at the mitochondrial level, lowering the threshold for BAX/BAK-mediated permeabilization. This approach is active in some but not all senescent cell types, and BCL-xL dependence of platelets imposes dose-limiting thrombocytopenia in every human context tested [15]. Dasatinib plus quercetin target multiple upstream survival signals — dasatinib inhibits ephrin receptors and SRC kinase; quercetin modulates PI3K and BCL-2 family transcription — disrupting AKT-mediated pro-survival signaling rather than directly attacking the apoptotic machinery. Both agents affect pathways active in proliferating as well as senescent cells, which broadens their target tissue footprint [15].

FOXO4-DRI acts on a protein–protein interaction interface (FOXO4 forkhead domain / p53 TAD2) that is selectively assembled in senescent cells because of the differential FOXO4 expression pattern, and triggers apoptosis through a p53-centric mitochondrial route rather than BCL-2-centric route [1]. The selectivity is architecturally different from the small-molecule strategies: it is built into the upstream expression landscape of the target rather than relying on differential sensitivity to a broadly active apoptogenic signal. This design is intellectually elegant and makes mechanistic sense. The open question — which only human trials can resolve — is whether the expression selectivity is physiologically sufficient in vivo in humans, where senescent cell heterogeneity and FOXO4 expression patterns across tissues, organ systems, and disease contexts have not been characterized.

## 2. Senolytic Efficacy: Preclinical Evidence Base

### 2.1 In vitro selectivity — the foundational mechanistic demonstration

The selectivity of FOXO4-DRI for senescent over non-senescent cells was established quantitatively in the Baar 2017 Cell paper using ionizing-radiation-induced senescent IMR90 human fetal lung fibroblasts treated alongside non-senescent (proliferating) IMR90 controls at 50 µM peptide [1]. FOXO4-DRI produced an 11.73-fold greater reduction in viability of senescent compared to proliferating IMR90 cells, assessed by annexin V / propidium iodide staining. A scrambled D-retro-inverso control peptide — same amino acid composition, different sequence, preserving the DRI modification but eliminating the binding specificity — lacked this differential activity, confirming that the senolytic effect is sequence-specific and not an artifact of D-amino acid composition or TAT-mediated membrane perturbation [1]. Non-senescent proliferating cells were largely spared at the active concentration, and the mechanism (p53 nuclear exclusion, caspase-3 cleavage) was confirmed by immunofluorescence and western blot.

The Bourgeois 2025 Nature Communications paper subsequently extended this selectivity characterization to human keloid-derived senescent fibroblasts, providing structural NMR confirmation that the binding interface is precisely the FOXO4-FH / p53 TAD2 coupled-folding surface and that the HIV-TAT sequence contributes binding contacts rather than being mechanistically inert [2]. The keloid-fibroblast extension is an in vitro demonstration in a disease-relevant human primary cell type, not a clinical finding.

### 2.2 The Baar 2017 Cell study: three mouse models

All published in vivo evidence for FOXO4-DRI as a systemic senolytic originates from a single publication — Baar et al. 2017, Cell, 169(1):132–147 [1]. This is the founding paper, and its full in vivo design has not been independently replicated end-to-end. The study used three distinct mouse models, each addressing a different context of senescent-cell burden.

**Model 1: Naturally aged mice.** Male C57BL/6 mice aged approximately 24 months received FOXO4-DRI at 5 mg/kg intraperitoneally on three alternating days. Treatment produced visibly improved coat density and reduced alopecia relative to vehicle-treated aged controls, increased spontaneous locomotor activity on open-field testing, and improvement in plasma urea/BUN and creatinine toward values more typical of younger animals — indicating reduced senescent-cell burden in kidney tissue, confirmed by approximately 30% reduction in p16-positive cells in the renal cortex [1]. The treated mice tolerated the dosing without overt clinical or biochemical toxicity over the observation window.

**Model 2: XpdTTD progeroid mice.** XpdTTD mice carry a homozygous Xpd^TTD/TTD mutation causing nucleotide-excision repair deficiency that drives accelerated cellular senescence and premature aging phenotypes including early alopecia, renal decline, and reduced locomotor fitness. These mice received FOXO4-DRI at 5 mg/kg intravenously — a different route from the aged-mouse arm — on three alternating days. Outcomes included marked recovery of coat density (one of the most visually striking results in the paper), improved plasma creatinine and urea toward younger ranges, and increased locomotor activity [1]. The progeroid mouse is a model of accelerated senescence rather than the normal aging trajectory; findings here are not directly predictive of outcomes in normally aged animals or humans, because the senescent-cell burden in XpdTTD mice is unusually high and generated by a genetic mechanism (repair deficiency) absent in natural aging.

**Model 3: Doxorubicin chemotoxicity.** Young male C57BL/6 mice received doxorubicin chemotherapy (two IP injections at 10 mg/kg each) to drive acute chemotherapy-induced senescence, a well-characterized mechanism by which cytotoxic agents that spare cancer cells leave behind a burden of senescent stromal and epithelial cells. FOXO4-DRI was subsequently administered intravenously at 5 mg/kg for three alternating doses. The peptide reduced the doxorubicin-induced senescent-cell burden, restored physical fitness and body weight, and was well tolerated [1]. This model is highly relevant to chemotherapy-induced aging phenotypes and is a distinct clinical scenario from natural aging; the translational question it addresses — whether senolytic therapy can attenuate the long-term side effects of curative cancer treatment — is a genuine unmet medical need.

Across all three models the dose was 5 mg/kg and the schedule was three doses every other day over approximately five days, delivered IP or IV. Per-group n values reported in the Baar et al. figure captions were n=7–8 for the XpdTTD cohorts and comparable numbers for the other groups; specific per-arm n values are reported in the original primary paper. These are adequate sample sizes for an initial proof-of-concept mouse study but do not provide the statistical power for subgroup analyses or to detect infrequent adverse events.

### 2.3 Independent replications and extensions

Multiple groups independent of the de Keizer/Erasmus group have tested FOXO4-DRI in different biological contexts. The body of evidence is primarily in vitro and in single-disease rodent models; no study has replicated the full Baar 2017 protocol in naturally aged mice with the same multi-outcome design.

**Leydig cell senescence and testosterone — Zhang et al. 2020.** Zhang C, Xie Y, Chen H, and colleagues at Sun Yat-sen University in Guangzhou — fully independent of de Keizer — administered FOXO4-DRI at 5 mg/kg IP every other day for three doses to naturally aged male C57BL/6 mice (n=6 per treated group, ages 20–24 months; n=10 young controls aged 3 months) [3]. Serum testosterone increased significantly; testicular p53, p21, and p16 protein levels decreased; interstitial SA-β-galactosidase activity was reduced; and SASP inflammatory mediators including IL-1β, IL-6, and TGF-β fell. This represents a genuine independent in vivo replication in an aged male cohort, though the outcome readouts are limited to the gonadal compartment and do not recapitulate the multi-organ design of Baar 2017. The nuclear p53 exclusion observed in Leydig cells was the proximal mechanistic event, directly confirming the three-step mechanism in an independent laboratory and a different tissue.

**Spermatogenesis extension — Li et al. 2024.** Li Y, Zhang C, Cheng H, and colleagues at Sun Yat-sen University's Sixth Affiliated Hospital extended the Leydig cell work in a 2024 Experimental Gerontology study, showing that aged male mice treated with FOXO4-DRI exhibited improved sperm quality and spermatogenesis, attributed to reduced SASP secretion from senescent Leydig cells diminishing paracrine suppression of germ cell proliferation [16]. This group shares substantial personnel overlap with the Zhang 2020 group (same institution, some shared authors) and should be understood as an extension in the same gonadal domain rather than a fully independent replication.

**Human chondrocytes in vitro — Huang et al. 2021.** Huang Y, He Y, Makarcyzk MJ, and Lin H at the University of Pittsburgh and Central South University — independent of de Keizer — treated in vitro expanded human articular chondrocytes from eight non-osteoarthritic donors (ages 15–73) with 25 µM FOXO4-DRI for five days [4]. Extensively expanded chondrocytes at passage 9 (high senescent fraction by SA-β-gal, p16, p21 criteria) showed significant reduction in cell number and senescence markers. Minimally expanded passage-3 chondrocytes were not significantly affected, confirming senolytic selectivity in a human primary cell type. This is an in vitro study in human cells — it establishes that the mechanism operates in human chondrocytes at the relevant concentration, which is meaningful preclinical context for osteoarthritis applications, but it does not constitute clinical evidence. A cautionary finding within this study deserves emphasis: FOXO4-DRI treatment increased p21 levels even in the senescent cultures, suggesting the compound may act as a stressor to cells beyond selectively executing apoptosis in target cells [4]. This observation was not followed up and its mechanistic basis is unclear; it cannot be dismissed but also should not be over-interpreted from a single study.

**Pulmonary fibrosis — Han et al. 2022.** Han X, Yuan T, Zhang J, and colleagues at the Institute of Radiation Medicine, Peking Union Medical College — independent of de Keizer — tested FOXO4-DRI at 5 mg/kg IP on days 14, 16, and 18 post-bleomycin instillation in young male C57BL/6J mice (aged 6–8 weeks, n=5 per group) [5]. FOXO4-DRI reduced senescent cell and SASP marker burden, decreased collagen deposition and myofibroblast numbers, and attenuated bleomycin-induced morphological lung damage. This is a disease model in young animals — not an aging study — and the bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis model is a well-established senescence-relevant context that the compound can address mechanistically. The sample sizes (n=5) are small, a limitation to note.

**Pulmonary hypertension — Born, Adnot et al. 2023 (adverse finding).** Born E, Lipskaia L, and colleagues at INSERM U955 / Hôpital Henri Mondor (Créteil, France) — fully independent of de Keizer — tested FOXO4-DRI and other senolytic strategies in mouse models of pulmonary hypertension, published in *Circulation* [28]. The finding is adverse and important: eliminating senescent pulmonary endothelial cells worsened pulmonary hemodynamics rather than improving them, indicating that senescent cells in the pulmonary vascular niche exert a protective or compensatory function in this disease context. This is the only primary FOXO4-DRI study to report a pathological worsening of disease with senolytic treatment, and it directly demonstrates that the blanket "senescent cells are harmful, remove them" framing does not hold across all tissue contexts. Published in a high-impact cardiovascular journal (*Circulation*), this paper is one of the most significant counterpoints in the FOXO4-DRI literature and is discussed further in Section 3.1.

**Keloid fibroblast senescence — Kong et al. 2025.** Kong Y-X, Li Z-S, and colleagues at the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College published in vitro work in Communications Biology (PMID 39994346) demonstrating that FOXO4-DRI induces apoptosis in keloid-derived senescent fibroblasts through nuclear exclusion of p53-Ser15 phosphoprotein — an independent cell-type validation of the canonical mechanism [6]. This is in vitro work in disease-relevant human primary cells.

**Endothelial senescence — Hu et al. 2025/2026.** Hu Z, Li F, Hu C, and colleagues at Wenzhou Medical University — independent of de Keizer — published the most recent extension in Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology (PMID 41625068) [7]. In vitro: 50 µM FOXO4-DRI applied to oxygen-glucose-deprived human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) reduced senescence markers (p21, p16, γ-H2AX) and improved tube formation and migration. In vivo: naturally aged male and female mice (17 months old, n ≥ 4–6 per group) and a D-galactose progeroid model received 5 mg/kg IP every two days for one month, a longer dosing course than the five-day Baar 2017 protocol. Outcomes included reduced aortic p21 and p16, decreased SA-β-galactosidase activity, improved pulse-wave velocity (lower arterial stiffness), and reduced reactive oxygen species. The inclusion of both male and female aged animals is a meaningful extension of the original all-male Baar 2017 aged mouse cohort. However, the D-galactose model is a progeroid accelerated-aging model, not natural aging, and Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology is a lower-tier venue than Cell or Aging Cell.

**Mechanistic structural extension — Bourgeois et al. 2025.** Bourgeois B, Spreitzer E, Plateco-Rochart D, and colleagues at the Medical University of Graz and Utrecht — with de Keizer as a non-lead co-author, making this a collaborative rather than fully independent paper — published the NMR structural characterization of the FOXO4-FH / p53 TAD2 interaction in Nature Communications (PMID 40593617) [2]. This is mechanism-only work, not a new efficacy study, and it reinforces the molecular rationale rather than extending the efficacy evidence.

### 2.4 The critical honest distinction: no human trial exists

The body of evidence reviewed above, taken in full, is impressively broad in cell-type and institutional diversity for a compound first published in 2017. Six of eight primary papers are from fully independent groups. The mechanism has been confirmed in multiple senescent cell types across four continents. But every data point is preclinical — mouse models and cell culture. There is no published human clinical trial at any phase for FOXO4-DRI; no published human pharmacokinetic data; and no human safety database beyond anecdotal self-experimentation reports in online communities, which are explicitly not cited here as evidence. The translation from preclinical efficacy to human efficacy is not established and involves species-specific differences in FOXO4 biology, senescent-cell burden and distribution, tissue expression of the target proteins, and peptide pharmacokinetics that remain entirely untested. The gap between the most advanced mouse data and the most minimal human Phase 1 safety assessment is a gap that cannot be reasoned across; it requires clinical data that do not yet exist.

## 3. The Senescence-Clearance Paradigm and FOXO4-DRI's Position in It

### 3.1 Genetic proof of concept: why senolytics exist

The pharmacological senolytic field was built on two foundational genetic ablation experiments in mice that established causal relationships between senescent-cell accumulation and aging phenotypes — relationships that were previously only correlational.

The INK-ATTAC 2011 paper by Baker, van Deursen, and colleagues engineered a transgenic mouse system in which cells expressing p16^Ink4a — a canonical senescence marker — could be selectively eliminated by administering the small molecule AP20187, which activates a caspase-8 fusion protein under the p16 promoter [17]. In male and female mice on a BubR1 progeroid background, continuous senescent-cell clearance beginning at 3 months of age delayed the onset of aging-associated disorders in adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and eye, and even late-life clearance attenuated the progression of established pathology. This was the first demonstration that senescent cells are causally linked to aging-associated tissue dysfunction — not merely correlated with it — establishing the hypothesis that pharmacological senolytic strategies could mimic the genetic result.

The 2016 follow-up by Baker, Childs, Durik, and colleagues applied the same INK-ATTAC system to naturally aged wild-type mice on two distinct genetic backgrounds (C57BL/6 and F1 hybrid), beginning clearance at midlife (one year of age) [18]. Elimination of naturally occurring p16-positive senescent cells extended median lifespan in both male and female mice — a median lifespan increase of approximately 25% in males — while also delaying spontaneous tumorigenesis and preserving renal, cardiac, and adipose function [18]. This was the first demonstration that senescent-cell clearance can extend lifespan, not only healthspan, in non-progeroid animals. The lifespan result in particular transformed the field and motivated intensive search for pharmacological compounds capable of delivering the same effect.

It is important to be precise about what the INK-ATTAC system is and is not: it is genetic ablation of all p16-expressing cells via an engineered apoptosis trigger, beginning in some experiments at very young ages. No pharmacological senolytic, including FOXO4-DRI, has reproduced this same experiment — achieving the same magnitude of clearance, begun at the same developmental timing, and run for a full lifespan with the same outcome measurements. The INK-ATTAC results establish a principle; they do not directly predict the magnitude of benefit achievable with any current pharmacological compound.

A necessary counterweight to the clearance-as-beneficial framing is that cellular senescence is not uniformly harmful. Senescent cells perform load-bearing physiological roles — suppressing tumorigenesis by permanently arresting damaged cells, contributing to wound healing by secreting growth factors that recruit immune cells and fibroblasts, participating in embryonic tissue remodeling, and supporting liver regeneration [27]. The theoretical cost of indiscriminate senolysis is the collateral removal of beneficial senescent-cell populations alongside the pathological ones. This tension was made concrete by Born, Adnot, and colleagues at INSERM/Hôpital Henri Mondor (2023, *Circulation*): eliminating senescent pulmonary endothelial cells — using several senolytic strategies including FOXO4-DRI — paradoxically worsened pulmonary hemodynamics rather than improving them, suggesting those senescent cells were exerting a protective or compensatory function in the vascular niche [28]. The Born et al. finding does not invalidate the senolytic paradigm, but it is a concrete example of the context-dependence that any systemic senolytic must navigate — and it is a FOXO4-DRI finding, not merely a theoretical caveat from the broader senescence literature.

### 3.2 The senolytic landscape: where FOXO4-DRI stands

Dasatinib plus quercetin were the first pharmacological senolytics identified, emerging from a bioinformatics screen of senescent cell anti-apoptotic pathways (SCAPs), and they remain the most translationally advanced combination [15]. Both agents have extensive safety databases from their respective approved uses (dasatinib as a tyrosine kinase inhibitor for chronic myeloid leukemia; quercetin as a widely available dietary supplement). The Hickson et al. 2019 Mayo Clinic open-label pilot in nine adults with diabetic kidney disease demonstrated that three days of oral dasatinib (100 mg/day) plus quercetin (1,000 mg/day) followed by an 11-day washout measurably reduced senescent-cell burden in adipose tissue and skin biopsies, lowered circulating SASP markers, and reduced macrophage infiltration without serious adverse events in this small sample [8]. This "hit-and-run" intermittent dosing approach — capitalizing on the fact that senescent cells, once killed, require time to re-accumulate — has become the design framework for subsequent senolytic human trials. The D+Q data establish proof-of-concept that pharmacological senolytic activity in a human target tissue is achievable.

Fisetin occupies a position between D+Q and FOXO4-DRI in the translational hierarchy. It is a naturally occurring flavone identified as a potent senolytic in a screen of flavonoids across multiple human cell types including HUVECs, fibroblasts, and preadipocytes [19]. Human clinical investigation is active: the Phase 2 TROFFi randomized placebo-controlled trial (NCT05595499) is evaluating fisetin's effect on physical function in breast cancer survivors [9], and the multicenter STOP-Sepsis adaptive Phase 2 trial (NCT05758246) is randomizing elderly sepsis patients [10]. Neither has yet published efficacy readouts from the completed study in the peer-reviewed literature, meaning fisetin's human senolytic activity is inferred from mechanism and early pilot signals rather than demonstrated in a completed controlled trial. But fisetin is ahead of FOXO4-DRI by the most critical measure: there are human randomized trials. FOXO4-DRI has no registered trial at any phase.

Navitoclax (ABT-263) targets BCL-2, BCL-XL, and BCL-W — anti-apoptotic proteins that senescent cells upregulate to resist their own apoptotic milieu. In mice, oral navitoclax rejuvenated aged hematopoietic stem cells and improved muscle stem cell function by depleting senescent cells in the bone marrow niche [20]. Its senolytic activity is confirmed across multiple cell types [21]. But navitoclax causes dose-limiting thrombocytopenia in humans because platelets depend on BCL-XL for survival, and this liability has effectively prevented its advancement as a senolytic in aging populations. No human aging trial of navitoclax as a senolytic has been completed.

FOXO4-DRI's position in this landscape is clearly defined: it is behind D+Q (human pilot published, measurable biomarker reduction demonstrated), behind fisetin (Phase 2 RCTs active), and broadly comparable to navitoclax in terms of human data (none for senolytic purposes), though navitoclax at least has extensive human pharmacology data from oncology. FOXO4-DRI is the most mechanistically characterized of these in its molecular detail — the 2025 Nature Communications NMR structural work is uniquely sophisticated — but mechanistic characterization does not substitute for the clinical data that are absent.

### 3.3 The lifespan question

A critical boundary that is frequently blurred in longevity-community discussions of FOXO4-DRI is the distinction between healthspan-adjacent outcomes (fur, fitness, renal function) and lifespan extension. The Baar 2017 Cell study assessed healthspan-adjacent markers across a five-day dosing window and a short subsequent observation period; it did not measure survival or lifespan. No subsequent peer-reviewed study of FOXO4-DRI or any Cleara Biotech analog has published a lifespan endpoint in any animal model. Cleara's corporate history page relays that "third-party experiments showed CL04183 to induce an impressive 29% overall survival increase in geriatric mice (825 days at start of treatment)" — but this figure is an unpublished company relay of third-party data that has not appeared in peer-reviewed literature and cannot be evaluated or cited as an established experimental finding [13]. It is a corporate claim in the conventional sense: potentially true, potentially inflated, not independently verifiable. The distinction between published peer-reviewed findings and corporate disclosure of unpublished third-party results matters enormously when the claim is lifespan extension.

### 3.4 The Kirkland caution

The senolytic field's leading translational researcher, James Kirkland, and colleague Tamara Tchkonia wrote in a 2020 Journal of Internal Medicine review that "it is too early for senolytics to be used outside of clinical trials" [22]. That statement was written about D+Q and fisetin — compounds with more human data than FOXO4-DRI. The caution applies with additional force to FOXO4-DRI, where not even Phase 1 human safety data exist. Any use outside a formal clinical trial framework with ethics board oversight cannot draw on a safety database of any kind.

## 4. Safety, Pharmacokinetics, Regulatory Status, and Monitoring

### 4.1 The pharmacokinetics gap

No formal pharmacokinetic study has been published for FOXO4-DRI in any species [3, 14]. There are no published measurements of plasma half-life, volume of distribution, tissue distribution, protein binding, metabolic fate, or clearance. The preclinical dosing interval — every 48 hours at 5 mg/kg IP or IV — implies that the peptide maintains functionally relevant intracellular concentrations for at least 48–72 hours per dose in mice, but this is inference from the dosing schedule, not pharmacokinetic data. All ADME characterization and human immunogenicity assessment are entirely uncharacterized.

The D-retro-inverso modification is expected to confer substantially greater serum stability than a native L-peptide of the same size. Native L-peptides of 48 residues are degraded within minutes in serum by circulating peptidases; the DRI backbone is expected to extend functional persistence to hours or days given the proteolytic invisibility of D-amino acid substrates. But "expected" is not the same as "measured," and the precise half-life in rodents and its cross-species implications for humans are unknown. Human IGFBP-3 concentrations, plasma protease profiles, and renal clearance of large D-amino acid peptides are different from rodents in ways that make allometric PK extrapolation particularly uncertain for a compound of this unusual stereochemistry.

### 4.2 Dosing and route: what the preclinical data used

All published animal efficacy data used intraperitoneal or intravenous injection. The Baar 2017 study administered FOXO4-DRI to naturally aged mice at 5 mg/kg IP (three doses every other day), while the XpdTTD progeroid and doxorubicin-chemotoxicity cohorts received IV injection at the same dose and schedule [1]. Subsequent independent mouse studies used IP injection at 5 mg/kg every two days for one to four-week courses [3, 7]. Subcutaneous administration has not been evaluated in any published study. The grey-market community protocols circulating online — commonly citing doses of 2–10 mg subcutaneously every other day for three doses per cycle, derived by loose allometric reasoning from the 5 mg/kg mouse figure — are entirely unvalidated, have no pharmacokinetic basis, and may not ground any numerical safety or efficacy claim. They are documented here only because they exist; they carry no evidentiary weight.

The allometric conversion from the 5 mg/kg mouse dose to a 70 kg human is not straightforward. Allometric scaling for peptides with unusual pharmacokinetics (all-D backbone, large molecular weight, unknown tissue distribution) has high uncertainty; even simple allometric conversion produces a human dose on the order of 175–350 mg per injection, which at grey-market research-chemical pricing (~$8–32/mg) would cost thousands of dollars per cycle — not even accounting for whether subcutaneous bioavailability comes close to IP bioavailability. Grey-market self-experimenter doses cited online are far lower than even simple allometric equivalents of the mouse dose, meaning they are also operating at concentrations that may be pharmacologically irrelevant even if the product is correctly synthesized.

### 4.3 The p53 activation / on-target off-tumor safety concern

The most important load-bearing safety concern for FOXO4-DRI is inherent to its mechanism of action. The peptide directly engages p53 — the guardian of the genome, the master regulator of apoptosis, cell-cycle arrest, DNA damage response, and dozens of downstream pathways active in proliferating cells, wound healing, immune function, and stress responses — and activates the p53 mitochondrial apoptosis program. The selectivity hypothesis rests entirely on FOXO4 being selectively elevated in senescent cells, such that only in senescent cells does a FOXO4–p53 nuclear complex exist for FOXO4-DRI to disrupt. If that expression filter is imperfect — if any tissue or cell population has sufficient FOXO4 expression and sufficient p53 activity that FOXO4-DRI can trigger apoptosis outside the intended senescent target — then the compound delivers p53-mediated apoptosis to non-senescent cells.

The Baar 2017 paper reported that naturally aged mice treated with FOXO4-DRI showed improved physical fitness and fur density without signs of gross toxicity over the five-day observation window, with blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, platelet counts, and cardiac measurements within normal range [1]. The endothelial cell study similarly found no significant differences in ALT, AST, BUN, or creatinine, and in vitro CCK-8 assays confirmed FOXO4-DRI did not adversely affect non-senescent endothelial cell viability at 50 µM [7]. These are reassuring observations for acute administration at 5 mg/kg IP/IV over days to weeks in mouse models.

However, the published mouse safety data cannot resolve the on-target off-tumor concern as applied to humans, for several reasons. First, none of the published studies was designed as a toxicology study: observation periods were short (maximum approximately 30 days post-dose), histopathology was not systematically conducted across all tissues, and no study evaluated repeat multi-cycle dosing. Second, mouse FOXO4 expression patterns across tissues do not necessarily match human patterns; the assessment of non-senescent toxicity in mouse tissue cannot be directly extrapolated to human tissue. Third, human p53 biology, including the frequency of cells with transiently elevated p53 at any given moment (due to background DNA damage rates, inflammation, or other stressors), differs from mouse biology and is not characterized in the FOXO4-DRI literature.

The concern is formally acknowledged in the published scientific literature. A 2018 FEBS Letters mechanism review of the FOXO4-p53 axis by Bourgeois and Madl — the same Madl who is senior author of the 2025 Nature Communications structural paper — states explicitly that the clinical-translation risk is that FOXO4-DRI "targets the tumor suppressor p53, a crucial protein, which may lead to various side effects in clinical trials" [11]. This is the canonical framing of the on-target off-tumor concern by researchers within the field, not a hypothetical hazard invented by outside critics.

### 4.4 The cardiotoxicity gap

FOXO4 protein is expressed in normal human heart and testis tissue in addition to senescent cells. This expression pattern is the basis of the cardiotoxicity concern flagged by the Zhang 2020 Leydig-cell study authors, who wrote explicitly that "special attention must be paid to muscle damage, especially cardiotoxicity," acknowledging an inability to fully assess this risk due to the technical limitations of their mouse model [3]. To date, no published study has assessed cardiac tissue histopathology, troponin I/T, CK-MB, or other cardiac biomarkers at time points appropriate for detecting delayed myocardial injury following FOXO4-DRI administration. The concern is identified in the literature and then left unaddressed — a gap that is significant precisely because the heart is a tissue that does not renew well and in which p53-mediated apoptosis of non-senescent cardiomyocytes would be particularly consequential.

The chondrocyte p21 signal adds a second layer to this concern. The increase in p21 levels observed in FOXO4-DRI-treated senescent chondrocyte cultures — above and beyond the cell death in the senescent fraction — suggests the peptide may activate a cell-stress program in neighboring or partially-senescent cells that is distinct from the three-step senolytic mechanism [4]. The biological significance of this p21 elevation is unclear and has not been followed up.

### 4.5 Immunogenicity of all-D-amino acid peptides

FOXO4-DRI presents its amino acids in non-natural D-enantiomeric form. The human immune system has not evolved to encounter D-amino acid peptides as antigens (endogenous proteins are uniformly L-amino acid). The immunogenicity of systemically administered all-D peptides in humans is unknown. It is possible that D-amino acid sequences are poorly recognized by antigen-presenting cells, resulting in low immunogenicity; it is also possible that DRI peptides generate novel antigenic epitopes not present in the natural proteome, resulting in unexpected immune reactions. No human immunogenicity data exist for FOXO4-DRI or any closely related DRI compound. This is not a speculative concern; it is simply an uncharacterized variable that every Phase 1 trial of a DRI compound would need to measure as a primary safety endpoint.

### 4.6 Regulatory status

FOXO4-DRI is not FDA-approved for any indication. No Investigational New Drug (IND) application for FOXO4-DRI has been identified in public records as of mid-2026. The compound is not assigned to any category under the FDA 503A bulk substance compounding framework (neither Category 1 for nominated and not yet reviewed, nor Category 2 for approved for compounding, nor Category 3 for nominated and being reviewed or rejected); it is not among the compounds nominated to or reviewed by the FDA Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee. The iRemedy Healthcare peptide intelligence grading characterizes it as "Research use only — not suitable for compounding, no clinical trials identified" — a commercial classification that is consistent with the FDA regulatory status but is not itself the basis for that status [23].

Cleara Biotech is advancing its next-generation analogs CL04177 and CL04183 through IND-enabling work. CL04183 is the current development candidate, with completed GLP-toxicology studies and advanced chemistry, manufacturing, and controls work per company disclosure [13]. Phase I trials have been designed targeting oncology (Phase 1a safety, Phase 1b efficacy in plasma cell malignancies), and a 2024 capital injection enabled Pre-IND/IMPD preparation. However, no Phase I trial for any Cleara Biotech compound including CL04183 has been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov or initiated as of mid-2026 [12, 13]. The seed financing round of approximately $2.5 million (September 2022, per a BusinessWire press release) is modest by clinical development standards, consistent with a company still in IND-enabling rather than clinical-stage work [24].

The critical regulatory point is that the jump from "GLP-tox complete" to "Phase I trial initiated" typically involves IND acceptance by a regulatory authority followed by IRB approval, site initiation, and patient enrollment — a process that can take years and for which no public regulatory milestone has been announced for any Cleara compound.

### 4.7 WADA prohibited status

FOXO4-DRI is not named explicitly on the 2026 WADA Prohibited List, but it falls within WADA Section S0 — Non-Approved Substances, which prohibits all pharmacological substances that have no current approval by any governmental regulatory health authority for human therapeutic use, including drugs under pre-clinical or clinical development, designer drugs, and substances approved only for veterinary use [25]. Since FOXO4-DRI holds no regulatory approval from any government health authority anywhere in the world, it is prohibited in-competition and out-of-competition under S0 for any athlete subject to the World Anti-Doping Code. No named exemption or Therapeutic Use Exemption pathway exists. The S0 classification does not require explicit listing by name; the absence of any approved regulatory status is itself sufficient.

### 4.8 Contraindications and monitoring guidance (preclinical-mechanism-derived)

The following guidance is derived from preclinical mechanism and theoretical risk, not from clinical experience. Because no human clinical data exist, these items cannot be considered clinically validated; they represent a pre-clinical-extrapolation framework for the risk-floor.

Absolute contraindications based on mechanism include active malignancy or history of treated malignancy (p53 pathway manipulation in oncology settings is unpredictable and carries uncharacterized risk), active infection or systemic inflammatory state (p53 is an active effector in immune-cell apoptosis and inflammation), pregnancy (FOXO4 is expressed in placenta; embryotoxic risk is uncharacterized), known hypersensitivity to D-amino acid peptides, and thrombocytopenia or active bleeding disorder (acute p53 apoptosis affecting platelet precursors has not been evaluated). High-caution populations include athletes subject to the WADA code, patients on concurrent cytotoxic chemotherapy or radiation, patients with significant cardiac disease given the unresolved cardiotoxicity concern, and patients with known germline TP53 mutations (Li-Fraumeni syndrome) given the mechanistic interaction with the p53 pathway.

If FOXO4-DRI were to be administered in a human experimental context under ethics board oversight, appropriate monitoring would include hepatic function (ALT, AST, bilirubin at baseline, 7 days, and 30 days post-dose), renal function (BUN, creatinine, eGFR at the same schedule), cardiac biomarkers (troponin I/T, CK-MB, and ECG at baseline, 48 hours, and 7 days, specifically to address the uncharacterized cardiotoxicity concern), complete blood count with differential, anti-drug antibody assay at 30 and 90 days to characterize immunogenicity of the D-amino acid scaffold, and a symptom diary for injection-site reactions, fatigue, myalgias, and palpitations. Stopping criteria derived from mechanism would include ALT/AST greater than three times the upper limit of normal, creatinine rise greater than 1.5-fold from baseline, any troponin elevation above the upper limit of normal, platelet count fall exceeding 30% from baseline, and any sign of systemic immunogenic reaction.

None of these stopping criteria are derived from human experience; they represent reasonable preclinical-extrapolation thresholds that a Phase 1 protocol would need to establish through its own dose-escalation design.

## 5. Research Concentration, Conflict of Interest, and Commercialization

### 5.1 Single-group concentration audit

A systematic audit of the FOXO4-DRI primary literature as of mid-2026 identifies eight peer-reviewed primary-data or direct-mechanism papers indexed on PubMed or equivalent databases.

The first paper (PMID 28340339) is Baar, de Keizer et al. 2017, the founding in vivo paper led by and corresponding-authored by Peter L. J. de Keizer at Erasmus MC Rotterdam, with Jan H. J. Hoeijmakers as penultimate author and Judith Campisi (Buck Institute) as a notable co-author contributing cellular senescence expertise. This is the only paper in the corpus with de Keizer as lead/senior author.

The second paper (PMID 31959736) is Zhang, Liu et al. 2020 from Sun Yat-sen University in Guangzhou — fully independent of de Keizer.

The third paper (PMID 33996787) is Huang, Lin et al. 2021 from the University of Pittsburgh and Central South University — fully independent.

The fourth paper (PMID 36515093) is Born, Adnot et al. 2023 from INSERM and Hôpital Henri Mondor in Paris — fully independent [28]. Notably, this paper's finding is adverse: FOXO4-DRI and other senolytics worsened pulmonary hemodynamics when senescent pulmonary endothelial cells were eliminated, illustrating the context-dependence of the senolytic paradigm (discussed in Section 3.1).

The fifth paper (PMID 39025385) is Li, Liu et al. 2024 from Sun Yat-sen University's Sixth Affiliated Hospital — overlapping personnel with the Zhang 2020 group; same institution, some shared authors.

The sixth paper (PMID 39994346) is Kong, Yan et al. 2025 from the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College, Beijing — fully independent.

The seventh paper (PMID 40593617) is Bourgeois, Madl et al. 2025 from the Medical University of Graz and Utrecht, with de Keizer as a non-lead co-author — a collaborative paper where de Keizer is not the lead.

The eighth paper (PMID 41625068) is Hu, Wang et al. 2025/2026 from Wenzhou Medical University — fully independent.

Counting by lead/corresponding authorship, de Keizer's group holds sole lead authorship on one of eight primary papers (12.5%). In the seventh paper, de Keizer appears as a non-lead co-author of a structural mechanistic study led by the Medical University of Graz group (Bourgeois/Madl). Even counting co-authorship credit generously, de Keizer's direct contribution touches at most two of eight papers (25%). **The 70% single-group concentration flag is not met.** FOXO4-DRI is not a single-group literature; it has genuine multi-continental independent coverage spanning Chinese university hospitals, a French INSERM consortium, a US bioengineering group, and a Beijing academic medical center.

The honest caveat to this positive concentration assessment is that most independent papers use FOXO4-DRI as a pharmacological tool to confirm mechanism in rodent disease models rather than advancing clinical-translation data, and the translational science itself — the path toward human trials and clinical development — remains entirely within de Keizer's orbit via Cleara Biotech.

### 5.2 Conflict of interest: Cleara Biotech B.V.

Peter de Keizer co-founded Cleara Biotech B.V. (incorporated Utrecht, Netherlands, 2018) directly from the 2017 Cell paper intellectual property, holds patent WO/2021/165538 covering FOXO4-p53 interaction-blocking compounds, and serves as Managing Director [13]. His dual role as active academic corresponding author and company founder/MD constitutes a direct financial COI, which is appropriately disclosed in papers where he is a co-author. The 2025 Nature Communications paper (PMID 40593617) explicitly discloses his Cleara Biotech B.V. affiliation alongside his university affiliation [2].

Marjolein Baar, co-first author on the founding 2017 Cell paper, is listed on Cleara's team page as Head of Research, tightening the academic-to-commercial COI loop [13]. The founding Cell paper predated the company's formal incorporation by approximately one year; Cleara was built explicitly to commercialize that work.

This COI is disclosed and should be understood in context: the founding scientific paper is the work on which the compound's entire evidence base rests, and the company developing the compound's most advanced successors (CL04183) was founded by the paper's corresponding author, with the paper's co-first author leading research. This is a common pattern in academic-origin biotech and is not inherently disqualifying, but it means that virtually all promotional characterizations of FOXO4-DRI's potential — including on the Cleara website, in Cleara-affiliated press releases, and in de Keizer's academic talks — should be read with COI awareness.

### 5.3 Pipeline status: from FOXO4-DRI to next-generation analogs

Cleara has evolved beyond FOXO4-DRI proper in its development pipeline. The next-generation compounds CL04177 and CL04183 emerged from a medicinal chemistry optimization campaign between approximately 2018 and 2022. CL04183 is the current development candidate. According to company disclosure, CL04183 shows enhanced binding to "scarred" (conformationally altered) p53 relative to first-generation FOXO4-DRI [13]. Preclinical claims reported by the company include efficacy in colorectal, triple-negative breast, and ovarian cancer models, as well as neurodegeneration and liver fibrosis models. The company has completed GLP-toxicology studies and advanced CMC work, and a 2024 capital injection enabled Pre-IND/IMPD studies. Phase I trials have been designed targeting oncology.

The "29% overall survival increase in geriatric mice" claim associated with CL04183 on the Cleara history page is a company relay of unpublished third-party data [13]. It has not appeared in peer-reviewed literature. It may reflect real experimental results; it may reflect results not yet replicated or analyzed at their full statistical complexity; it may be accurate or inflated. There is no basis in the public literature for evaluating it, and it should be treated as a corporate claim rather than an established finding.

As of mid-2026, no trial for any Cleara compound has been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov [12]. The company's seed financing round of approximately $2.5 million in September 2022 is modest by clinical-development standards, appropriate for a company completing IND-enabling studies but insufficient to carry a program through Phase I–II without additional capital [24]. Cleara is actively seeking Series A financing or pharma partnership, per company disclosure.

### 5.4 Grey-market sourcing: the identity verification problem

FOXO4-DRI is commercially available only as a research chemical from grey-market peptide suppliers, labeled "For Research Use Only / Not For Human Use." No approved product exists in any regulatory framework. More fundamentally, the product sold under that name may not be what the label claims.

The D-retro-inverso chirality is essential to the compound's mechanism: D-amino acids confer proteolytic resistance; the retro-inversion restores the side-chain geometry required for binding p53 TAD2. A peptide synthesized with any L-amino acid substitution — at any of the 46 non-D positions — has the same molecular weight as correctly synthesized FOXO4-DRI and is indistinguishable by mass spectrometry. Standard reversed-phase HPLC, which measures purity by retention time and peak area, does not confirm D-amino acid configuration — it confirms that the sample is a dominant species of a particular size and hydrophobicity, not that the dominant species has the correct stereochemistry. Only chiral-specific HPLC (using chiral stationary phases) or two-dimensional NMR (which can assign configuration at each residue) can verify D-chirality, and these analyses are not part of consumer-grade certificates of analysis from grey-market suppliers.

Coupling failures at any of the approximately 46 coupling steps in the solid-phase peptide synthesis produce truncated or deletion peptides with near-identical molecular weight but zero or reduced senolytic activity. A consumer-grade CoA reporting "98% purity by HPLC" means the sample is predominantly one species of approximately the right molecular weight — it does not mean the species is correctly synthesized, correctly chiral, or the same species the Baar 2017 study used.

One representative supplier (NovoPro Labs) lists FOXO4-DRI at 98.03% HPLC purity with a net peptide content of 69.92% [26] — the 28-point gap between HPLC purity and net peptide content reflects trifluoroacetic acid counter-ion and water content, meaning the effective peptide dose from a nominally "10 mg" vial is approximately 7 mg if the purity claim is accurate, with the caveat that this calculation itself assumes the product is correctly synthesized and chiral. Pricing from grey-market suppliers runs approximately $8–32 per mg depending on quantity. At simple allometric conversion from the mouse dose to a 70 kg human (approximately 350 mg per dose), the cost per three-dose cycle would be approximately $2,800–$11,200 at research-chemical prices — assuming correct synthesis, which cannot be verified.

No compounding pharmacy or licensed clinic can legitimately prepare FOXO4-DRI for human use under any current regulatory framework. The compound lacks the regulatory basis for compounding under US 503A/503B or the EU hospital exemption. Any seller offering it for human use is operating outside regulatory frameworks in every major jurisdiction.

## 6. Synthesis and Honest Bottom Line

FOXO4-DRI represents one of the more intellectually rigorous proposals in the senolytic field: a rationally designed peptidomimetic that exploits a senescent-cell-specific protein–protein interaction identified through mechanistic inquiry into why senescent cells resist apoptosis, backed by structural characterization at NMR resolution. The mechanism is well-defined, the selectivity rationale is biologically grounded, and the evidence that the mechanism operates across multiple cell types and biological contexts is genuine. Independent groups on four continents have confirmed the core finding. The founding paper appeared in Cell, the structural extension in Nature Communications — not predatory-journal artifacts.

But the honest evidence state is: a mechanistically elegant senolytic peptide with zero human data, behind the small-molecule senolytics in clinical translation, carrying a real on-target p53/cardiotoxicity safety concern that preclinical short-course mouse data have not resolved, with unverifiable identity in grey-market form, WADA-prohibited, and FDA-regulatory-basis absent. The Cleara Biotech commercialization path is real but slow: IND-enabling studies are complete or near-complete for CL04183, but no Phase I trial is registered or has begun, the company has raised only seed financing, and the development timeline to any human data is unknown.

The senolytic hypothesis — that periodic pharmacological clearance of senescent cells will translate to healthspan or lifespan benefits in humans — remains preclinical even for the most advanced compounds (D+Q, fisetin). For FOXO4-DRI, the question is not even at the stage of "does the mechanism work in humans" — that question requires a Phase I trial, which does not exist.

Any consideration of FOXO4-DRI as a self-experimentation compound must be evaluated against this full evidence state: no human data of any kind; a p53-centric mechanism that operates on one of the most consequential and broadly active proteins in the human cell; a cardiotoxicity concern explicitly flagged in the published literature and unresolved; and a grey-market product whose D-chirality — the entire basis of both its stability and its selectivity — cannot be verified by standard consumer CoA.
