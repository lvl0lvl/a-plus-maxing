## Section A — Identity, structure, mechanism

### Identity and classification

Hexarelin (also called examorelin; developmental code EP 23905) is a **synthetic hexapeptide growth hormone secretagogue (GHS)** belonging to the **growth hormone-releasing peptide (GHRP) family** [mechanism_review][1][3]. Its defining pharmacological feature is that it is an **agonist of the ghrelin receptor — the growth hormone secretagogue receptor type 1a (GHS-R1a / GHSR-1a)** — the same seven-transmembrane G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) targeted by the other peptidyl secretagogues of its class (GHRP-6, GHRP-2, ipamorelin) and by the endogenous hormone ghrelin [mechanism_review][1][4][6].

This receptor-class identity is the single most important fact for correctly situating hexarelin: **it is a GHRP / ghrelin-receptor agonist, NOT a GHRH (growth-hormone-releasing-hormone) analogue.** It must not be conflated with sermorelin, CJC-1295, or tesamorelin, which are GHRH-receptor agonists acting through an entirely separate receptor and signaling cascade. The mechanistic distinction is foundational and was established experimentally well before hexarelin: in normal men, GHRP and GHRH stimulate GH release through **distinct, non-cross-desensitizing receptor sites**, and their combined administration is synergistic rather than merely additive [rct][2] (discussed below).

### Discovery and research lineage

The peptidyl GHS class originated with C.Y. Bowers and F.A. Momany, whose late-1980s structure–activity work on small enkephalin-derived peptides produced **GHRP-6 (His-D-Trp-Ala-Trp-D-Phe-Lys-NH₂)** as the prototype potent GH releaser [rct][2]. Hexarelin was developed in the early 1990s by **Romano Deghenghi and colleagues (Europeptides / the Italian research lineage centered on Eugenio Müller, Vittorio Locatelli, and the Turin endocrine group of Ezio Ghigo)** as a **structural modification of GHRP-6** [animal (rat)][3][rct][1]. The modification substitutes the second residue's tryptophan with **D-2-methyl-tryptophan (D-Mrp)**, conferring enhanced metabolic/enzymatic stability and increased GH-releasing potency relative to the parent peptide [animal (rat)][3].

The biological target of this peptide class remained an "orphan" until **Howard, Smith, van der Ploeg and colleagues cloned the growth hormone secretagogue receptor (GHS-R)** in 1996 (Science 273:974–977), identifying it as a 7-transmembrane GPCR of the pituitary and hypothalamus and the molecular target of the synthetic GHSs [mechanism_review][6]. Three years later ghrelin was identified as the receptor's endogenous ligand, retrospectively recasting the GHRPs (hexarelin included) as **synthetic ghrelin-receptor agonists** [mechanism_review][4][6].

### Structure

Hexarelin is a six-residue peptide with the sequence:

**His – D-2-methyl-Trp – Ala – Trp – D-Phe – Lys – NH₂**
(His-D-Mrp-Ala-Trp-D-Phe-Lys-NH₂) [animal (rat)][3][vendor_label][5]

- CAS number: 140703-51-1 [vendor_label][5]
- Molecular formula: C₄₇H₅₈N₁₂O₆; molecular weight ≈ 887 Da [vendor_label][5]
- C-terminal amidation (–NH₂) and two D-amino-acid substitutions (D-Mrp at position 2, D-Phe at position 5) are the features that protect the peptide from rapid proteolysis [animal (rat)][3]

(The two D-amino-acid substitutions and the amide cap are structurally load-bearing for protease resistance; the structure is corroborated by both the primary developmental literature [3] and a chemical-supplier identity record [5] — no numeric pharmacology claim rests on the vendor source.)

### Mechanism of action — GH-releasing pathway

Hexarelin's canonical action is to **stimulate endogenous pulsatile GH release** by agonizing GHS-R1a on **anterior-pituitary somatotrophs** and on hypothalamic neurons [mechanism_review][1][4]. The intracellular cascade is the classic **Gq/11 → phospholipase C (PLC) pathway**:

1. GHS-R1a (Gq/11-coupled) activation stimulates PLC [mechanism_review][1][4]
2. PLC hydrolyzes PIP₂ to **inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP₃)** and **diacylglycerol (DAG)** [mechanism_review][4]
3. IP₃ mobilizes **intracellular Ca²⁺**, raising cytosolic calcium [mechanism_review][1][4]
4. The rise in cytosolic Ca²⁺ triggers **exocytosis of GH-containing secretory vesicles** from somatotrophs [mechanism_review][4]

A nitric-oxide-synthase / NO → guanylate cyclase / cGMP arm has also been implicated as a secondary contributor to GHS-R1a–driven GH release [mechanism_review][4].

Beyond the direct somatotroph effect, hexarelin (like ghrelin and the other GHRPs) acts **at two levels**: directly on pituitary somatotrophs, and indirectly within the hypothalamus, where it functions as a **functional antagonist of somatostatin** (the tonic inhibitor of GH) and facilitates endogenous GHRH tone [mechanism_review][4]. Because part of its effect requires an intact hypothalamic GHRH system, the maximal GH response to GHRP-class peptides depends on endogenous GHRH availability [rct][2][mechanism_review][4].

### Synergy with GHRH and the receptor-class contrast

The GHRP/GHRH synergy is mechanistically a **two-receptor convergence onto the somatotroph**. GHRH acts via the **GHRH receptor** (Gs-coupled → adenylate cyclase → cAMP/PKA), priming GH synthesis and release; hexarelin acts via the **GHS-R1a** (Gq → PLC → IP₃/Ca²⁺) while simultaneously withdrawing somatostatin restraint [rct][2][mechanism_review][4]. Because the two converge through **independent receptors and independent second-messenger systems** (cAMP vs IP₃/Ca²⁺), co-administration yields a GH pulse markedly greater than either agent alone — Bowers et al. demonstrated this **synergy in normal men**, showing GHRP and GHRH act through distinct receptor sites [rct][2]. This is the pharmacological rationale for clinical and protocol-level **GHRP+GHRH "stacks"** (e.g., a GHRP such as hexarelin combined with a GHRH analogue) [rct][2][mechanism_review][4]. This synergy is *only* coherent if hexarelin is understood as a non-GHRH agent: a second GHRH analogue added to a GHRH analogue would compete at one receptor, whereas a GHRP plus a GHRH analogue engage two complementary receptors.

### Second receptor: CD36 and GH-independent cardiac action

Hexarelin has a **second, GH-independent molecular target: CD36**, a scavenger-receptor glycoprotein. Bodart et al. radiolabeled rat cardiac membranes with a photoactivatable hexarelin derivative and purified a binding protein of Mr ≈ 84,000 whose N-terminal sequence was identical to **rat CD36**, expressed in cardiomyocytes and microvascular endothelial cells [animal (rat)][7]. Activation of cardiac CD36 by hexarelin in perfused hearts produced a **dose-dependent increase in coronary perfusion pressure** [animal (rat)][7]. This CD36-mediated pathway is the basis for hexarelin's reported cardiovascular/cardioprotective effects that occur **independently of growth-hormone secretion** [animal (rat)][7]. Notably, hexarelin's affinity is **higher for GHS-R1a than for CD36** — published binding shows it binds GHS-R preferentially over CD36 (reported GHS-R/CD36 IC₅₀ relationship favoring GHS-R by roughly two orders of magnitude) [vendor_label][5] — consistent with GH release being its dominant high-affinity action and the CD36/cardiac action engaging at the second site.

### Pharmacokinetics and routes of administration

The most rigorous human PK data come from Ghigo et al., who studied hexarelin after **intravenous, subcutaneous, intranasal, and oral** administration in healthy young volunteers [rct][1]:

- Hexarelin produced **strong, reproducible, dose-dependent GH release by all four routes** [rct][1]
- IV hexarelin at 1 µg/kg produced GH responses roughly **double those of GHRH** at an equivalent dose, underscoring the potency of the GHRP class [rct][1]
- Intranasal hexarelin at 20 µg/kg gave GH responses comparable to 1 µg/kg IV [rct][1]
- **Biological bioavailability** (relative to IV) was **≈ 77% subcutaneous, ≈ 4.8% intranasal, and ≈ 0.3% oral** [rct][1] — i.e., SC is the efficient parenteral route, intranasal is modest, and oral bioavailability of the intact peptide is negligible

The plasma GH response peaks at roughly 30 minutes and returns toward baseline within ~3–4 hours; the peptide itself is short-lived in circulation, consistent with a short plasma half-life typical of small peptides (on the order of tens of minutes), which is why dosing is parenteral and the GH effect is pulsatile rather than sustained [rct][1].

### Potency relative to GHRP-6 and GHRP-2

In the rat, hexarelin and GHRP-6 showed **similar effectiveness** at most tested IV doses, but **hexarelin given subcutaneously elicited a longer-lasting GH release and was slightly more effective than GHRP-6** — attributable to the D-2-methyl-Trp stabilization [animal (rat)][3]. In humans, hexarelin is among the **most potent acute GH releasers** of the peptidyl GHRP class [rct][1]. GHRP-2 is generally regarded as comparably potent within the same ghrelin-receptor-agonist family; all three (GHRP-6, GHRP-2, hexarelin) share the **identical receptor mechanism (GHS-R1a / Gq-PLC-Ca²⁺)** and differ mainly in potency, duration, and off-target (e.g., cortisol/prolactin/ACTH) liability rather than in fundamental pathway [rct][1][2][mechanism_review][4].
