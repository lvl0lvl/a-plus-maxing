## Section D — Safety & adverse effects

> **Defining fact for this section.** Hexarelin is a synthetic hexapeptide growth-hormone-releasing peptide (**GHRP**, a GH-secretagogue-receptor / GHS-R1a agonist — *not* a GHRH analogue). It has **no regulatory approval and no product label** in any jurisdiction; it never advanced past phase-II human pharmacology. There is therefore **no approved-label adverse-event table**. The safety picture here is built from (a) adverse events actually observed in the short human pharmacodynamic/clinical studies (largely the Turin group — Ghigo, Arvat, Broglio — and the Manchester group — Rahim, Shalet), and (b) **class/mechanism reasoning** extrapolated from the GH/IGF-1 axis it activates. Off-label "research-peptide" use is at doses, frequencies, and durations never formally studied **[dose-extrapolation]** — the trial AE profile below should be read as a *floor*, not a ceiling.

### D.1 Adverse effects observed in human studies

The human literature on hexarelin is small (single-dose or short multi-week pharmacodynamic studies in healthy young/elderly adults and selected patient groups), and these studies were powered to measure hormone release, not safety. Within that limit, the consistently reported acute events are mild and transient.

- **Transient facial flushing / warmth** is the most frequently noted acute event after IV/SC hexarelin and is captured as an indexed adverse-effect term in the comparative GHRP pharmacology work; it is short-lived and self-limiting `[1]` *(rct; healthy young + elderly, n=6+6)*. This is a recognized class effect of GHRP-6-family secretagogues.
- **Cortisol / ACTH / prolactin elevation (the key non-selectivity issue).** Unlike the selective secretagogue ipamorelin, hexarelin is **not GH-selective**: acutely it also stimulates the lactotroph and corticotroph axes. In direct human comparison, hexarelin's **ACTH/cortisol-releasing activity was similar to that of human CRH (hCRH)**, and it produced a **slight prolactin rise** (lower than the TRH-driven response) `[1]` *(rct; n=6+6)*. A dose-response study in adults reported a **stepwise cortisol increase (~40% above baseline at 0.5 µg/kg)** and a **prolactin peak ~180% above baseline (ED50 ≈ 0.39 µg/kg)** alongside the GH response `[2]` *(rct; J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1996)*. These are pharmacologic (on-mechanism) endocrine responses rather than toxic events, but they are the distinctive feature that separates hexarelin from selective GHRPs and the basis for the prolactin/cortisol monitoring note below.
- **Chronic dosing does NOT cause sustained over-stimulation of the cortisol/prolactin axes.** In the longest human study — **1.5 µg/kg SC twice daily for 16 weeks in 12 healthy elderly subjects** — there was **no significant change in ACTH AUC, no rise in 24-h urinary free cortisol, and no significant prolactin change**; cortisol if anything transiently *fell* at week 16 and normalized by week 20. The authors concluded over-stimulation of the pituitary-adrenal axis and prolactin "do not occur" with chronic use and that any changes were unlikely to be clinically significant `[3]` *(open_label/clinical trial; Rahim, O'Neill, Shalet, Clin Endocrinol 1999, n=12)*. This is reassuring but applies to a *trial dose in elderly subjects* — not to high-dose off-label cycling **[dose-extrapolation]**.
- **Cardiac / hemodynamic effects (the "double-edged" item).** Hexarelin acts on the myocardium partly through **CD36** (a non-GHS-R1a cardiac receptor), which underlies a **cardioprotective / positive-inotropic signal** but also means it has measurable *acute hemodynamic effects*. In 24 male CAD patients (mean age ~60) given **2.0 µg/kg IV during bypass surgery**, hexarelin produced a prompt rise in LV ejection fraction, cardiac index, and cardiac output with a fall in wedge pressure — i.e. *improved* cardiac performance — but this occurred with a **measurable rise in mean arterial pressure (P<0.05)** and a transient drop in central venous pressure. **Heart rate was unchanged (similar to placebo)** and systemic vascular resistance index did not change `[4]` *(open_label/clinical; Broglio et al., Eur J Pharmacol 2002, n=24)*. The honest reading: the cardiac signal is genuinely **double-edged** — a cardioprotection rationale on one hand, and a real acute pressor/hemodynamic effect on the other, which is the basis for the cardiac-caution note below. There is **no long-term cardiovascular outcome data in humans**.
- **Sleep architecture.** Hexarelin given to healthy volunteers **decreased slow-wave sleep** while stimulating overnight GH/ACTH/cortisol/prolactin secretion `[1]`-adjacent class data; this is a documented pharmacodynamic effect, relevant if dosed at night.
- **Injection-site reactions** are not systematically quantified in the trial literature; SC dosing was used for weeks without reported local intolerance in the chronic study `[3]`, but no numerical injection-site AE rate exists from an admissible source. **No vendor or anecdote source is used to assign any numerical AE rate.**

### D.2 Tolerance / desensitization (a use-pattern risk, not a toxicity)

Repeated hexarelin dosing produces **partial, reversible desensitization of the GH response**. In the 16-week SC study (1.5 µg/kg BID), the GH AUC fell progressively — **19.1 → 13.1 → 12.3 → 10.5 µg/L/h at weeks 0, 1, 4, 16** (most of the attenuation occurring within the first 1–4 weeks) — and **recovered to baseline ~4 weeks after stopping** `[5]` *(open_label; Rahim & Shalet, Growth Horm IGF Res 1998, n=12)*. Mechanistically this reflects GHS-R1a downregulation with sustained agonism. Practical implication: continuous off-label dosing loses efficacy within weeks while continuing to expose the user to the endocrine and hemodynamic effects above — an argument for cycling rather than continuous use.

### D.3 GH/IGF-1-axis class risks (theoretical / extrapolated — not all observed for hexarelin)

These are **class risks inferred from activating the GH→IGF-1 axis**, drawn from the GH-replacement literature; they are *not* demonstrated hexarelin trial AEs and must be read as such.

- **Glucose intolerance / insulin resistance.** GH is counter-regulatory and GH therapy can mildly raise fasting glucose and insulin, especially early and in older/obese individuals `[6]` *(mechanism_review; Reed, Merriam, Kargi, Front Endocrinol 2013)*. **Importantly, acute hexarelin itself did NOT modify glucose or insulin** in healthy young adults despite a marked GH rise — in contrast to ghrelin, which raised glucose and lowered insulin in the same paradigm `[7]` *(rct; Broglio et al., J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2001, n=11)*. So the glucose risk here is a **sustained-exposure / class** concern via chronic IGF-1 elevation, not an acute hexarelin effect — relevant mainly to prolonged off-label use **[dose-extrapolation]**.
- **Fluid retention, peripheral edema, arthralgia/myalgia, carpal tunnel, paresthesia** are the **most common GH-therapy side effects**, dose-dependent, more frequent in older/obese/female patients, and usually reversible on dose reduction `[6]` *(mechanism_review)*. Plausible at supraphysiologic IGF-1 exposure from aggressive hexarelin dosing; not quantified for hexarelin specifically.
- **IGF-1 elevation and theoretical malignancy risk (associational — flag).** GH replacement has **not** been shown to increase de-novo or recurrent cancer, but **higher circulating IGF-1 is epidemiologically associated** with increased risk of several cancers (breast, prostate, colorectal) in large prospective cohorts — modest hazard ratios on the order of ~1.07–1.10 per unit increase `[6]`,`[8]` *(mechanism_review + cohort; UK Biobank prospective analyses)*. This is an **association, not established causation by exogenous secretagogues**, and is the conservative basis for the active-malignancy precaution — not evidence that hexarelin causes cancer.

### D.4 Contraindications (fillable)

Given the absence of a label, these are **precautionary** contraindications derived from mechanism and the AE profile above:

- **Active or recent malignancy** — avoid; GH/IGF-1 axis activation is a theoretical concern given the IGF-1–cancer association `[6]`,`[8]` (precautionary).
- **Uncontrolled diabetes / significant glucose intolerance** — caution/avoid; GH-axis activation can worsen glycemic control with sustained use `[6]`.
- **Cardiac conditions / hemodynamically fragile patients** — caution given the documented acute rise in mean arterial pressure and the CD36-mediated hemodynamic effects `[4]`; no long-term CV safety data.
- **Hyperprolactinemia** — caution; hexarelin acutely raises prolactin `[1]`,`[2]`.
- **Pregnancy and lactation** — avoid; **no human data exist** (no studies in pregnant or lactating subjects).
- **Known hypersensitivity** to the peptide or excipients.

### D.5 Monitoring & stopping criteria (fillable)

- **IGF-1** (interpreted against an **age- and sex-specific reference range**) — baseline and periodically; the central biomarker of cumulative GH-axis exposure.
- **Fasting glucose ± HbA1c** — baseline and periodically, especially with prolonged use or metabolic risk factors `[6]`.
- **Prolactin and morning cortisol** — consider with **chronic** dosing given the non-selective profile, though the 16-week trial found no sustained axis disturbance `[3]`.
- **Blood pressure / heart rate** — reasonable given acute hemodynamic effects, particularly in anyone with cardiac risk `[4]`.
- **Stopping criteria:** IGF-1 above the upper end of the age/sex range; new/worsening hyperglycemia; significant fluid retention, arthralgia, or carpal-tunnel-type symptoms; sustained blood-pressure rise; any new cardiac symptom; pregnancy. Because efficacy desensitizes within weeks, **scheduled cycling/washout** is advisable independent of AEs `[5]`.
