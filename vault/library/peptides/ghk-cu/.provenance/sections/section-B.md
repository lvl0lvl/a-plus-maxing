# Section B — Mechanism of Action (GHK-Cu)

GHK-Cu is the copper(II) complex of the human plasma tripeptide glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine (GHK). Its proposed mechanisms fall into five linked buckets: (1) copper carriage/homeostasis, (2) extracellular-matrix (ECM) remodeling by fibroblasts, (3) broad gene-expression modulation, (4) antioxidant / anti-inflammatory activity, and (5) angiogenic, stem-cell and wound-healing signaling. The strongest mechanistic data are in vitro and animal; human evidence is largely limited to skin-appearance endpoints, so almost every cellular mechanism below is extrapolated to humans rather than demonstrated in them. A second structural caveat: a large share of the "GHK resets gene expression" narrative traces to the Pickart group and rests on a small, single-platform microarray dataset (see Claims 6–7).

## Claims (numbered; inline [N, tag]; model/cell-line + n where stated)

### Copper delivery / homeostasis

1. GHK binds Cu(II) with high affinity — a stability constant of log K ≈ 16.44, essentially equal to that of serum albumin (log K ≈ 16.2) and far above the dipeptide GH-Cu complex (log K ≈ 8.68) [1, mechanism_review][2, in_vitro]. This affinity is the canonical rationale that GHK can hold copper in circulation yet still hand it off to higher-affinity cellular targets [1, mechanism_review][3, mechanism_review]. The constant itself is a physicochemical (potentiometric/spectrophotometric) measurement [2, in_vitro]; the "delivery to higher-affinity targets" framing is interpretive, not a measured flux [1, mechanism_review].

2. Copper coordinates GHK through a square-planar geometry involving the histidine imidazole nitrogen, the N-terminal glycine α-amino nitrogen, and the deprotonated glycyl-histidyl amide nitrogen (a 3N donor set), with axial/carboxylate oxygen participation [2, in_vitro]. This was characterized by potentiometric titration and visible-absorption spectrophotometry in 0.15 M NaCl at 25 °C [2, in_vitro].

### ECM remodeling (collagen, elastin, GAG/proteoglycan)

3. GHK-Cu stimulates collagen synthesis in cultured fibroblasts at extremely low concentrations: stimulation begins between 10⁻¹² and 10⁻¹¹ M, peaks near 10⁻⁹ M, and occurs independently of any change in cell number — i.e., it is a per-cell synthetic effect, not proliferation [4, in_vitro]. The authors note a GHK triplet exists in the α2(I) chain of type I collagen, suggesting wound proteases could liberate endogenous GHK in situ [4, in_vitro].

4. In vivo, GHK-Cu injected into rat subcutaneous wound chambers (steel-mesh cylinders) produced a concentration-dependent increase in dry weight, DNA, total protein, collagen, and glycosaminoglycan content; collagen synthesis was stimulated ~2× that of non-collagen protein, type I and III collagen mRNAs rose (TGF-β mRNA did not), and the relative amount of dermatan sulfate increased. A control tripeptide had no effect, indicating specificity [5, animal]. (Exact n / per-group fold values were not stated in the available text.)

5. GHK-Cu acts on ECM turnover, not just deposition: in fibroblast culture it raised MMP-2 (gelatinase A) secretion and mRNA, and also increased the inhibitors TIMP-1 and TIMP-2 — consistent with controlled remodeling rather than uncontrolled degradation. Notably the MMP-2 effect was reproduced by copper ions but not by GHK peptide alone, implicating the copper moiety [6, in_vitro]. Copper additionally serves as the obligate cofactor for lysyl oxidase, the enzyme that cross-links collagen and elastin — a homeostatic link between buckets (1) and (2), though this is a cofactor-supply inference rather than a directly measured GHK-Cu → lysyl-oxidase activation experiment in these papers [3, mechanism_review].

### Gene-expression modulation (represent carefully)

6. Using the Broad Institute Connectivity Map (cMap), GHK is reported to change expression by ≥50% in ~31.2% of assayed human genes, increasing expression in ~59% and suppressing it in ~41% of affected genes [3, mechanism_review][7, in_vitro]. CRITICAL BASIS: this figure derives from only 3 cMap microarray profiles — 2 from PC3 (prostate) and 1 from MCF7 (breast) cancer cell lines, all at a single 1 µM dose on the Affymetrix Human Genome U133A array (the standard U133A spec is ~22,277 probe sets / ~13,000 genes; [7] specifies the platform and MAS5 processing but does not itself enumerate the probe-set count), with no independent replication, dose-response, or non-cancer validation reported [7, in_vitro]. The "gene-resetting toward a younger profile" language is the Pickart group's interpretive synthesis of this small dataset, not a separate experimental result [3, mechanism_review].

7. The most-cited downstream "reset" claims are external cMap-prediction studies, not GHK assays: (a) Hong et al. 2010 found GHK among the most active of 1,309 cMap compounds at reversing a 54-gene (74-probe-set) "metastasis-prone" signature in mismatch-repair-proficient early colorectal cancer, predicted active at ~1 µM [8, in_vitro]; (b) Campbell et al. 2012 identified a 127-gene emphysema-severity signature and predicted GHK would reverse it toward TGF-β-pathway activation / tissue repair — then showed in cultured human fibroblasts that GHK recapitulated TGF-β-induced expression, organized the actin cytoskeleton, and raised integrin-β1 [9, in_vitro]. Both are computational predictions with limited wet-lab confirmation, and the colon-cancer result (a) is a prediction with no GHK treatment performed in that study [8, in_vitro][9, in_vitro].

### Antioxidant / anti-inflammatory

8. GHK shows antioxidant activity by quenching reactive carbonyl/lipid-peroxidation products. GHK (the peptide alone, not the Cu complex) was shown to quench α,β-4-hydroxy-trans-2-nonenal (4-HNE) [3, mechanism_review]. The Pickart reviews also attribute to GHK the blocking of Cu²⁺-dependent LDL oxidation and inhibition of iron release from ferritin, but these specific quantitative figures are carried in the secondary reviews citing older primary work I could not independently verify; they are reported here only as review-level claims and should not ground numbers [3, mechanism_review].

9. In an animal model of acute lung injury, GHK blocked activation of NF-κB p65 and p38 MAPK, raised superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, and lowered TNF and IL-6 — an anti-inflammatory signature [3, mechanism_review]. This is reported via the 2018 review citing a mouse study; treat as animal-level evidence summarized in review [3, mechanism_review].

### Angiogenesis / stem-cell / wound-healing signaling

10. GHK and related peptides are described as biphasic in vessel signaling — promoting new-vessel outgrowth early in healing (linked to SPARC proteolysis) and restraining it later [3, mechanism_review]. No quantitative VEGF induction is reported in the reviewed sources; the angiogenic claim is mechanistic/qualitative, not a measured vessel-count or VEGF-fold result [3, mechanism_review].

11. GHK-Cu increased keratinocyte proliferation and, in skin-equivalent (3D) models, raised integrin α6 and β1 and p63 positivity (p63 is a putative epidermal stem-cell marker) while making basal cells more cuboidal — interpreted as enhanced "stemness"/proliferative potential of basal keratinocytes; integrin changes confirmed by Western blot [10, in_vitro]. This is in-vitro / organotypic, not in-vivo human.

## Coverage gaps / honest limits

- Human mechanism evidence is essentially absent. Collagen/elastin/GAG, gene-expression, antioxidant, angiogenic and stem-cell mechanisms are demonstrated in vitro or in rats/mice; human data in the reviewed literature are skin-appearance endpoints (not part of Section B). Treat the cellular cascade as plausible-but-extrapolated for humans.
- The gene-expression "resetting" story is thinly grounded. The headline ~31.2% / 59%-up-41%-down figure rests on only 3 cMap microarray profiles (2 PC3 + 1 MCF7), one dose (1 µM), one array platform, no replication or dose-response, and cancer cell lines rather than normal/aged skin or neural tissue [7]. The frequently-quoted colon-cancer (Hong 2010 [8]) and COPD (Campbell 2012 [9]) results are cMap *predictions*; Hong performed no GHK experiment at all, and Campbell's wet-lab confirmation was limited to fibroblast TGF-β-like profiling. The "reverses 70% of a metastatic signature / shifts COPD lungs toward repair" claims circulating online overstate these.
- Pickart-group dependence is high for the integrative narrative. Of the synthesis-level claims (copper-as-master-regulator, gene-resetting, antioxidant breadth, angiogenesis biphasia), the connective tissue is supplied mainly by Pickart/Margolina reviews [1, 3] and the Pickart cMap re-analysis [7]. The hard, independent primaries — Lau & Sarkar binding chemistry [2], Maquart collagen in vitro [4] and in vivo [5], Siméon MMP-2 [6], Hong [8], Campbell [9], Kang [10] — are not Pickart-authored and carry the load for the verifiable, quantitative claims. Roughly: copper-binding and ECM mechanisms = independently grounded; the broad "gene-resetting/anti-aging" mechanism = predominantly Pickart-group and dataset-thin.
- Copper-vs-peptide attribution is unsettled. MMP-2 induction was driven by copper, not the peptide [6]; some antioxidant quenching (4-HNE) was the peptide without copper [3]. So "GHK-Cu" mechanisms are a mix of copper-carrier effects and peptide-intrinsic effects, and few studies cleanly dissect the two.
- No verified binding/kinetic flux data for actual cellular copper *delivery*; log K [2] establishes affinity, not transport. The delivery model is inference.
- Could not independently verify the specific LDL-oxidation-blocking and 87%/75% ferritin-iron figures to original primaries within scope — reported only as review-level claims [3], explicitly not used to ground numbers.
