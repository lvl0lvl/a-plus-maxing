## Section F — Practitioner-practice + non-English

This section covers the authoritative label dosing/titration, the compounded-semaglutide access landscape, muscle-preservation convention, qualitative cost, and a non-English literature survey. Per scope discipline: the FDA-label titration schedules are `regulatory` (authoritative); compounding and adjunct items are reported as practice/regulatory context and **do not ground efficacy or adverse-event-rate claims**, which live in §B/C/D as Tier-1.

### Dosing & titration

Semaglutide is marketed in three FDA-approved formulations, each with a distinct label-mandated titration. All three share the same design logic: a low, sub-therapeutic starting dose escalated slowly to mitigate gastrointestinal (GI) adverse reactions (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea), which cluster during dose escalation.

**Ozempic (semaglutide injection, subcutaneous once-weekly, type 2 diabetes).** Initiate at **0.25 mg once weekly for 4 weeks** (an initiation, not a therapeutic, dose), then increase to **0.5 mg** once weekly. If additional glycemic control is needed after ≥4 weeks, increase to **1 mg**, and if still needed after a further ≥4 weeks, to a maximum of **2 mg** once weekly. The label directs prescribers to "Follow the dosage escalation below to reduce the risk of gastrointestinal adverse reactions." Maintenance is 0.5, 1, or 2 mg weekly [1] `regulatory`.

**Wegovy (semaglutide injection, subcutaneous once-weekly, chronic weight management).** A fixed 16-week escalation to the 2.4 mg maintenance dose: **0.25 mg (weeks 1–4) → 0.5 mg (weeks 5–8) → 1 mg (weeks 9–12) → 1.7 mg (weeks 13–16) → 2.4 mg from week 17 onward** [2] `regulatory`. The label again ties the schedule to GI tolerability ("Follow the dosage escalation in Table 1 to reduce the risk of gastrointestinal adverse reactions") and, importantly, gives explicit tolerability guidance: "If patients do not tolerate a dose during dosage escalation, consider delaying dosage escalation for 4 weeks" [2] `regulatory`. This formalizes the common practitioner practice of "pausing" at a tolerated dose rather than forcing the schedule.

**Rybelsus (oral semaglutide tablets, once-daily, type 2 diabetes).** Start at **3 mg once daily for 30 days** — explicitly stated to be ineffective for glycemic control and used only to acclimate the GI tract — then **7 mg** daily; after ≥30 more days, escalate to **14 mg** daily if additional control is needed [3] `regulatory`. Oral semaglutide must be taken on an empty stomach with ≤4 oz of plain water, ≥30 minutes before the first food, drink, or other oral medication, because absorption is highly dependent on the co-formulated absorption enhancer (SNAC) and is degraded by food/fluid [3] `regulatory`.

The recurring theme — a non-therapeutic starting dose and stepwise 4-week (or 30-day) escalation — is the single most clinically important practitioner-facing fact about semaglutide and is fully label-driven, not improvised by prescribers.

### Compounded / access

During the 2022–2024 semaglutide shortage, compounded semaglutide became widely available through telehealth and compounding pharmacies, sold far below brand price. The regulatory status of this practice has since materially changed, and it should be reported accurately rather than endorsed.

**Shortage resolution and wind-down.** FDA declared the semaglutide injection shortage **resolved on February 21, 2025** (declaratory order) [4] `regulatory`. Compounding of a drug that is a copy of an FDA-approved product is generally permitted only while that product is on the shortage list; resolution therefore triggered a wind-down. FDA set phased enforcement deadlines: **503A pharmacies** (traditional, patient-specific compounding) were given until **~April 22, 2025**, and **503B outsourcing facilities** (which compound in bulk) until **~May 22, 2025**, to stop compounding semaglutide from bulk substance [4][6] `regulatory`. (Tirzepatide's shortage had resolved earlier, in December 2024.)

**Salt-form caution.** FDA and the manufacturer have repeatedly warned that some compounders used **salt forms — semaglutide sodium or semaglutide acetate — which are not the same active ingredient** as the semaglutide base in the approved products and have not been evaluated for safety or efficacy [6] `regulatory`. This is a quality/identity concern, not an efficacy claim.

**Dosing errors.** On **July 26, 2024**, FDA issued an alert on **dosing errors with compounded injectable semaglutide**, largely because patients self-measure and self-draw doses from multi-dose vials (rather than using the pre-filled, fixed-dose pens of Ozempic/Wegovy), producing 10-fold and other overdoses [5] `regulatory`. FDA's general statement applies: "Compounded drugs pose a higher risk to patients than FDA-approved drugs because compounded drugs do not undergo FDA premarket review for safety, quality or effectiveness" [5] `regulatory`.

**Microdosing.** A telehealth/consumer trend of deliberately using **sub-label "microdoses"** (e.g., fractions of the 0.25 mg starting dose) emerged, marketed for gentler appetite effects or metabolic "wellness." This is an off-label, non-evidence-based practice with no FDA-recognized efficacy or safety data; report it only as a practice phenomenon, not as a validated protocol `practitioner_protocol` (trend only — not efficacy-grounding).

### Muscle preservation (practitioner convention)

Substantial weight loss on semaglutide includes loss of lean (fat-free) mass alongside fat mass. The STEP 1 body-composition (DXA) substudy reported that semaglutide produced a greater reduction in fat mass than in lean mass, improving the proportion of fat-free mass, but absolute lean mass still declined [7] (this composition finding is Tier-1, cited here only to frame the convention below). In response, the **consistent practitioner convention** is to pair semaglutide with **(a) resistance/strength training and (b) elevated dietary protein** to blunt lean-mass loss — commonly framed as roughly 1.2–1.6 g protein/kg/day with progressive resistance training during the weight-loss phase `practitioner_protocol` (convention; the specific gram and percentage targets are practitioner guidance, **not** efficacy-grounding). This is a reasonable, physiology-aligned convention and is broadly recommended, but the precise lean-mass-sparing magnitude attributable to it in semaglutide users is not established by Tier-1 trial evidence and must not be reported as a quantified efficacy outcome.

### Cost (qualitative)

Brand semaglutide carries a high U.S. cash list price — roughly on the order of **~$1,300/month for Wegovy and ~$1,000/month for Ozempic** without insurance (manufacturer list prices, 2025) [8] `vendor_label` (pricing only). Manufacturer cash-pay programs (e.g., NovoCare) have reduced the effective cash price for self-pay patients, though access varies. Compounded semaglutide, during its availability window, was dramatically cheaper — commonly cited in the **~$150–$500/month** range [8] `vendor_label` (pricing only) — which was the primary driver of its popularity; that cost gap is now largely moot for legal supply following the shortage wind-down.

### Non-English literature

Semaglutide's evidence base is overwhelmingly global and **English-published**, even for trials run entirely outside English-speaking countries. The major program names — SUSTAIN (T2D injection), STEP (obesity), PIONEER (oral) — are international Novo Nordisk trials whose primary results appear in English-language journals (NEJM, Lancet, JAMA, Diabetes Care, Diabetologia, Diabetes Obesity & Metabolism).

Dedicated **China-population trials** exist (e.g., PIONEER 11 and PIONEER 12, evaluating oral semaglutide in predominantly Chinese participants with type 2 diabetes), but their primary results were published **in English** — e.g., in *Diabetologia* and *Diabetes Obesity & Metabolism* — not in Chinese-language journals [9] `regulatory`-adjacent (peer-reviewed primary; language verification only). Similarly, SUSTAIN China analyses appear in English-language venues.

**Honest result: no distinct non-English-language primary literature was identified that adds findings beyond the English corpus.** Where trials were conducted in non-English-speaking populations (notably China and Japan), the authoritative primary publications are in English. Secondary/clinical-practice commentary undoubtedly exists in Chinese, Japanese, Russian, and other languages, but these are derivative of the English primaries and were not relied upon. There is therefore no separate non-English primary evidence stream to integrate for semaglutide.
