## Section E — Regulatory & sport status

Semaglutide (Novo Nordisk) is a long-acting GLP-1 receptor agonist marketed under three distinct brands with three distinct, separately-approved indications and dosing schemes: **Ozempic** (subcutaneous, type 2 diabetes), **Rybelsus** (oral, type 2 diabetes), and **Wegovy** (subcutaneous 2.4 mg, chronic weight management). The diabetes approvals (Ozempic, Rybelsus) and the obesity approval (Wegovy) are **separate FDA/EMA authorizations with separate maximum doses** — Ozempic is dosed up to 2 mg once weekly for glycemic control, while Wegovy is titrated to a 2.4 mg once-weekly maintenance dose for weight management. They are not interchangeable products.

### FDA approvals (United States)

**Ozempic (semaglutide injection, subcutaneous) — type 2 diabetes.** `[regulatory]` Ozempic was first FDA-approved on **December 5, 2017** (NDA 209637) as an adjunct to diet and exercise to improve glycemic control in adults with type 2 diabetes; it was the first once-weekly GLP-1 receptor agonist with a semaglutide backbone brought to the U.S. market [1][4]. The current FDA label (DailyMed, Novo Nordisk) lists three indications: "as an adjunct to diet and exercise to improve glycemic control in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus"; "to reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus and established cardiovascular disease"; and "to reduce the risk of sustained eGFR decline, end-stage kidney disease, and cardiovascular death in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease" `[regulatory]` [1]. The cardiovascular-outcomes indication followed the SUSTAIN-6 data, and the renal/CKD expansion followed the FLOW trial; both are reflected in the present label's indications section [1].

**Rybelsus (oral semaglutide tablet) — type 2 diabetes.** `[regulatory]` Rybelsus was FDA-approved in **September 2019** as an adjunct to diet and exercise to improve glycemic control in adults with type 2 diabetes; it is the first oral GLP-1 receptor agonist `[regulatory]` [4][5]. It is taken by mouth once daily (a co-formulation with the absorption enhancer SNAC enables oral delivery of the peptide). Its EU label confirms the same therapeutic scope: "treatment of adults with insufficiently controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus to improve glycaemic control as an adjunct to diet and exercise" [5].

**Wegovy (semaglutide injection 2.4 mg, subcutaneous) — chronic weight management.** `[regulatory]` Wegovy was FDA-approved in **June 2021** for chronic weight management `[regulatory]` [4]. The current FDA label (DailyMed) states it is indicated "to reduce excess body weight and maintain weight reduction long term in: adults and pediatric patients aged 12 years and older with obesity" and in overweight adults with at least one weight-related comorbidity, and — as a separately-added indication — "to reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular (CV) events (CV death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, or non-fatal stroke) in adults with established CV disease and either obesity or overweight" [2]. The recommended maintenance dose is **2.4 mg administered subcutaneously once weekly** [2].

**Wegovy cardiovascular indication (post-SELECT).** `[regulatory]` On **March 8, 2024**, FDA approved the new cardiovascular-risk-reduction indication for Wegovy, making it the first weight-loss medication also approved to reduce the risk of cardiovascular death, heart attack, and stroke in adults with established cardiovascular disease and either obesity or overweight `[regulatory]` [3]. The approval rested on the SELECT trial, a multinational placebo-controlled study of more than 17,600 participants in which major adverse cardiovascular events occurred in 6.5% of Wegovy recipients versus 8.0% on placebo [3]. Notably, this indication is in a population **without** type 2 diabetes — distinguishing it from Ozempic's diabetes-tied CV indication.

The key regulatory point for the wiki: **obesity (Wegovy) and type 2 diabetes (Ozempic/Rybelsus) are governed by separate FDA approvals, separate labels, and separate maximum doses.** `[regulatory]`

### EMA approvals (European Union)

All three brands are centrally authorized across the EU `[regulatory]`:
1. **Ozempic** — EU marketing authorization granted **8 February 2018**; indicated for "treatment of adults with insufficiently controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus as an adjunct to diet and exercise," as monotherapy when metformin is inappropriate or in combination with other glucose-lowering drugs [6].
2. **Rybelsus** — EU marketing authorization granted **3 April 2020**; the first oral GLP-1 receptor agonist authorized in the EU, indicated for type 2 diabetes glycemic control as an adjunct to diet and exercise [5].
3. **Wegovy** — EU marketing authorization granted **6 January 2022**; indicated as an adjunct to a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity for weight management in adults (BMI ≥30, or ≥27 with weight-related comorbidity) and in adolescents aged 12+ with obesity and body weight over 60 kg [7].

### The shortage and compounded semaglutide (the regulatory arc)

`[regulatory]` Surging demand for semaglutide in 2022–2024 outstripped supply, and FDA placed **semaglutide injection on its Drug Shortage list**. Under U.S. law, a drug's presence on that list opens the door to compounding: pharmacies operating under section **503A** and outsourcing facilities under section **503B** of the FD&C Act may compound copies of an FDA-approved drug that is in shortage. This drove a large, well-documented market in compounded semaglutide during the shortage years [8].

That window then closed. FDA determined the **semaglutide injection shortage was resolved**, per a decision memorandum dated **February 21, 2025** [8][10]. Because compounding had become widespread, FDA announced **enforcement-discretion transition periods** rather than an immediate cutoff: it did not intend to act against **503A** compounders for shortage-dependent violations until **April 22, 2025**, and gave **503B** outsourcing facilities a longer runway, until **May 22, 2025** [8]. After those dates the legal basis for compounding semaglutide as a shortage drug fell away. FDA has further **proposed to exclude semaglutide (along with tirzepatide and liraglutide) from the 503B bulk-substances list**, which would foreclose a separate compounding pathway [9].

It is important not to overstate current legality: routine large-scale compounding of semaglutide as a shortage substitute is **no longer permitted** now that the shortage is resolved `[regulatory]`. FDA has issued sustained warnings about non-FDA-approved semaglutide products `[regulatory]`: compounded versions "do not undergo FDA premarket review for safety, quality or effectiveness"; FDA identified "serious concerns with compounded versions of semaglutide and tirzepatide, including dosing errors, use of unapproved **salt forms** and adverse events—some requiring hospitalization" [9]. The salt forms in question — **semaglutide sodium** and **semaglutide acetate** — are different chemical entities from the **semaglutide base** in FDA-approved products, and FDA has stated these salt forms are not the approved active ingredient [9]. Separately, FDA has warned consumers about **counterfeit Ozempic** (semaglutide 1 mg) found in the legitimate U.S. drug supply chain, of which it has seized thousands of units [11]. The net regulatory picture as of mid-2026: only Novo Nordisk's FDA-approved Ozempic/Rybelsus/Wegovy are sanctioned; compounded, salt-form, and counterfeit products carry explicit FDA safety warnings and, post-shortage, largely lack a lawful compounding basis.

### WADA / sport status

`[regulatory]` **The load-bearing, primary-anchored fact: semaglutide and GLP-1 receptor agonists are NOT on the WADA Prohibited List** — they are not prohibited in or out of competition `[regulatory]`. This was verified directly against the U.S. Anti-Doping Agency's published Prohibited List page, which was opened in this pass; USADA is a WADA-signatory national anti-doping organization that adopts the WADA International Standard, and its page contains **no listing of semaglutide or GLP-1 receptor agonists** among prohibited substances or methods [12]. The practical takeaway for athletes: semaglutide is **permitted** in sport and does **not** require a Therapeutic Use Exemption on prohibited-list grounds.

`[regulatory]` Separately, **GLP-1 weight-loss agonists (semaglutide and tirzepatide) are reportedly being added to the WADA Monitoring Program for 2026** `[regulatory]`. This Monitoring-Program statement rests on secondary reporting (insidethegames.biz coverage of the 2026 list) and **could not be confirmed against the wada-ama.org primary in this pass** — the official WADA 2026 Monitoring Program document and the WADA Summary of Major Modifications PDFs would not render on repeated attempts [13][14]. The USADA page opened here references a separate "Monitoring Program – 2026 WADA Prohibited List" resource as existing but does **not** enumerate its contents, so it does **not** independently confirm the GLP-1 entry [12]. This claim is therefore presented as **reported, not primary-verified**.

The Monitoring Program, in any case, is explicitly **not** a prohibition: WADA monitors substances to detect patterns of potential misuse and to inform whether future control is warranted, and a finding for a monitored substance is **not** an anti-doping rule violation. The bottom line — semaglutide is permitted in sport today — is unaffected by whether the monitoring-program addition is confirmed. This status should be re-verified against each annual WADA list directly from wada-ama.org once it renders, as monitored substances are the most likely candidates for future scheduling.

### Cost / access (qualitative)

`[anecdote_aggregate]` In the U.S., branded semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) historically carried a high list price, and insurance coverage for the **obesity** indication has been substantially more restrictive than for the **diabetes** indication — a coverage gap that fueled cash-pay demand and, during the shortage, the compounded-semaglutide market `[anecdote_aggregate]`. With the shortage resolved and compounding curtailed, access has shifted back toward branded supply and manufacturer cash-pay/self-pay programs. Affordability and payer coverage (especially for weight management) remain the dominant practical access barriers rather than legal availability `[anecdote_aggregate]`.
