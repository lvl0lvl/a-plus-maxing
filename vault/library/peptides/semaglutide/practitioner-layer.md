# Semaglutide — Prescribing-Practice Layer

> **Scope discipline.** This document records *how semaglutide is actually dosed, titrated, compounded, and accessed in practice*. For semaglutide — unlike a gray-market peptide — the authoritative prescribing source is the **FDA label**, so the titration schedules here are `regulatory` (authoritative), not extrapolated convention. The compounding/access, microdosing, and muscle-preservation items are reported as **practice/regulatory context** and are admissible **only** for dose/route/access conventions. **None of them grounds an efficacy or adverse-event-rate claim** — those live in the semaglutide research report (§2/§3/§5) as Tier-1 trial evidence and are unaffected by anything below.
>
> **Regulatory state, read first.** Semaglutide is **FDA- and EMA-approved** across T2D (Ozempic SC, Rybelsus oral), chronic weight management (Wegovy SC 2.4 mg), cardiovascular risk reduction, MASH (accelerated), and renal indications. The diabetes and obesity approvals are **separate authorizations with separate maximum doses** and are **not interchangeable products**. The 2022–2024 shortage opened a large compounded-semaglutide market; FDA declared the injection shortage **resolved February 21, 2025**, and the lawful compounding window has since largely closed (full arc below). Nothing here endorses current compounding legality.

## Dose / route conventions (label-driven — `regulatory`, authoritative)

All three formulations share one design logic: a **low, sub-therapeutic starting dose escalated slowly** to mitigate GI adverse reactions (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea), which cluster during escalation. This — a non-therapeutic starting dose and stepwise 4-week (or 30-day) escalation — is the single most clinically important practitioner-facing fact, and it is fully label-driven, not improvised.

**Ozempic (SC, once-weekly, T2D).** Initiate **0.25 mg once weekly for 4 weeks** (an initiation, not a therapeutic, dose), then **0.5 mg** once weekly. If additional glycemic control is needed after ≥4 weeks, increase to **1 mg**, and if still needed after a further ≥4 weeks, to a maximum of **2 mg** once weekly. The label directs: "Follow the dosage escalation below to reduce the risk of gastrointestinal adverse reactions." Maintenance is 0.5, 1, or 2 mg weekly [1] `regulatory`.

**Wegovy (SC, once-weekly, chronic weight management).** A fixed escalation to the 2.4 mg maintenance dose: **0.25 mg (weeks 1–4) → 0.5 mg (weeks 5–8) → 1 mg (weeks 9–12) → 1.7 mg (weeks 13–16) → 2.4 mg from week 17 onward** [2] `regulatory`. The label ties the schedule to GI tolerability and gives explicit guidance: "If patients do not tolerate a dose during dosage escalation, consider delaying dosage escalation for 4 weeks" [2] `regulatory` — formalizing the common practitioner practice of "pausing" at a tolerated dose rather than forcing the schedule (this is why the full escalation often spans ~16–20 weeks in practice).

**Rybelsus (oral tablets, once-daily, T2D).** Start at **3 mg once daily for 30 days** — explicitly stated to be ineffective for glycemic control and used only to acclimate the GI tract — then **7 mg** daily; after ≥30 more days, escalate to **14 mg** daily if additional control is needed [3] `regulatory`. Oral semaglutide must be taken on an empty stomach with ≤4 oz of plain water, ≥30 minutes before the first food, drink, or other oral medication, because absorption depends on the co-formulated absorption enhancer (SNAC) and is degraded by food/fluid [3] `regulatory`.

## Compounded / access landscape (practice/regulatory context — NOT efficacy)

During the 2022–2024 semaglutide shortage, compounded semaglutide became widely available through telehealth and compounding pharmacies, sold far below brand price. The regulatory status has since materially changed and is reported here accurately rather than endorsed.

**Shortage resolution and wind-down.** FDA declared the semaglutide injection shortage **resolved on February 21, 2025** (declaratory order) [4] `regulatory`. Compounding a copy of an FDA-approved product is generally permitted only while that product is on the shortage list, so resolution triggered a wind-down. FDA set phased enforcement deadlines: **503A pharmacies** (patient-specific compounding) until **~April 22, 2025**, and **503B outsourcing facilities** (bulk compounding) until **~May 22, 2025**, to stop compounding semaglutide from bulk substance [4][6] `regulatory`. FDA has further proposed to exclude semaglutide (with tirzepatide and liraglutide) from the 503B bulk-substances list. (Tirzepatide's shortage had resolved earlier, December 2024.) After these dates the legal basis for compounding semaglutide as a shortage drug fell away — routine large-scale compounding as a shortage substitute is **no longer permitted**.

**Salt-form caution.** FDA and the manufacturer have repeatedly warned that some compounders used **salt forms — semaglutide sodium or semaglutide acetate — which are not the same active ingredient** as the semaglutide base in approved products and have not been evaluated for safety or efficacy [6] `regulatory`. This is a quality/identity concern, not an efficacy claim. FDA separately warned consumers about **counterfeit Ozempic (1 mg)** found in the legitimate U.S. supply chain.

**Dosing errors.** On **July 26, 2024**, FDA issued an alert on **dosing errors with compounded injectable semaglutide**, largely because patients self-measure and self-draw doses from multi-dose vials (rather than using the pre-filled, fixed-dose pens of Ozempic/Wegovy), producing 10-fold and other overdoses [5] `regulatory`. FDA's general statement applies: "Compounded drugs pose a higher risk to patients than FDA-approved drugs because compounded drugs do not undergo FDA premarket review for safety, quality or effectiveness" [5] `regulatory`.

**Microdosing.** A telehealth/consumer trend of deliberately using **sub-label "microdoses"** (e.g., fractions of the 0.25 mg starting dose), marketed for gentler appetite effects or metabolic "wellness," emerged. This is an off-label, non-evidence-based practice with no FDA-recognized efficacy or safety data; it is reported here **only as a practice phenomenon, not a validated protocol** `practitioner_protocol` (trend only — not efficacy-grounding).

## Muscle-preservation adjuncts (practitioner convention)

Substantial weight loss on semaglutide includes loss of lean (fat-free) mass alongside fat mass (the STEP-1 DXA substudy: greater fat-mass than lean-mass reduction, improving the fat-free proportion, but absolute lean mass still declined [7] — a Tier-1 composition finding cited here only to frame the convention). In response, the **consistent practitioner convention** is to pair semaglutide with **(a) resistance/strength training and (b) elevated dietary protein** to blunt lean-mass loss — commonly framed as roughly **1.2–1.6 g protein/kg/day with progressive resistance training** during the weight-loss phase `practitioner_protocol` (convention; the specific gram targets are practitioner guidance, **not** efficacy-grounding). This is a reasonable, physiology-aligned, broadly-recommended convention, but the precise lean-mass-sparing magnitude attributable to it in semaglutide users is **not established by Tier-1 trial evidence** and must not be reported as a quantified efficacy outcome.

## Named-prescriber / guideline protocols

No single named-prescriber "off-label protocol" supersedes the FDA label for semaglutide, which is the unusual case for this library: because semaglutide is an approved Rx with label-mandated titration, the authoritative dosing is the label itself ([1]–[3] above), and prescribers titrate within it. The principal practitioner-level *deviations* from the label that circulate are (a) the compounded-supply and (b) sub-label microdosing phenomena described above — both reported as practice/regulatory phenomena, neither grounded as a validated protocol. No additional named-prescriber or society protocol with a distinct, citable semaglutide dose/cycle beyond the label was relied upon here; this is reported honestly rather than fabricated.

## Cost (qualitative — `vendor_label`, pricing only)

Brand semaglutide carries a high U.S. cash list price — roughly **~$1,300/month for Wegovy and ~$968–1,000/month for Ozempic** without insurance (manufacturer list prices, 2025) [8] `vendor_label` (pricing only). Manufacturer cash-pay programs (e.g., NovoCare) have reduced the effective self-pay price; access varies, and payer coverage for the **obesity** indication is more restrictive than for **diabetes**. Compounded semaglutide, during its availability window, was dramatically cheaper — commonly cited **~$150–$500/month** [8] `vendor_label` (pricing only) — the primary driver of its popularity; that cost gap is now largely moot for legal supply following the shortage wind-down.
