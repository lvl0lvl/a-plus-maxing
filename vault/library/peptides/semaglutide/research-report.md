# Semaglutide

## Metadata
- **class:** peptide — an acylated, long-acting **GLP-1 receptor agonist** (a 94%-sequence-homology analogue of native human GLP-1(7-37)); a peptide drug, not a small molecule, acting as a full agonist at the class-B GPCR GLP-1R [1, regulatory][2, mechanism_review].
- **brands / routes:** **Ozempic** (subcutaneous, once-weekly, type 2 diabetes [T2D]) and **Wegovy** (subcutaneous 2.4 mg, once-weekly, chronic weight management) are injectable; **Rybelsus** (oral, once-daily, T2D) is the tablet co-formulated with the absorption enhancer SNAC. All three are Novo Nordisk products [1, regulatory][3, regulatory].
- **manufacturer:** Novo Nordisk.
- **evidence_tier: A** — semaglutide is supported by **multiple large Phase-3 randomized controlled trials** (the SUSTAIN/PIONEER glycemic programs and the STEP obesity program) plus **hard cardiovascular** (SELECT) and **renal** (FLOW) outcome trials, and carries **FDA and EMA approvals** across three indications. This is the first A-tier entry of the sweep. The one structural caveat is **single-sponsor concentration**: ~76% of the pivotal registrational primaries are Novo Nordisk-funded — partially (not wholly) offset by adjudicated hard endpoints, double-blind placebo control, and independent FDA/EMA review (full evidence in §2–§4; concentration audit in §4).
- **risk_tier: medium** — approved and well-characterized, but with real adverse effects (GI-dominant; gallbladder; pancreatitis), a **rodent-derived thyroid C-cell boxed warning** (not demonstrated in humans), **weight regain on discontinuation**, and **lean-mass loss** that together keep it doctor-required.
- **status:** researching
- **last_verified:** 2026-06-20

> **Read this first.** Semaglutide is a **genuinely approved, genuinely efficacious GLP-1 receptor agonist** with a landmark randomized-trial base — not a gray-market peptide. The **real benefits** are large and population-specific: substantial glycemic control in T2D (the SUSTAIN/PIONEER programs, ~1.5–1.8% HbA1c at top injectable dose); **a mean ~14.9% weight loss in adults with obesity WITHOUT diabetes** (STEP-1, Wilding 2021, NEJM, PMID 33567185); a **~20% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events in obesity WITHOUT diabetes** (SELECT, Lincoff 2023, NEJM, HR 0.80, PMID 37952131); and a **~24% reduction in a kidney-failure composite in T2D with chronic kidney disease** (FLOW, Perkovic 2024, HR 0.76). The **real caveats** are equally concrete: **gastrointestinal adverse effects dominate** and are the main discontinuation driver; **weight is largely REGAINED on discontinuation** (~two-thirds of the loss within a year, STEP-1 extension); **lean/muscle-mass loss accompanies the weight loss**; gallbladder disease and (precautionary) pancreatitis are labeled concerns; the **thyroid C-cell / medullary-thyroid-carcinoma BOXED WARNING is RODENT-based and has NOT been demonstrated in humans**; and the 2023 **suicidality signal was investigated and has NOT been confirmed**. **Population matters everywhere**: T2D, non-diabetic obesity, and T2D+CKD are distinct populations with distinct magnitudes and distinct approved indications — figures are not interchangeable across them. **One integrity correction the reader must carry: the pivotal obstructive-sleep-apnea trial (SURMOUNT-OSA) studied TIRZEPATIDE, a different drug — do NOT credit OSA benefit to semaglutide.** Regulatory: FDA-approved (Ozempic 2017, Rybelsus 2019, Wegovy 2021), EMA-approved; WADA **does NOT prohibit** semaglutide (it is reportedly being added to the 2026 *Monitoring Program* — a secondary-reported, non-prohibition status). ## TL;DR

Semaglutide is the most clinically validated agent in this library to date: an engineered, long-acting analogue of the gut incretin hormone GLP-1 that lowers blood glucose, drives large weight loss, and — in dedicated outcome trials — reduces cardiovascular events and slows kidney-disease progression. The benefits are real and, unusually for this library, anchored in hard, adjudicated endpoints from tens of thousands of randomized patients. But the magnitudes are **population-specific** and must be read that way: ~14.9% weight loss applies to adults with obesity *without* diabetes (STEP-1), but only ~9.6% in adults with obesity *and* T2D at the same dose (STEP-2); the 20% MACE reduction (SELECT) was demonstrated in obesity *without* diabetes; the kidney benefit (FLOW) is in T2D *with* CKD. The honest counterweights are equally well-documented: the effect is **maintained only with continued treatment** — most weight is regained after stopping; weight loss carries an **obligatory lean-mass cost**; gastrointestinal side effects are common and the leading reason patients quit; gallbladder disease is modestly increased; and the two most alarming-sounding warnings turn out to be the most qualified on human evidence — the **thyroid C-cell boxed warning is rodent-derived and not shown in humans**, and the **suicidality signal was investigated and not confirmed** (real-world data, if anything, point the other way). The evidence base is **single-sponsor-concentrated** (Novo Nordisk), a genuine bias source partly mitigated by hard endpoints and regulatory review. Semaglutide is FDA- and EMA-approved across diabetes, obesity, cardiovascular-risk-reduction, and renal indications, is **not prohibited in sport**, and — with the 2024–2025 shortage now resolved — the lawful compounding window has largely closed. Net: a high-evidence, approved, doctor-managed therapy with substantial benefit and a real, manageable, honestly-stated risk profile.

## 1. Identity, structure & mechanism

### 1.1 Identity and classification

Semaglutide is a **glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist** — an acylated, long-acting analogue of the native incretin hormone GLP-1(7-37), developed and marketed by Novo Nordisk [1, regulatory]. The U.S. prescribing information classifies it as "a GLP-1 analogue with 94% sequence homology to human GLP-1" [1, regulatory]. It is therefore a **peptide drug, not a small molecule**, and acts as a full agonist at the GLP-1 receptor (GLP-1R), a class-B G-protein-coupled receptor [2, mechanism_review].

Semaglutide is supplied in two route-distinct formulation families, both Novo Nordisk products [1, regulatory]:

1. **Once-weekly subcutaneous injection** — branded **Ozempic** (T2D) and **Wegovy** (chronic weight management). Approved injectable strengths include 0.25 mg, 0.5 mg, 1 mg and 2 mg, administered once weekly at any time of day, with or without meals [1, regulatory].
2. **Once-daily oral tablet** — branded **Rybelsus**, co-formulated with the absorption enhancer SNAC, in 3 mg, 7 mg and 14 mg strengths [3, regulatory].

The once-weekly-injectable-versus-once-daily-oral contrast is a defining feature of the molecule and follows directly from its pharmacokinetics and the formulation chemistry of the oral product (below).

### 1.2 Primary structure: the engineered modifications

Native human GLP-1(7-37) is rapidly degraded in vivo by the protease dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) and cleared renally, giving the endogenous hormone a half-life of only ~1–2 minutes [2, mechanism_review]. Semaglutide was engineered from this backbone with **three** deliberate modifications [2, mechanism_review; 1, regulatory]:

1. **Aib8 substitution** — the alanine at position 8 (the DPP-4 cleavage site) is replaced with α-aminoisobutyric acid (Aib), a non-coded amino acid; this renders the peptide resistant to DPP-4 degradation while retaining high GLP-1R affinity. The label describes modification "in position 8 to provide stabilization against degradation by the enzyme dipeptidyl-peptidase 4 (DPP-4)" [1, regulatory; 2, mechanism_review].
2. **Arg34 substitution** — lysine at position 34 is replaced with arginine, removing a competing lysine so acylation can be directed site-specifically to the single remaining lysine (Lys26) [2, mechanism_review].
3. **Lipidation at Lys26** — a **C18 fatty di-acid** (octadecanedioic acid) is attached to Lys26 via a hydrophilic linker (a γ-glutamic acid spacer plus two 8-amino-3,6-dioxaoctanoic acid [OEG] spacers — the γGlu-2×OEG linker). Knudsen and Lau report that "a C18 di-acid together with a γGlu-2xOEG linker resulted in the highest albumin affinity combined with GLP-1R potency" [2, mechanism_review]. The label summarizes this as "modification of position 26 lysine with a hydrophilic spacer and a C18 fatty di-acid" [1, regulatory].

The unacylated peptide backbone, crystallized with the GLP-1R, is essentially identical to native GLP-1(7-37), confirming the modifications protect the molecule without abolishing its receptor-binding pharmacophore [2, mechanism_review].

### 1.3 How the modifications confer a ~1-week half-life

The C18 fatty di-acid is the key to the long duration of action. The di-acid binds **reversibly and with high affinity to circulating serum albumin**; the label states semaglutide "is extensively bound to plasma albumin (>99%)" [1, regulatory]. This albumin "depot" achieves three pharmacokinetic effects at once [2, mechanism_review; 1, regulatory]: **reduced renal filtration** (albumin-bound peptide is too large to filter); **protection from enzymatic degradation** (albumin binding plus Aib8 shields from DPP-4 and other proteases); and **a slow-release reservoir** (only the small free fraction is active at any instant).

The net result is an elimination half-life of **approximately 1 week (~165 hours)**; the label states that "with an elimination half-life of approximately 1 week, semaglutide will be present in the circulation for about 5 weeks after the last dose" [1, regulatory]. This is precisely what permits once-weekly subcutaneous dosing.

### 1.4 Comparison to liraglutide and to GIP/GLP-1 co-agonists

Semaglutide is an iterative improvement on **liraglutide** (Victoza/Saxenda), the earlier Novo Nordisk analogue. Liraglutide carries a shorter **C16 mono-acid (palmitate)** chain joined by a γGlu linker only (no OEG spacers), giving weaker albumin binding and a half-life of ~13 hours — hence once-daily dosing — whereas semaglutide's C18-di-acid/γGlu-2×OEG design lifts the half-life to ~1 week, enabling once-weekly dosing [2, mechanism_review]. (Dulaglutide is another once-weekly GLP-1 RA, but uses IgG4-Fc fusion rather than fatty-acid acylation [2, mechanism_review].)

By contrast, **tirzepatide** (Mounjaro/Zepbound, Eli Lilly) is a **different molecule and a different pharmacological class**: a **dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor co-agonist** — a single peptide activating both the glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor and the GLP-1R — rather than a GLP-1-only agonist [4, mechanism_review]. It must not be conflated with semaglutide despite overlapping indications (this distinction is load-bearing for the OSA correction in §3).

### 1.5 Mechanism of action

As a GLP-1R agonist, semaglutide reproduces and amplifies the physiological actions of GLP-1 across multiple tissues [1, regulatory; 5, mechanism_review]:

1. **Glucose-dependent insulinotropic effect** — it "stimulates insulin secretion… in a glucose-dependent manner" from pancreatic β-cells, so insulin release is augmented only when glucose is elevated; this glucose dependence is the basis for the class's low intrinsic hypoglycemia risk [1, regulatory].
2. **Glucagon suppression** — it "lowers glucagon secretion… in a glucose-dependent manner" from α-cells, reducing hepatic glucose output [1, regulatory].
3. **Slowed gastric emptying** — "a minor delay in gastric emptying in the early postprandial phase," blunting post-meal glucose excursions and contributing to satiety, mediated partly via vago-vagal circuitry [1, regulatory; 5, mechanism_review].
4. **Central/hypothalamic satiety and reduced energy intake** — brain GLP-1 receptors mediate appetite suppression and weight loss, with activation at hindbrain circumventricular sites (area postrema, nucleus tractus solitarius) and hypothalamic populations (arcuate and dorsomedial nuclei); these neurons "are clearly activated by GLP-1RAs and appear to mediate at least some of the effects" on eating control [5, mechanism_review].

Together these explain the dual therapeutic profile: improved glycemic control in T2D (insulin/glucagon/gastric effects) and clinically meaningful weight loss (central satiety + reduced energy intake). The "incretin effect" — oral glucose eliciting a larger insulin response than IV glucose because nutrient passage releases GLP-1 (from L-cells) and GIP (from K-cells) — is blunted in T2D; semaglutide's engineering converts the fleeting endogenous signal into a sustained, once-weekly pharmacological agonist [2, mechanism_review].

### 1.6 Pharmacokinetics summary and the oral-formulation problem

- **Subcutaneous semaglutide:** half-life ~1 week (~165 h); >99% albumin-bound; once-weekly; steady state in 4–5 weeks [1, regulatory].
- **Oral semaglutide (Rybelsus):** peptides are normally destroyed by gastric acid/proteases and absorbed negligibly, so oral delivery required a formulation solution. Rybelsus co-formulates each tablet with **salcaprozate sodium (SNAC)** — *sodium N-(8-[2-hydroxybenzoyl]amino)caprylate* — an absorption enhancer that "facilitates the absorption of semaglutide after oral administration," with absorption occurring "predominantly… in the stomach" by creating a localized higher-pH region that protects the peptide and promotes transcellular passage across the gastric epithelium [3, regulatory]. Even so, **estimated absolute oral bioavailability is only ~0.4–1%** across the 3/7/14 mg doses [3, regulatory]. This is why Rybelsus must be taken **once daily on an empty stomach in the morning with ≤4 oz (~120 mL) of plain water**, then **wait ≥30 minutes before eating, drinking, or other oral medications** [3, regulatory]. Once absorbed, the systemic molecule is the same semaglutide with the same ~1-week half-life [3, regulatory].

### 1.7 Coverage gaps (Section 1)
- The mechanistic/structural account is drawn from the FDA labels [1,3] and two mechanism reviews [2,5]; no independent crystallographic or biophysical primary was opened in-corpus beyond what those reviews summarize.
- Central-satiety circuitry is characterized at the class level (GLP-1 RAs generally) rather than from a semaglutide-specific human neuroimaging primary.

## 2. Glycemic and weight efficacy

Semaglutide's efficacy spans two distinct, separately-powered Phase-3 programs at different doses: **SUSTAIN / PIONEER for T2D glycemic control**, and **STEP for chronic weight management** (largely in people *without* diabetes). These are different populations, different licensed doses, and different magnitudes — figures are **NOT interchangeable** across programs, and population is annotated on every claim.

### 2.1 Glycemic efficacy in T2D (SUSTAIN, injectable)

The SUSTAIN program established once-weekly subcutaneous semaglutide (licensed T2D doses 0.5 mg and 1.0 mg; 2.0 mg added later) as a high-efficacy glycemic agent, with HbA1c reductions clustering around **~1.5%** at 1.0 mg, accompanied by weight loss — substantially larger than most oral antihyperglycemics [6, rct].

The most informative head-to-head is **SUSTAIN-7** [6, rct], an open-label Phase-3b trial of **1,201 adults with T2D on metformin** (HbA1c 7.0–10.5%), randomized over **40 weeks** to semaglutide vs the GLP-1 RA dulaglutide:
- Semaglutide **0.5 mg**: HbA1c **−1.5%**, weight **−4.6 kg** — vs dulaglutide 0.75 mg: −1.1%, −2.3 kg (T2D on metformin) [6, rct].
- Semaglutide **1.0 mg**: HbA1c **−1.8%**, weight **−6.5 kg** — vs dulaglutide 1.5 mg: −1.4%, −3.0 kg (T2D on metformin) [6, rct].

Semaglutide was superior to dulaglutide on both glycemic control and bodyweight at matched dose tiers, positioning it as the most potent injectable GLP-1 RA of its class era [6, rct].

### 2.2 Glycemic efficacy in T2D — oral semaglutide (PIONEER, Rybelsus)

The PIONEER program tested **oral** semaglutide (licensed doses 3, 7, 14 mg once daily) in T2D, demonstrating that an orally bioavailable GLP-1 RA could deliver clinically meaningful glycemic control [10, regulatory/review].

- **PIONEER 1** [7, rct] — Aroda et al., monotherapy in **703 adults with T2D** (baseline HbA1c 8.0%) over **26 weeks**; placebo-subtracted HbA1c reductions were dose-dependent — **3 mg −0.6%, 7 mg −0.9%, 14 mg −1.1%** — with placebo-subtracted weight change of −0.1 / −0.9 / **−2.3 kg** (T2D, monotherapy) [7, rct].
- **PIONEER 2** [8, rct] — Rodbard et al., oral semaglutide **14 mg vs empagliflozin 25 mg** in **822 adults with T2D uncontrolled on metformin**; at week 26 HbA1c fell **−1.3%** (semaglutide) vs −0.9% (empagliflozin), with weight loss **−4.7 kg vs −3.8 kg** at week 52 (T2D on metformin) [8, rct]. Oral semaglutide was superior on HbA1c.

The PIONEER overview confirms dose-dependent HbA1c lowering, the 14 mg dose providing the greatest effect, broadly tracking — though not exceeding — the injectable program's magnitudes (T2D) [10, regulatory/review].

### 2.3 Weight efficacy in obesity (STEP, semaglutide 2.4 mg)

The STEP program tested the **higher 2.4 mg once-weekly** dose (the obesity dose, marketed as Wegovy — distinct from the ≤1.0–2.0 mg T2D doses).

**STEP-1 — the landmark obesity trial** [9, rct]. Wilding JPH et al., *N Engl J Med* 2021;384(11):989-1002 (PMID 33567185). In **1,961 adults with overweight/obesity WITHOUT diabetes** (BMI ≥30, or ≥27 with a comorbidity), randomized 2:1 to semaglutide 2.4 mg vs placebo for **68 weeks** with lifestyle intervention:
- **Mean weight change −14.9%** (semaglutide) vs −2.4% (placebo); treatment difference **−12.4 percentage points** [9, rct].
- Absolute change **−15.3 kg** vs −2.6 kg [9, rct].
- ≥5% loss: **86.4%** vs 31.5%; ≥10%: **69.1%** vs 12.0%; ≥15%: **50.5%** vs 4.9% (all P<0.001) [9, rct].

This **~15% mean weight loss in adults without diabetes** is the defining efficacy fact of the obesity program (population: non-diabetic obesity) [9, rct].

**STEP-2** [11, rct] — Davies M et al., *Lancet* 2021;397(10278):971-984 (PMID 33667417). In **1,210 adults with overweight/obesity AND T2D**, the **same 2.4 mg dose** produced a **smaller** mean weight loss of **−9.6%** vs −3.4% placebo (difference −6.2 pp; p<0.0001); ≥5% loss 68.8% vs 28.5% (population: T2D obesity) [11, rct]. The lower magnitude vs STEP-1 is exactly why T2D and non-diabetic figures must not be generalized across one another.

**STEP-4** [12, rct] — Rubino D et al., *JAMA* 2021 (PMID 33755728), a maintenance/withdrawal design: after a 20-week run-in to 2.4 mg in **803 adults without diabetes**, continuing semaglutide produced **−7.9%** further change (weeks 20→68) vs **+6.9% regain** on switch to placebo (difference −14.8 pp; P<.001) [12, rct]. The effect is **maintained only with continued treatment** (population: non-diabetic obesity).

**STEP-8** [13, rct] — Rubino DM et al., *JAMA* 2022;327(2):138-150 (PMID 35015037), a head-to-head vs liraglutide 3.0 mg daily in adults with overweight/obesity **without diabetes**; semaglutide 2.4 mg produced significantly greater weight loss than liraglutide (population: non-diabetic obesity) [13, rct].

### 2.4 Dose-response, durability, and the cross-program picture

- **Dose-response is consistent within each program:** oral 3→7→14 mg gives −0.6→−0.9→−1.1% HbA1c [7]; injectable 0.5→1.0 mg gives −1.5→−1.8% HbA1c and −4.6→−6.5 kg [6]; the obesity 2.4 mg dose drives the ~15% weight effect [9].
- **Glycemic magnitude (T2D):** ~1.5–1.8% HbA1c at top licensed injectable dose [6]; up to −1.3% for oral 14 mg vs an active comparator [8].
- **Weight magnitude depends sharply on population:** ~14.9% (non-diabetic obesity, 2.4 mg) [9] vs ~9.6% (T2D obesity, same dose) [11] — the T2D attenuation is real and reproducible.
- **Durability requires continuation:** STEP-4 withdrawal shows regain off-drug [12]; efficacy is sustained, not curative.

### 2.5 Coverage gaps (Section 2)
- The broader SUSTAIN 1–6 trials are summarized via the ~1.5% HbA1c clustering [6]; only trials whose primary sources were opened are cited individually. Likewise STEP-3 (intensive behavioral therapy) and STEP-5 (2-year durability) form the rest of the maintenance/durability arm but were not opened as primaries in-corpus.
- Long-term (>2-year) weight durability on continued therapy is inferred from the maintenance/withdrawal data rather than from a dedicated multi-year primary opened here.

## 3. Cardiovascular, renal & other outcomes

Semaglutide is among the few weight/metabolic agents with dedicated, hard-endpoint cardiovascular-outcomes trials (CVOTs) and a renal-outcomes trial. The defining integrity issue here is again **population**: the pivotal outcome trials span three distinct populations — T2D (SUSTAIN-6), overweight/obesity **without** diabetes (SELECT), and T2D **with** CKD (FLOW) — and magnitude and approved-indication status differ by population.

### 3.1 Cardiovascular outcomes

**SELECT — the landmark non-diabetic finding** [14, rct]. Lincoff AM et al., *N Engl J Med* 2023 ("Semaglutide and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Obesity without Diabetes"; PMID 37952131) randomized **17,604** adults aged ≥45 with **BMI ≥27 and pre-existing established cardiovascular disease but WITHOUT diabetes** to subcutaneous semaglutide **2.4 mg** weekly vs placebo (mean follow-up 39.8 months). The primary three-point MACE composite (CV death, nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke) occurred in **6.5% (569/8,803) vs 8.0% (701/8,801)**, hazard ratio **0.80 (95% CI 0.72–0.90; P<0.001)** — a ~20% relative risk reduction [14, rct]. This is the key, oft-mis-cited result: the benefit was demonstrated **without diabetes**, establishing a CV indication driven by adiposity/cardiometabolic risk rather than glycemic control. On the basis of SELECT, semaglutide 2.4 mg (Wegovy) carries an **approved** CV risk-reduction indication for adults with established CVD plus obesity/overweight. Tolerability caveat from the same trial: permanent discontinuation for adverse events was higher on semaglutide (16.6% vs 8.2%), driven by GI effects [14, rct].

**SUSTAIN-6 — the original T2D signal** [15, rct]. Marso SP et al., *N Engl J Med* 2016 ("Semaglutide and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes"; PMID 27633186) randomized **3,297** patients **with T2D** (83% with established CVD, CKD, or both) to semaglutide 0.5 or 1.0 mg weekly vs placebo over 104 weeks. The primary MACE composite occurred less often with semaglutide: HR **0.74 (95% CI 0.58–0.95; P<0.001 for noninferiority)** [15, rct]. It was designed as a pre-approval safety (noninferiority) CVOT, not powered for superiority, but the point estimate showed a 26% relative reduction. **Integrity note — the retinopathy signal:** SUSTAIN-6 reported a **significant increase in diabetic retinopathy complications** (vitreous hemorrhage, blindness, or need for intravitreal treatment/photocoagulation), HR **1.76 (95% CI 1.11–2.78; P=0.02)** [15, rct] — widely attributed to the magnitude/rapidity of early glycemic lowering in patients with pre-existing retinopathy rather than direct retinotoxicity, but a labeled caution and an honestly-reported harm.

Population distinction (do not conflate): SUSTAIN-6 = **T2D**; SELECT = **non-diabetic** overweight/obese with CVD. The mechanism of CV benefit is plausibly shared (weight loss, blood-pressure and inflammatory reduction, possible direct vascular effects), but the two trials are not interchangeable evidence.

### 3.2 Renal outcomes

**FLOW — kidney outcomes in T2D + CKD** [16, rct]. Perkovic V et al., *N Engl J Med* 2024 ("Effects of Semaglutide on Chronic Kidney Disease in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes"; PMID 38785209) randomized **3,533** patients **with T2D AND CKD** (eGFR 50–75 with UACR >300, or eGFR 25–<50 with UACR >100) to semaglutide 1.0 mg weekly vs placebo; the trial was stopped early for efficacy at median **3.4 years**. The primary composite (kidney failure, ≥50% sustained eGFR decline, or death from kidney or CV causes) occurred in **331 vs 410 first events**, HR **0.76 (95% CI 0.66–0.88)** — a **24% relative risk reduction** [16, rct]. This population is again distinct: T2D **with** established CKD. FLOW underpins semaglutide's role in diabetic kidney disease and complements the SGLT2-inhibitor and finerenone renal evidence.

### 3.3 Other / emerging indications

- **MASH/NASH — ESSENCE** [17, rct]. Sanyal AJ et al., *N Engl J Med* 2025 ("Phase 3 Trial of Semaglutide in Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatohepatitis"; PMID 40305708). In the part-1 histology analysis (**800 patients**; biopsy-confirmed MASH, fibrosis F2–F3) semaglutide 2.4 mg produced **steatohepatitis resolution without worsening fibrosis in 62.9% vs 34.3%** (difference 28.7 pts; 95% CI 21.1–36.2; P<0.001) and **fibrosis improvement without worsening MASH in 36.8% vs 22.4%** (difference 14.4 pts; 95% CI 7.5–21.3; P<0.001) [17, rct]. **Status:** semaglutide 2.4 mg received **FDA accelerated approval for MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis in August 2025** — now an approved indication (accelerated; confirmatory data pending), not purely investigational.
- **Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction — STEP-HFpEF** [18, rct]. Kosiborod MN et al., *N Engl J Med* 2023 ("Semaglutide in Patients with Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction and Obesity"; PMID 37622681). In adults with **obesity-related HFpEF**, semaglutide 2.4 mg improved symptoms/physical limitations (KCCQ clinical summary score), body weight, and exercise function vs placebo [18, rct]. **Status: INVESTIGATIONAL/emerging for HFpEF** — symptom and functional endpoints, not a dedicated hard-outcome (hospitalization/mortality) approval; HFpEF is not an approved indication.
- **Alcohol use disorder** [19, rct]. Hendershot CS et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2025 ("Once-Weekly Semaglutide in Adults With Alcohol Use Disorder"; PMID 39937469). A randomized trial reporting reductions in alcohol consumption/craving. **Status: INVESTIGATIONAL/emerging** — early-phase signal, no regulatory indication. (Notably investigator-driven, not a Novo registrational trial — see §4.)
- **Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) — INTEGRITY CORRECTION.** The pivotal OSA GLP-class trial (**SURMOUNT-OSA**) studied **TIRZEPATIDE, a different drug** — it is **NOT** semaglutide and is **not attributable here**. No dedicated semaglutide OSA outcomes trial was found in-corpus; any OSA benefit for semaglutide is at most an unproven, weight-loss-mediated hypothesis and must be treated as **INVESTIGATIONAL/unestablished**. **Do not credit OSA to semaglutide.**

Emerging/investigational tracks (4): MASH (approved, accelerated), HFpEF (investigational), alcohol use disorder (investigational), OSA (no semaglutide-specific trial — unestablished).

### 3.4 Coverage gaps (Section 3)
- The emerging tracks (HFpEF, AUD) rest on single trials each; no independent replication was opened in-corpus.
- No real-world/registry CV or renal corroboration was opened to the cite-only-what-you-open standard (see §4 concentration audit).
- ESSENCE part-1 histology is reported; longer-term clinical-outcome (cirrhosis/decompensation) confirmatory data for MASH were not opened.

## 4. Concentration / sponsor audit

The pivotal semaglutide outcomes evidence is **highly concentrated in a single sponsor — Novo Nordisk**, the molecule's manufacturer. Of the registrational/clinical-program outcome trials cited here, the cardiometabolic primaries (SELECT, SUSTAIN-6, FLOW, ESSENCE, STEP-HFpEF [14–18], plus the SUSTAIN/PIONEER/STEP efficacy trials [6–13]) are Novo Nordisk-funded, with sponsor involvement in design, conduct, analysis, and reporting standard for an industry drug-development program. Counting the registrational primaries, **~76% are Novo-sponsored** (the single-sponsor caveat) — a real source of potential publication, analytic, and endpoint-selection bias. The cardiometabolic semaglutide evidence base is therefore **not independent of the manufacturer**, and that must be stated plainly. The notable in-corpus exception is the **alcohol-use-disorder trial** [19], an investigator/academically-driven study (JAMA Psychiatry) — i.e., the off-label/emerging signals are where non-sponsor groups are active.

**Mitigating factors that partially offset the concentration:** (a) the trials are **large, multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled, with hard adjudicated endpoints** (MACE, kidney failure, biopsy histology), which constrains some bias; (b) **independent regulatory review** (FDA/EMA) scrutinized the primary datasets; and (c) **directional consistency across three different populations** (non-diabetic CVD, T2D, T2D+CKD) and organ systems (heart, kidney, liver) is harder to explain by sponsor bias alone.

**Honest limitation of this retrieval:** within the corpus session no **independent real-world/registry cohort** (e.g., target-trial-emulation EHR studies) was opened to a degree sufficient to cite under the cite-only-what-you-open rule; such corroborating observational evidence is reported to exist but is **not cited here** because it was not verified in-session. The reader should weight that gap when assessing independence.

### 4.1 Coverage gaps (Section 4)
- No independent (non-Novo) registry/RWE cohort was opened; the ~76% single-sponsor figure is the headline integrity caveat.
- The mitigation rests on regulatory review and cross-population consistency, not on demonstrated reproduction by an unaffiliated group for the cardiometabolic endpoints.

## 5. Safety & adverse effects

Semaglutide is among the most extensively characterized GLP-1 RAs in clinical use, with a safety profile defined by large randomized CV/weight trials, two mature FDA labels (Ozempic, Wegovy), and a growing pharmacovigilance record. The dominant story is **gastrointestinal and class-typical**; the most consequential long-term caveats are **weight regain after discontinuation** and **body-composition (lean-mass) change**; and the most prominent label warnings (thyroid C-cell tumors, suicidality) are, on close reading, **considerably more qualified** than their prominence suggests.

### 5.1 Gastrointestinal adverse effects (the dominant signal)

GI adverse events are by far the most common reactions and the principal driver of discontinuation [20, regulatory]. In the Wegovy (2.4 mg) program, the most common reactions (≥5%) were nausea (44%), diarrhea (30%), vomiting (24%), constipation (24%), and abdominal pain (20%), then headache (14%), fatigue (11%), dyspepsia (9%) [20, regulatory]. At lower diabetes doses (Ozempic 0.5–1 mg), GI rates are lower — nausea 15.8–20.3%, vomiting 5–9.2%, diarrhea 8.5–8.8%, constipation 3.1–5.0% [21, regulatory] — illustrating clear dose-dependence.

A 2026 update synthesizing trial and real-world literature confirms GI events as the leading reason patients stop therapy: a meta-analysis of 48 RCTs (27,729 participants) reported an overall GI-AE incidence of 11.6% with nausea most common (21.4%), and a physician survey identified persistent nausea/vomiting as the primary reasons for discontinuation within the first six months [22, meta_analysis]. GI symptoms are mitigated by **slow dose titration**: standard practice begins low with gradual escalation, and persistent symptoms are managed with dose stabilization, reduction, or slower re-titration [22, mechanism_review]. Most GI events are mild-to-moderate, dose-related, and concentrated in the titration window. Because vomiting/diarrhea can cause volume depletion, the labels carry an **acute-kidney-injury caution**: postmarketing AKI has occurred, mostly with GI-driven dehydration; renal function should be monitored in affected patients [20, regulatory].

### 5.2 Weight regain after discontinuation (a defining limitation)

Semaglutide is a chronic therapy, and its weight benefit is largely contingent on continued use. In the **STEP-1 trial extension** (Wilding et al., *Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism* 2022), participants stopped semaglutide 2.4 mg at week 68 and were followed off-treatment to week 120. Over that year off-drug, the semaglutide group regained a mean of **11.6 percentage points** of body weight (vs 1.9 in placebo) — **roughly two-thirds of the weight previously lost** — leaving net loss from baseline of 5.6% (vs 0.1% placebo) at week 120 [23, rct]. Cardiometabolic improvements (waist circumference, HbA1c, blood pressure) largely reverted toward baseline, though HDL, triglycerides, and CRP retained some relative benefit [23, rct]. This regain-on-withdrawal is one of the most important honest caveats for counseling: the drug **manages, rather than cures**, the underlying physiology.

### 5.3 Body-composition / lean-mass loss

Weight loss with semaglutide is not exclusively fat. In STEP-1 body-composition analysis, **lean (fat-free) mass accounted for a substantial fraction of total weight lost** — on the order of ~39–45% (reported figures include ~5.3 kg lean vs ~8.4 kg fat, and a lean-mass contribution of roughly 6.9 kg of ~15.3 kg total) [24, mechanism_review]. Whether this is pathologic muscle loss or expected physiologic remodeling is debated: the proportion is broadly comparable to other forms of substantial weight loss and muscle *quality* may improve, but the absolute lean-mass loss is a genuine concern for older, frail, or sarcopenia-prone patients [24, mechanism_review]. Resistance exercise and adequate protein intake are the standard mitigations (see §8). This caveat should not be buried: rapid, large-magnitude weight loss carries an obligatory lean-mass cost.

### 5.4 Hepatobiliary and pancreatic

**Gallbladder disease.** Cholelithiasis and cholecystitis are increased on therapy. The Wegovy label reports cholelithiasis in 1.6% vs 0.7% (placebo) [20, regulatory], and a meta-analysis of 76 RCTs (>100,000 participants) found GLP-1 RA use raised gallstone/biliary-disease risk by ~37% (RR 1.37), without a significant increase in *serious* biliary disease [22, meta_analysis]. Rapid weight loss itself promotes gallstones, compounding the drug effect.

**Pancreatitis.** Acute pancreatitis, including fatal cases, is a labeled warning across GLP-1 RAs; the label advises discontinuation if pancreatitis is suspected [20, regulatory]. However, contemporary pooled data do not demonstrate a consistent or clinically meaningful increase in pancreatitis risk attributable to GLP-1 RA therapy — the warning is **precautionary and class-based** rather than a confirmed strong signal [22, meta_analysis].

### 5.5 Gastroparesis, ileus, and peri-operative aspiration

Delayed gastric emptying is an on-target effect underlying both the satiety benefit and a cluster of GI risks. Evidence on frank gastroparesis and intestinal obstruction is inconsistent — some analyses report associations (including a ~4-fold obstruction signal), while large population studies find no significant increase [22, mechanism_review]. The most actionable downstream concern is **peri-operative pulmonary aspiration**: delayed emptying can leave residual gastric contents despite standard fasting, raising aspiration risk under general anesthesia or deep sedation. The Wegovy label notes rare postmarketing aspiration reports during anesthesia/sedation [20, regulatory], and the American Society of Anesthesiologists recommends withholding GLP-1 RAs on the day of the procedure for daily formulations and ~one week before surgery for weekly formulations such as semaglutide; some authors argue even these holds may be insufficient given the long half-life and documented retained gastric contents past fasting cutoffs [25, mechanism_review].

### 5.6 Diabetic retinopathy

A diabetic-retinopathy-complications signal emerged in SUSTAIN-6: complications occurred in 3.0% of semaglutide-treated patients vs 1.8% with placebo, rising to 8.2% vs 5.2% among those with pre-existing retinopathy [21, regulatory/rct]. The leading hypothesis attributes this to rapid early glycemic improvement (an "early worsening" phenomenon known from intensive glucose lowering) rather than direct retinal toxicity. Patients with a history of diabetic retinopathy warrant monitoring [21, regulatory].

### 5.7 Thyroid C-cell tumors / medullary thyroid carcinoma — the boxed warning, stated precisely

Both labels carry a **boxed warning** for thyroid C-cell tumors, and its own wording is the key to interpreting it: *"In rodents, semaglutide causes thyroid C-cell tumors at clinically relevant exposures. It is unknown whether [semaglutide] causes thyroid C-cell tumors, including medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), in humans"* [20, 21, regulatory]. The signal is therefore **rodent-derived and has NOT been demonstrated in humans** — the human risk is explicitly stated by FDA as undetermined, not established. Human evidence is reassuring: a 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 RCTs (84,237 participants) found **no statistically significant association** between incretin-based therapy and thyroid cancer (pooled OR 1.52, 95% CI 0.86–2.68; GLP-1 RA subgroup OR 1.46, 0.64–3.35), with certainty rated very low owing to event rarity and limited follow-up **rather than because harm was shown** [26, meta_analysis]. The honest framing: a real, dose-dependent **rodent** C-cell tumorigenesis signal drives a precautionary boxed warning and contraindication, but **no human C-cell/MTC excess has been demonstrated**; long-term surveillance remains warranted.

### 5.8 Suicidality / self-harm — investigated, not confirmed

A suicidality signal was raised in 2023, prompting regulatory review. The precise status: investigation has **not confirmed a causal association**. A real-world emulation cohort (Wang et al., *Nature Medicine* 2024) compared semaglutide with non-GLP-1 anti-obesity and anti-diabetes medications and found **lower**, not higher, associations with suicidal ideation: in overweight/obesity, incident-ideation HR 0.27 (95% CI 0.20–0.36) and recurrent HR 0.44 (0.32–0.60); in T2D, incident HR 0.36 (0.25–0.53) and recurrent HR 0.51 (0.31–0.83); the authors conclude the data do not support higher suicidal-ideation risk [27, cohort]. This aligns with regulators not having established causation. The defensible statement: a suicidality signal was investigated; available controlled and pharmacovigilance evidence has **not confirmed a causal link** — **not** "semaglutide causes suicidality." Standard clinical vigilance for new/worsening mood symptoms remains appropriate.

### 5.9 Contraindications, monitoring, and stopping criteria

**Contraindications** (both labels): personal or family history of **medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC)**; **Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2)**; and prior serious hypersensitivity to semaglutide or any excipient [20, 21, regulatory]. **Pregnancy** is a further important caution — semaglutide is not recommended in pregnancy (animal reproductive toxicity; weight loss offers no benefit to a pregnancy), and the long half-life argues for discontinuation well before a planned conception.

**Monitoring:** renal function during significant GI-driven volume loss; gallbladder symptoms; signs of pancreatitis; retinal status in patients with prior diabetic retinopathy; and, when co-administered with insulin or sulfonylureas, hypoglycemia (semaglutide alone is not a major hypoglycemia driver) [20, 21, regulatory]. Objective labs a clinician would track include **HbA1c** (glycemic efficacy/safety in T2D), **eGFR + serum creatinine** (renal-function/AKI monitoring), and **lipase** if pancreatitis is suspected — standard assays, not semaglutide-specific surrogates. **Stopping criteria:** discontinue promptly if pancreatitis is suspected; hold per ASA guidance before anesthesia; discontinue for serious hypersensitivity [20, 25, regulatory/mechanism_review].

### 5.10 Bottom line

The safety burden is dominated by titration-window GI effects (manageable, dose-related, the main discontinuation driver). The most important honest caveats are **weight regain on cessation** (~two-thirds within a year) and **lean-mass loss**. Gallbladder disease is modestly increased; pancreatitis, gastroparesis/ileus, and diabetic retinopathy are real but qualified concerns; peri-operative aspiration is an actionable, mechanism-driven risk. The two most alarming-sounding warnings are the most qualified on human evidence: the **thyroid C-cell boxed warning is rodent-based and not demonstrated in humans**, and the **suicidality signal has been investigated but not confirmed**.

### 5.11 Coverage gaps (Section 5)
- AE rates derive from the Wegovy/Ozempic labels [20,21] and pooled meta-analyses [22,26]; head-to-head AE comparisons vs tirzepatide were not opened in-corpus.
- Long-term (multi-year) carcinogenicity and pancreatitis data remain limited by event rarity and follow-up duration (acknowledged in [26]).
- The suicidality null rests primarily on one large RWE cohort [27]; it is reassuring but not a definitive exclusion.

## 6. Regulatory & sport status

Semaglutide is marketed under three brands with three distinct, separately-approved indications and dosing schemes: **Ozempic** (SC, T2D), **Rybelsus** (oral, T2D), and **Wegovy** (SC 2.4 mg, chronic weight management). The diabetes approvals (Ozempic, Rybelsus) and the obesity approval (Wegovy) are **separate FDA/EMA authorizations with separate maximum doses** — Ozempic up to 2 mg once weekly for glycemic control, Wegovy titrated to 2.4 mg once weekly for weight management. They are **not interchangeable products**.

### 6.1 FDA approvals (United States)

- **Ozempic (SC) — T2D.** First FDA-approved **December 5, 2017** (NDA 209637) as an adjunct to diet and exercise for glycemic control in adults with T2D; the first once-weekly GLP-1 RA with a semaglutide backbone in the U.S. [28, 31, regulatory]. The current label lists three indications: T2D glycemic control; **MACE reduction in adults with T2D and established CVD** (post-SUSTAIN-6); and **reduction of sustained eGFR decline, ESKD, and CV death in adults with T2D and CKD** (post-FLOW) [28, regulatory].
- **Rybelsus (oral) — T2D.** FDA-approved **September 2019** as an adjunct for glycemic control in adults with T2D; the **first oral GLP-1 RA** [31, 32, regulatory]. Taken once daily; the SNAC co-formulation enables oral peptide delivery.
- **Wegovy (SC 2.4 mg) — chronic weight management.** FDA-approved **June 2021** for chronic weight management [31, regulatory]; the label indicates it "to reduce excess body weight and maintain weight reduction long term" in adults and pediatric patients ≥12 with obesity (or overweight adults with ≥1 weight-related comorbidity), and — separately added — **CV risk reduction** in adults with established CV disease and either obesity or overweight [29, regulatory]. Maintenance dose **2.4 mg SC once weekly** [29, regulatory].
- **Wegovy cardiovascular indication (post-SELECT).** On **March 8, 2024**, FDA approved the CV-risk-reduction indication for Wegovy — the first weight-loss medication also approved to reduce CV death, heart attack, and stroke in adults with established CVD and obesity/overweight; the approval rested on SELECT (>17,600 participants; MACE 6.5% vs 8.0%) [30, regulatory]. This indication is in a population **without** T2D, distinguishing it from Ozempic's diabetes-tied CV indication.
- **MASH (accelerated).** Semaglutide 2.4 mg received **FDA accelerated approval for MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis in August 2025** (post-ESSENCE; §3.3) [17, rct] — now an approved indication.

Key regulatory point: **obesity (Wegovy) and T2D (Ozempic/Rybelsus) are governed by separate FDA approvals, separate labels, and separate maximum doses** [28, 29, regulatory].

### 6.2 EMA approvals (European Union)

All three brands are centrally authorized across the EU [33–35, regulatory]:
1. **Ozempic** — EU marketing authorization **8 February 2018**; T2D as an adjunct to diet and exercise, monotherapy when metformin is inappropriate or in combination [34, regulatory].
2. **Rybelsus** — EU marketing authorization **3 April 2020**; the first oral GLP-1 RA authorized in the EU; T2D glycemic control [32, regulatory].
3. **Wegovy** — EU marketing authorization **6 January 2022**; weight management in adults (BMI ≥30, or ≥27 with comorbidity) and adolescents ≥12 with obesity and body weight >60 kg [35, regulatory].

### 6.3 The shortage and compounded semaglutide (the regulatory arc)

Surging 2022–2024 demand outstripped supply, and FDA placed **semaglutide injection on its Drug Shortage list**. Under U.S. law, a drug's presence on that list opens the door to compounding: **503A** pharmacies and **503B** outsourcing facilities may compound copies of an FDA-approved drug in shortage. This drove a large compounded-semaglutide market during the shortage years [36, regulatory].

That window then closed. FDA determined the **semaglutide injection shortage was resolved**, per a decision memorandum dated **February 21, 2025** [36, 38, regulatory]. Because compounding had become widespread, FDA announced **enforcement-discretion transition periods**: no action against **503A** compounders for shortage-dependent violations until **April 22, 2025**, and a longer runway for **503B** facilities until **May 22, 2025** [36, regulatory]. After those dates the legal basis for compounding semaglutide as a shortage drug fell away. FDA has further **proposed to exclude semaglutide (with tirzepatide and liraglutide) from the 503B bulk-substances list**, which would foreclose a separate compounding pathway [37, regulatory].

It is important not to overstate current legality: routine large-scale compounding of semaglutide as a shortage substitute is **no longer permitted** now that the shortage is resolved. FDA has issued sustained warnings about non-FDA-approved semaglutide products: compounded versions "do not undergo FDA premarket review for safety, quality or effectiveness"; FDA identified "serious concerns… including dosing errors, use of unapproved **salt forms** and adverse events—some requiring hospitalization" [37, regulatory]. The salt forms — **semaglutide sodium** and **semaglutide acetate** — are different chemical entities from the **semaglutide base** in approved products and are **not** the approved active ingredient [37, regulatory]. Separately, FDA warned consumers about **counterfeit Ozempic** (1 mg) found in the legitimate U.S. supply chain, seizing thousands of units [39, regulatory]. Net as of mid-2026: only Novo Nordisk's FDA-approved Ozempic/Rybelsus/Wegovy are sanctioned; compounded, salt-form, and counterfeit products carry explicit FDA safety warnings and, post-shortage, largely lack a lawful compounding basis.

### 6.4 WADA / sport status

**The load-bearing, primary-anchored fact: semaglutide and GLP-1 receptor agonists are NOT on the WADA Prohibited List** — not prohibited in or out of competition [40, regulatory]. This was verified directly against the U.S. Anti-Doping Agency's published Prohibited List page (USADA is a WADA-signatory NADO adopting the WADA International Standard), which contains **no listing of semaglutide or GLP-1 receptor agonists** among prohibited substances or methods [40, regulatory]. Practical takeaway: semaglutide is **permitted** in sport and does **not** require a Therapeutic Use Exemption on prohibited-list grounds.

Separately, **GLP-1 weight-loss agonists (semaglutide and tirzepatide) are reportedly being added to the WADA Monitoring Program for 2026** [41, secondary]. This Monitoring-Program statement rests on **secondary reporting** (insidethegames.biz coverage of the 2026 list) and **could not be confirmed against the wada-ama.org primary in-corpus** — the official 2026 Monitoring Program document and the Summary of Major Modifications PDFs would not render on repeated attempts [42, regulatory(primary)]. The USADA page references a separate "Monitoring Program – 2026 WADA Prohibited List" resource as existing but does **not** enumerate its contents, so it does not independently confirm the GLP-1 entry [40, regulatory]. **This claim is therefore presented as reported, not primary-verified.** The Monitoring Program is explicitly **not** a prohibition: WADA monitors substances to detect potential-misuse patterns; a finding for a monitored substance is **not** an anti-doping rule violation. The bottom line — semaglutide is permitted in sport today — is unaffected by whether the monitoring-program addition is confirmed, but should be re-verified against each annual WADA list directly from wada-ama.org once it renders.

### 6.5 Cost / access (qualitative)

In the U.S., branded semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) historically carried a high list price — roughly **~$1,300/month for Wegovy and ~$968–1,000/month for Ozempic** without insurance (manufacturer list prices, 2025) [43, vendor_label] — and insurance coverage for the **obesity** indication has been substantially more restrictive than for the **diabetes** indication, a coverage gap that fueled cash-pay demand and, during the shortage, the compounded market. Compounded semaglutide, during its availability window, was dramatically cheaper (commonly cited ~$150–500/month) [43, vendor_label], which drove its popularity; that cost gap is now largely moot for legal supply following the wind-down. With the shortage resolved and compounding curtailed, access has shifted back toward branded supply and manufacturer cash-pay/self-pay programs (e.g., NovoCare). Affordability and payer coverage (especially for weight management) remain the dominant practical access barriers rather than legal availability.

### 6.6 Coverage gaps (Section 6)
- The WADA 2026 Monitoring-Program addition is **secondary-reported only**; the wada-ama.org primary [42] did not render in-corpus and must be re-checked.
- The FDA shortage/compounding documents [36–39] were retrieved via fda.gov; some sub-pages returned partial content and dates rest on the decision memorandum plus corroborating FDA statements.
- Cost figures are vendor/list-price aggregations [43] (qualitative pricing context only — not efficacy or AE evidence).

## 7. Why evidence_tier A / risk_tier medium

### 7.1 Why evidence_tier A

Semaglutide is the **first A-tier entry of this sweep**, and the grade is earned:
1. **Multiple large Phase-3 RCTs** across two separately-powered programs — SUSTAIN/PIONEER (glycemic, T2D) and STEP (weight, largely non-diabetic) — with dose-response consistency and head-to-head superiority over comparator GLP-1 RAs [6–13].
2. **Hard, adjudicated outcome trials**: cardiovascular (SELECT, HR 0.80, ~17,600 patients [14]; SUSTAIN-6 [15]) and renal (FLOW, HR 0.76 [16]) — MACE, kidney failure, and biopsy histology are hard endpoints, not surrogates.
3. **FDA and EMA approval** across T2D, obesity, CV-risk-reduction, MASH (accelerated), and renal indications [28–35].
4. **Directional consistency across three distinct populations** (non-diabetic CVD, T2D, T2D+CKD) and organ systems (heart, kidney, liver).

The **one structural caveat** keeping the A-tier honest is **single-sponsor concentration** (~76% Novo-funded primaries; §4), partially offset by adjudicated endpoints, double-blind placebo control, and independent regulatory review — but not eliminated, and not corroborated in-corpus by an independent RWE cohort.

### 7.2 Why risk_tier medium (not low)

Despite approval and characterization, semaglutide is **doctor-required** and lands at medium — not low — because the adverse-effect profile is **real and clinically consequential**, not theoretical:
- **GI AEs** are common and the main discontinuation driver (§5.1).
- **Weight regain on discontinuation** (~two-thirds within a year, §5.2) and **lean-mass loss** (§5.3) are defining limitations of the weight indication.
- **Gallbladder disease** is modestly increased; **pancreatitis** is a labeled (precautionary) warning; **peri-operative aspiration** is an actionable mechanism-driven risk (§5.4–5.5).
- The **rodent thyroid-C-cell boxed warning** (not human-demonstrated, §5.7) and the contraindications (MTC/MEN2/pregnancy) keep it firmly clinician-managed.
- The **suicidality signal** (investigated, not confirmed, §5.8) does not raise the tier but warrants standard vigilance.

What keeps it at **medium rather than high**: it is approved, extensively studied, has hard-endpoint benefit, low intrinsic hypoglycemia risk, and a safety profile that is largely manageable with titration and monitoring. **A = high-evidence; medium = real, manageable, doctor-supervised risk.**

## 8. Prescribing-practice + non-English summary

This section is **brief** — the authoritative titration detail lives in the FDA labels [1, 3, 21, 29, 44, 45]; compounding/adjunct items are practice/regulatory context and **do not ground efficacy or AE-rate claims**, which live in §2/§3/§5 as Tier-1.

### 8.1 Dosing & titration (label-driven)

All three formulations share one design logic: a **low, sub-therapeutic starting dose escalated slowly** to mitigate GI adverse reactions, which cluster during escalation.

- **Ozempic (SC, T2D).** Initiate **0.25 mg once weekly for 4 weeks** (an initiation, not therapeutic, dose), then **0.5 mg** weekly; if more control is needed after ≥4 weeks, **1 mg**, then a maximum **2 mg** weekly. The label directs: "Follow the dosage escalation below to reduce the risk of gastrointestinal adverse reactions" [44, regulatory].
- **Wegovy (SC, weight management).** A fixed 16-week escalation to 2.4 mg maintenance: **0.25 mg (wk 1–4) → 0.5 mg (wk 5–8) → 1 mg (wk 9–12) → 1.7 mg (wk 13–16) → 2.4 mg from wk 17** [45, regulatory]. The label gives explicit tolerability guidance: "If patients do not tolerate a dose during dosage escalation, consider delaying dosage escalation for 4 weeks" [45, regulatory] — formalizing the common practice of "pausing" at a tolerated dose.
- **Rybelsus (oral, T2D).** Start **3 mg once daily for 30 days** (explicitly stated ineffective for glycemic control — used only to acclimate the GI tract), then **7 mg** daily; after ≥30 more days, **14 mg** daily if more control is needed [3, regulatory]. Must be taken on an empty stomach with ≤4 oz plain water, ≥30 minutes before the first food, drink, or other oral medication, because absorption depends on the co-formulated SNAC and is degraded by food/fluid [3, regulatory].

The recurring theme — a non-therapeutic starting dose and stepwise 4-week (or 30-day) escalation — is the single most clinically important practitioner-facing fact and is fully label-driven, not improvised.

### 8.2 Compounded / access landscape

During the 2022–2024 shortage, compounded semaglutide was widely available via telehealth/compounding pharmacies far below brand price; the regulatory status has since materially changed (full arc in §6.3). FDA declared the injection shortage **resolved February 21, 2025**, triggering a wind-down with phased enforcement deadlines (503A ~April 22, 2025; 503B ~May 22, 2025) [36, 46, regulatory]. (Tirzepatide's shortage had resolved earlier, December 2024.) FDA and the manufacturer repeatedly warned that some compounders used **salt forms — semaglutide sodium or acetate — not the same active ingredient** as the base in approved products [46, regulatory], and on **July 26, 2024** FDA issued an alert on **dosing errors with compounded injectable semaglutide**, largely because patients self-measure/self-draw from multi-dose vials, producing 10-fold and other overdoses [47, regulatory]. A separate telehealth trend of deliberate sub-label **"microdosing"** (fractions of the 0.25 mg starting dose) emerged; it is off-label and non-evidence-based with no FDA-recognized efficacy or safety data — **report only as a practice phenomenon, not a validated protocol** [practitioner_protocol; trend only].

### 8.3 Muscle-preservation convention

Substantial weight loss on semaglutide includes lean-mass loss alongside fat (the STEP-1 DXA substudy: greater fat-mass than lean-mass reduction, improving the fat-free proportion, but absolute lean mass still declined [24, mechanism_review]). The **consistent practitioner convention** is to pair semaglutide with **(a) resistance/strength training and (b) elevated dietary protein** — commonly framed as roughly **1.2–1.6 g protein/kg/day with progressive resistance training** during the weight-loss phase [practitioner_protocol; convention]. This is a physiology-aligned, broadly-recommended convention, but the **precise lean-mass-sparing magnitude attributable to it in semaglutide users is not established by Tier-1 trial evidence** and must not be reported as a quantified efficacy outcome.

### 8.4 Non-English literature

Semaglutide's evidence base is overwhelmingly global and **English-published**, even for trials run entirely outside English-speaking countries. The major programs (SUSTAIN, STEP, PIONEER) are international Novo Nordisk trials whose primary results appear in English-language journals (NEJM, Lancet, JAMA, Diabetes Care, Diabetologia, Diabetes Obesity & Metabolism). Dedicated **China-population trials** exist (e.g., PIONEER 11 and PIONEER 12 in predominantly Chinese participants with T2D), but their primary results were published **in English** — e.g., in *Diabetologia* and *Diabetes Obesity & Metabolism* — not in Chinese-language journals [48, regulatory-adjacent]. SUSTAIN China analyses likewise appear in English venues. **Honest result: no distinct non-English-language primary literature was identified that adds findings beyond the English corpus.** Where trials were conducted in non-English-speaking populations (notably China and Japan), the authoritative primaries are in English; secondary/clinical-practice commentary exists in other languages but is derivative and was not relied upon. There is **no separate non-English primary evidence stream to integrate** for semaglutide.

### 8.5 Coverage gaps (Section 8)
- Titration schedules are label-authoritative [3, 44, 45]; the microdosing and muscle-preservation items are convention/trend, not Tier-1.
- The non-English survey found no distinct primary stream; a proprietary-language trial registered under a non-English alias could in principle be missed, but the China/Japan primaries publishing in English makes a hidden distinct stream unlikely.
- Compounded-landscape dose-error and salt-form items are regulatory-context, not AE-rate evidence.
