# Epitalon (Synthetic Pineal Tetrapeptide, AEDG)

## Metadata

- **class:** peptide (synthetic pineal-derived tetrapeptide, peptide bioregulator)
- **evidence_tier:** D — The human clinical record consists entirely of single-institution, open-label, non-randomized Khavinson-group studies using the crude bovine extract Epithalamin rather than the synthetic AEDG tetrapeptide sold commercially as epitalon. The telomerase/telomere-elongation mechanism has in-vitro support from two independent laboratories (Khavinson 2003; Al-Dulaimi et al. 2025 at Brunel), though those two studies partially disagree on mechanism: Brunel found hTERT downregulated (not upregulated) in cancer cell lines, with ALT activation instead. ## Summary

Epitalon (CAS 307297-39-8, free peptide; 307297-40-1, acetate salt) is a synthetic tetrapeptide with the amino acid sequence **Ala-Glu-Asp-Gly (AEDG)**, molecular weight approximately 390.35 Da. It is not a naturally occurring compound but rather a synthetic analog derived from the sequence analysis of **Epithalamin** — a heterogeneous low-molecular-weight polypeptide fraction extracted from bovine pineal gland tissue. The two names are routinely conflated in the secondary literature and in grey-market commerce, yet they describe chemically distinct preparations with distinct regulatory profiles and distinct clinical evidence records. Every major human clinical outcome claimed for the compound class — reduced mortality, improved survival in coronary disease, restored melatonin circadian rhythms — was generated using the crude bovine extract Epithalamin, not the synthetic AEDG tetrapeptide that circulates today on the grey market as "epitalon." This extract-versus-synthetic confound is the most consequential interpretive point in the entire literature and must be held in view throughout any evaluation of efficacy claims.

Epitalon was developed by **Vladimir Khavinson** and colleagues at the **St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology** (North-Western Division of the Russian Academy of Medical Sciences), where the research program began in the 1970s and 1980s within the Soviet military medicine infrastructure. The founding conceptual framework — that aging reflects declining tissue-specific synthesis of short organ-specific peptide regulators, and that synthetic analogs of these peptides can restore the organ's gene-expression set-point — is Khavinson's "peptide bioregulator" (cytomedine) theory. Epitalon represents the synthetic tetrapeptide derived from that research program's pineal gland arm.

**Evidence reality — the three load-bearing honest points:**

**First: The telomerase/telomere claim has genuine but narrow in-vitro support — and the two independent laboratories partially disagree on mechanism.** Khavinson, Bondarev, and Butyugov (2003) reported induction of hTERT expression, telomerase enzymatic activity, and telomere elongation in telomerase-negative human fetal fibroblast cultures [2, in_vitro]. This single-group finding went unreplicated for over two decades. The first substantive independent replication arrived in September 2025, when Al-Dulaimi, Thomas, Matta, and Roberts at Brunel University London published in *Biogerontology* a study confirming dose-dependent telomere length extension across six human cell lines [3, in_vitro]. The Brunel study is genuine independent replication for the telomere-elongation observation in normal cells — but it introduced a mechanistic discrepancy that is not merely a complexity: in cancer cell lines, hTERT was downregulated (not upregulated), and elongation proceeded via Alternative Lengthening of Telomeres (ALT) instead of canonical telomerase activation. The two independent laboratories thus agree on the outcome (telomere elongation) in normal cells and disagree on mechanism in cancer cells — a partial confirmation, not a straightforward corroboration. This distinction matters for oncogenic-safety reasoning. Both studies are in-vitro only. No controlled animal experiment by an independent group has measured telomere length change. No blinded human trial has measured blood or tissue telomere length before and after epitalon administration. The translation from cell-culture telomerase activation to meaningful in-vivo telomere extension is entirely unestablished.

**Second: The human longevity and mortality claims rest entirely on non-randomized Khavinson-group studies using the wrong compound.** The most-cited human mortality data — a 1.6–4.1-fold reduction in mortality in 266 elderly participants over 6–8 years — comes from an open-label, non-blinded Khavinson-institute cohort with historical and population controls and no independent audit [8, open_label]. The most methodologically developed human study is Korkushko et al. 2011, described as a "randomized comparative study" in 79 elderly coronary patients followed 15 years [10, rct]; this is the most rigorous piece of human data in the corpus, but it remains single-site, Khavinson-affiliated, published in a Russian-translated journal, and unreplicated. Crucially, both studies used **Epithalamin** administered intramuscularly — not the synthetic AEDG tetrapeptide. No clinical outcome study of synthetic epitalon has been conducted. No Western or Asian independent group has attempted to replicate any of the human mortality or longevity findings. Zero Phase 2 or Phase 3 controlled efficacy trials of synthetic epitalon (AEDG) appear in any clinical trial registry as of mid-2026.

**Third: Research concentration exceeds ≥80% from a single institutional cluster, and the telomerase-oncogenic tension is biologically real.** Of approximately 40–50 identifiable primary efficacy papers in the epitalon and Epithalamin literature, an estimated ≥80% originate from Khavinson's St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology and its direct St. Petersburg collaborators at the N.N. Petrov Research Institute of Oncology (the Anisimov group). This exceeds any reasonable threshold for single-group evidentiary concentration. The two genuinely independent primary-replication studies that exist — Al-Dulaimi et al. 2025 (Brunel, telomerase/telomere in human cell lines) and Yue et al. 2022 (Shanxi/Chinese Academy of Sciences/University of Missouri, antioxidant protection of mouse oocytes) — are both in-vitro, both on narrow mechanistic endpoints, and neither tests the longevity, anti-cancer, melatonin-restoration, or clinical outcomes that constitute the headline claims. Meanwhile, the theoretical oncogenic tension is substantive: telomerase reactivation is a hallmark of malignant transformation; the same mechanism that prevents replicative senescence in aged somatic cells is the mechanism that enables cancer cell immortality. The 2025 Brunel finding that cancer cell lines showed ALT-pathway activation rather than conventional telomerase-based elongation deepens rather than resolves this concern.

**Regulatory and sourcing context:** Epitalon is FDA-unapproved. It was removed from the FDA 503A Category 2 list effective April 15, 2026 (Federal Register 91 FR 20465), but this removal does not authorize compounding — it moves the substance to pending PCAC review scheduled July 23–24, 2026. Russian MoH registration applies to Epithalamin only, not to the synthetic tetrapeptide. WADA S0 captures epitalon by mechanism — it requires no individual listing to be prohibited in sanctioned sport. Grey-market epitalon circulates as an unregulated research chemical with unverifiable purity, uncertain stereoisomeric composition, and no chain-of-custody guarantees. The Epithalamin/Epitalon naming confusion in commerce means consumers cannot reliably identify which preparation they are obtaining.

**Khavinson's COI is structural and undisclosed in his papers:** as the inventor, primary researcher, institute director, and developer of commercial Epithalamin pharmaceuticals and branded peptide supplement lines, he occupies a triple-conflict position that Western journal COI policies would require mandatory disclosure of — yet Khavinson-group papers routinely state "no conflict of interest."

The honest bottom line on epitalon is this: it is a Khavinson-single-group, mostly-Russian, in-vitro-telomerase-plus-open-label-longevity compound with a real oncogenic-mechanism tension, an unresolved extract-versus-synthetic confound, an ≥80% single-institution evidence base, and no independent replication of any human clinical outcome. The in-vitro telomerase biology is real at the cell level — with the important qualification that the only independent replication (Brunel 2025) shows hTERT downregulated in cancer cells, not the simple "telomerase activator" picture. Everything above the cell level is either uninvestigated or supported only by the unconflicted work of a single research group whose claims have remained extraordinary and unverified for over four decades. That research group operates within a "peptide bioregulator" (cytomedine) theoretical framework that is not a recognized paradigm in Western gerontology — it has not been validated, subjected to paradigm-level independent review, or adopted by any Western regulatory or academic body. Claims about epitalon must be read in that epistemological context, not merely as individual compound findings.

## 1. Identity, Nomenclature, and Origin

### 1.1 Chemical identity

Epitalon (also spelled Epithalon) is the synthetic tetrapeptide Ala-Glu-Asp-Gly, abbreviated AEDG from the one-letter amino acid codes. Its CAS registry numbers are 307297-39-8 for the free peptide and 307297-40-1 for the acetate salt form used in most compounded preparations. Molecular weight is approximately 390.35 Da. At this size, the peptide falls below the threshold of most conventional small-molecule drugs but is orders of magnitude smaller than therapeutic proteins. The compound exists in eight possible stereoisomeric forms, corresponding to the L/D configurations at each of the four amino acid positions; only the all-L (natural) configuration has been studied in any published research, and the PK and safety profiles of all other stereoisomers are entirely uncharacterized.

The parenthetical confusion that runs through the secondary literature deserves explicit handling. **Epithalamin** is a chemically heterogeneous crude polypeptide fraction extracted from bovine pineal gland tissue. It contains multiple peptide fragments of varying sequence and molecular weight. Epithalamin is the preparation actually used in the Khavinson group's human clinical programs and is the compound registered with the Russian Ministry of Health. **Epitalon (AEDG)** is a defined synthetic tetrapeptide developed to reproduce the proposed biological activity of Epithalamin in a chemically pure, reproducible form. These two preparations are not interchangeable, do not carry the same regulatory status, and do not have equivalent clinical evidence records. Any argument that the human clinical data for Epithalamin supports the efficacy of synthetic Epitalon involves an inferential step — from crude extract to synthetic tetrapeptide — that has not been formally validated in any controlled experiment.

### 1.2 Origin and the peptide bioregulator framework

The conceptual origins of epitalon lie in a Soviet-era military medicine research program initiated at the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology under Khavinson's leadership beginning in the 1970s. The founding hypothesis was that organ-specific short peptides (typically 2–4 amino acids) extracted from target tissues function as gene-expression modulators in an age-dependent, tissue-specific manner, and that delivering these peptides exogenously can restore the declining expression of tissue-specific genes associated with aging. The "peptide bioregulator" framework generated a class of compounds Khavinson terms "cytomedines" — derived from bovine tissue extracts of various organs including pineal gland, thymus, liver, and cerebral cortex. Epithalamin is the pineal gland member of this family.

The development of synthetic analogs like Epitalon was a subsequent step aimed at addressing the chemical consistency and intellectual property limitations of crude tissue extracts. By identifying the putative minimal active tetrapeptide sequence within the Epithalamin fraction — through sequence analysis rather than structure-activity optimization in the Western pharmaceutical sense — the Khavinson group produced a chemically defined compound that could be synthesized reproducibly. The entire subsequent literature builds on this derivation claim: that the biological activity of Epithalamin resides meaningfully in the AEDG tetrapeptide sequence. This foundational claim has not been independently validated by a controlled head-to-head comparison of equivalent doses of Epithalamin versus synthetic AEDG.

### 1.3 The Polish independent synthesis review (2025)

Araj, Brzezik, Mądra-Gackowska, and Szeleszczuk from the Medical University of Warsaw and Nicolaus Copernicus University, Torun published a comprehensive narrative review of epitalon in *International Journal of Molecular Sciences* (2025) [5, mechanism_review]. This is currently the most substantive secondary synthesis of epitalon's properties by authors with no Khavinson co-authorship and no declared COI. The review synthesizes 25 years of literature and endorses antioxidant, neuroprotective, anti-aging, and antimutagenic properties while explicitly acknowledging that "the quantity of physico-chemical and structural investigations of this peptide remains quite limited" and that uncertainty persists about whether the proposed molecular mechanisms are the only pathways through which the compound operates. The Araj et al. review functions as a secondary synthesis and endorsement of the Khavinson-group claims rather than an independent experimental replication; it generates no new primary data. Its existence does, however, confirm that researchers outside the Khavinson ecosystem regard the published literature as worth synthesizing — while also highlighting the data gaps the synthesis inherits.

## 2. Proposed Molecular Mechanisms

### 2.1 Telomerase activation and telomere elongation

The mechanism most prominently cited in epitalon marketing and public discourse is induction of telomerase activity leading to telomere elongation in somatic cells. The foundational report is a 2003 paper from Khavinson, Bondarev, and Butyugov at the St. Petersburg Institute, published in the *Bulletin of Experimental Biology and Medicine* — a Russian-language journal translated into English, with limited Western peer-review infrastructure [2, in_vitro]. They introduced the Epithalon peptide into cultures of telomerase-negative human fetal fibroblasts and reported expression of hTERT (the catalytic subunit of telomerase), detectable telomerase enzymatic activity, and telomere elongation. A companion 2004 study by the same group reported that the peptide enabled human somatic cells to surpass their normal replicative division limit — the Hayflick limit — in culture, extending the finding from telomerase-activity induction to functional replication-capacity rescue [23, in_vitro]. The Hayflick limit finding is conceptually significant because it suggests epitalon may restore proliferative capacity in cells that have entered replicative senescence, not merely activate telomerase enzymatically — though, as with the telomerase activation claim, the translational gap from cultured fetal fibroblasts to aged somatic tissue in a living organism remains unbridged by any controlled in-vivo experiment. This body of work is single-institution and describes in-vitro experiments in a cell type — fetal fibroblasts — that is not representative of somatic cells in an aged organism. Telomerase-negative cell lines can be unusually responsive to telomerase-activating stimuli precisely because they lack the normal regulatory machinery that suppresses telomerase in differentiated adult cells. The clinical relevance of reactivating telomerase in a fetal fibroblast culture to healthy human aging was never addressed by the original authors.

This finding received no independent replication for over two decades. The first substantive independent test came from Al-Dulaimi, Thomas, Matta, and Roberts at Brunel University London's Centre for Genome Engineering and Maintenance, in collaboration with Royal Brompton Hospital, published in *Biogerontology* in September 2025 [3, in_vitro]. Their study used six human cell lines: two breast cancer lines (21NT and BT474), two normal lines (fibroblast IBR.3 and mammary epithelial HMEC), one ALT-positive osteosarcoma line (U2OS), and one telomerase-expressing prostate cancer line (PC3-hTERT). Treatment at 0.5–1 µg/mL for four days produced dose-dependent telomere length extension across cell types. In normal fibroblasts and epithelial cells, the elongation proceeded via conventional hTERT upregulation — consistent with the original Khavinson claim. In cancer cell lines (21NT and BT474), however, the mechanism was entirely different: ALT (Alternative Lengthening of Telomeres) pathway activation was detected via increased PML nuclear body formation, with hTERT appearing downregulated in the cancer lines even as their telomeres lengthened. Normal cells required a longer exposure period (three weeks at 1 µg/mL) to achieve significant elongation compared to the shorter 4-day protocol for cancer cells.

A published erratum to the Brunel paper appeared in November 2025 (PMID 41240216) clarifying figure elements but not altering conclusions. None of the Brunel authors have affiliation with the Khavinson group, no Russian funding is disclosed, and the study was conducted entirely independently.

The scientific significance of the Brunel study is real: it confirms that the in-vitro telomere-elongation biology is not a single-group artifact. But the mechanistic finding that cancer cells activate ALT rather than telomerase — and do so after only four days of treatment at concentrations that have no established in-vivo equivalent — is a complexity absent from every version of the "epitalon activates telomerase and extends telomeres" narrative that circulates in longevity communities. ALT pathway activation is associated with aggressive tumor biology: it is the mechanism by which tumors lacking canonical telomerase reactivation achieve replicative immortality, and its presence in treated cancer lines at pharmacological epitalon concentrations is precisely the kind of finding that demands long-term carcinogenicity investigation before any human dosing.

To be precise about what the telomerase evidence does and does not establish: in normal human cells in culture, epitalon upregulates hTERT and extends telomeres. This is a real cell-culture observation with one independent replication. In cancer cells, the picture is mechanistically distinct — Brunel found hTERT downregulated while ALT is activated, meaning the two laboratories reached partially different mechanistic conclusions depending on cell type, not a simple mutual confirmation. The translation from cell-culture telomerase activation to meaningful telomere extension in the tissues of a living, aging organism involves at minimum: (a) pharmacokinetic delivery of the peptide to aged somatic cells at active concentrations (uncharacterized in vivo); (b) the same hTERT-upregulation response occurring in differentiated, non-dividing, and slowly dividing somatic cell types rather than in the actively proliferating fetal fibroblasts and cell-line models studied; and (c) sufficient sustained telomere elongation at the population level to materially alter the aging trajectory of tissues. None of these translational steps has been demonstrated. No controlled animal study by an independent group has measured telomere length before and after epitalon treatment. No human trial of any design has measured blood or tissue telomere length in response to epitalon. The compound's popular reputation as a "telomerase activator that extends human telomeres and lifespan" exceeds what the evidence can sustain by at least two full translational levels.

### 2.2 Pineal peptide framing and melatonin restoration

Epitalon's derivation from pineal gland tissue motivates a distinct mechanism claim: that the compound restores melatonin secretion lost in aging. The pineal gland's production of melatonin declines progressively with age, and reduced melatonin is associated with disrupted circadian rhythm, impaired sleep architecture, and loss of the nocturnal melatonin peak that regulates numerous downstream endocrine functions. The peptide bioregulator theory proposes that AEDG acts on pineal-specific transcriptional elements to restore melatonin biosynthetic capacity.

The published evidence for this claim is Korkushko et al. (2007), a study in aged rhesus monkeys (Macaca mulatta) and elderly humans with "pineal gland functional insufficiency," published in *Advances in Gerontology* — a Russian-language specialty journal edited within the bioregulator school [4, open_label]. The paper reported recovery of the nocturnal melatonin peak and normalization of the circadian melatonin rhythm in plasma, along with reductions in basal glucose and insulin in treated old monkeys. Khavinson is the fourth of eight authors. The study lacks a placebo control arm; blinding details are not reported in the abstract; the sample size in the human component is not specified. Tier 3 is the honest evidence-quality assignment. No English-language, independently conducted, randomized trial has replicated circadian melatonin normalization in either primates or humans for either Epithalamin or synthetic Epitalon.

Khavinson's 2002 comprehensive review in *Neuro Endocrinol Lett* summarizes the theoretical basis and collects rodent, primate, and human observations — all originating from the St. Petersburg group — for melatonin restoration alongside other anti-aging endpoint claims [1, mechanism_review]. The review covers lifespan extension in SHR rats and Drosophila, reduced tumor incidence, restored circadian rhythmicity, and antioxidant effects. All primary data cited in that review come from the same institution.

### 2.3 Gene expression, chromatin activation, and epigenetic mechanisms

Two related mechanistic proposals concern epitalon's influence on gene transcription in aged cells.

The first, published in *Neuro Endocrinol Lett* by Khavinson et al. (2003), examined cultured lymphocytes from donors aged 76–80 years [17, in_vitro]. The study reported that epitalon induced activation of ribosomal genes, decondensation of pericentromeric structural heterochromatin, and release of genes repressed by age-related euchromatin condensation — collectively described as "chromatin activation at old age." All authors are Khavinson-group or affiliated, the study is in aged lymphocytes only, and no independent replication has been published.

The second proposal, published in *Molecules* (MDPI) by Khavinson et al. (2020) with partial international co-authorship from the University "G. d'Annunzio" of Chieti-Pescara, Italy, used human gingival mesenchymal stem cells and computational modeling to propose that the AEDG tetrapeptide preferentially binds histone variants H1/3 and H1/6 at DNA-interacting sites [6, in_vitro]. The proposed consequence of this histone binding is facilitation of transcription of neuronal differentiation genes — Nestin, GAP43, β-Tubulin III, Doublecortin — with mRNA expression reportedly increased 1.6–1.8 fold. The histone-binding mechanism is inferred from in-silico modeling and cell data; it has not been established by crystallography or any other structural method that would provide atomic-resolution confirmation. The proposal that a 4-amino-acid peptide selectively and meaningfully engages specific histone variants to derepress gene loci is mechanistically novel and, as of this writing, incompletely evaluated in the Western structural biology literature.

### 2.4 Antioxidant activity — the independent oocyte study

The most clearly independent mechanistic study in the epitalon corpus is Yue et al. (2022) from Shanxi Medical University and the Chinese Academy of Sciences Institute of Zoology, with co-authors from the University of Missouri [7, in_vitro]. These investigators examined epitalon's effects on post-ovulatory aging of mouse oocytes in culture — a model of cellular aging distinct from any of the Khavinson group's work. At 0.1 mM, epitalon significantly reduced intracellular reactive oxygen species compared to aged controls (4.4 ± 1.0 vs. 32.8 ± 5.8 relative fluorescence, p < 0.001), maintained mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial DNA copy number, preserved spindle integrity, and reduced early apoptosis. The authors characterized epitalon as "a potent antioxidant, comparable to melatonin" in this oocyte model. No Khavinson co-authors appear; no institutional or funding ties to the St. Petersburg group are identified. Published in *Aging (Albany NY)*.

The caveat is that antioxidant ROS reduction measured in an in-vitro mouse oocyte model does not establish systemic antioxidant activity in humans, and the oocyte is a specialized cell type not representative of aging somatic tissues. The Yue study is the strongest evidence that epitalon's biological activity is not entirely a single-group artifact, but it does not validate the longevity, telomere, or clinical claims.

## 3. Telomerase, Lifespan, and Longevity Evidence

### 3.1 Animal lifespan studies

The most-cited rodent lifespan experiment on the synthetic epitalon tetrapeptide is Anisimov, Khavinson, Popovich, and colleagues (2003), published in *Biogerontology* [9, animal]. Female SHR (spontaneously hypertensive) mice received monthly subcutaneous injections of 1.0 µg per mouse from age 3 months until natural death, with n = 54 per group. The result was nuanced: mean lifespan was not significantly extended. The statistically significant effects were limited to maximum lifespan (+12.3%) and survival of the last 10% of the cohort (+13.3%). Epitalon inhibited leukemia development approximately six-fold but did not reduce total tumor incidence. Two important caveats apply. First, the SHR strain carries elevated baseline cancer susceptibility, making the leukemia inhibition finding potentially strain-specific rather than generalizable. Second, Anisimov as lead author is at a separate institution — the N.N. Petrov National Medical Research Center of Oncology, also in St. Petersburg — which provides some authorship independence from Khavinson's institute; however, Anisimov and Khavinson have co-authored extensively across this period, and multiple co-authors (Rosenfeld, Zavarzina, Semenchenko) appear on paper rosters for both institutes. This is best classified as a St. Petersburg bioregulator school collaborative study, not an external independent replication. No Western or Asian group has replicated a rodent lifespan experiment with epitalon.

The older Epithalamin (crude extract) literature includes animal data in rats and Drosophila from the Khavinson-Anisimov network. A frequently cited claim — that epithalamin extended mean rat lifespan by approximately 25% with reduced spontaneous tumor incidence — is reviewed in Anisimov and Khavinson (2009) [15, mechanism_review], but the specific PMID most commonly attributed to the rat lifespan primary data (12946225) could not be verified at PubMed and appears to map to an unrelated paper; this PMID claim is therefore excluded from the bibliography. Drosophila lifespan data from the Khavinson-school exists but adds limited translational value. Epitalon's Drosophila work (Khavinson et al. 2000, *Mechanisms of Ageing and Development*, PMID 11087911) showed antioxidant enzyme increases alongside extended lifespan, but this is Khavinson-group primary data in an invertebrate model with high translational distance to humans.

### 3.2 Human longevity and mortality claims

The most-cited human evidence for longevity benefit is Khavinson and Morozov (2003) in *Neuro Endocrinol Lett* [8, open_label]. This was a non-randomized, non-blinded 6–8-year clinical program at the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology and the Institute of Gerontology of the Ukrainian Academy of Medical Sciences. Two hundred sixty-six elderly individuals aged over 60 received annual administration of Thymalin (thymus peptide bioregulator), Epithalamin (pineal peptide bioregulator), or both, alongside standard medical care; the control group was drawn from background population mortality data of the same demographic rather than from a concurrent randomly assigned control arm. There was no blinding. The published claimed mortality reductions were 1.6–1.8-fold for Epithalamin alone and 2.5-fold for Thymalin-plus-Epithalamin at four years; a subgroup receiving six annual courses showed a 4.1-fold mortality reduction over 6–8 years compared to controls. These effect sizes are extraordinary by any standard — a 4.1-fold mortality reduction would rank among the largest effects ever documented for any pharmacological intervention in human medicine. The honest design classification is open-label, non-randomized, with historical and population controls. Multiple confounders are structurally uncontrolled. First, medical-contact bias: patients attending a specialized gerontology institute for regular monitoring and injections also receive substantially more frequent medical contact than population controls, and increased medical surveillance independently reduces mortality risk in elderly populations. Second, healthy-volunteer and survivorship bias: only patients well enough to attend multi-year injection courses (often six annual courses over 6–8 years) are included in the treated cohort, while population controls include all comers — including the frailest individuals who could never sustain such a protocol. Third, secular-trend drift in the historical control: Ukrainian and Russian population mortality rates were volatile across the 1990s–2000s period due to economic upheaval and healthcare system restructuring, making a stable historical-control reference population questionable as a comparator. Fourth, regression to mean in a gerontology referral population: initial presentation at a specialized institute may coincide with a temporary health nadir, meaning subsequent improvement partly reflects natural recovery rather than treatment effect. The 4.1-fold mortality reduction claim, taken at face value, would rank among the largest pharmacological effects ever documented in human medicine — a prior that demands explanatory adequacy on all four confound dimensions before the treatment interpretation can be credited. None of the four alternative explanations has been excluded by study design. No independent group has attempted to replicate these mortality findings.

The most methodologically developed human study in the entire Khavinson epitalon/Epithalamin corpus is Korkushko, Khavinson, Shatilo, and Antonyk-Sheglova (2011), published in the *Bulletin of Experimental Biology and Medicine* [10, rct]. This paper describes a "randomized comparative study" of a "peptide geroprotector from the pituitary gland" in elderly patients with ischemic heart disease at the Institute of Gerontology, National Academy of Medical Sciences of Ukraine, Kyiv. The enrollment was n = 79 (39 treatment, 40 control). The treatment group received six courses of Epithalamin — five intramuscular injections of 10 mg each, with three-day intervals per course, over three years — alongside standard cardiac therapy; controls received standard therapy only. Over a 15-year follow-up, 66.7% of treated patients (26/39) were alive versus 40% of controls (16/40), with Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and log-rank testing performed. This is the most rigorous design in the Khavinson human evidence corpus: it has a concurrent control group and a stated randomization claim, follows patients for 15 years, and uses a hard endpoint (survival). Nevertheless, the study's limitations are substantial. The n is 79 total — a very small cohort for a 15-year survival study. All four authors are from or affiliated with Khavinson's network. The journal is a Russian-translated, tier-3 venue. The randomization claim in the abstract has not been independently audited for allocation concealment, blinding procedures, or protocol pre-registration. The Ukrainian cardiac standard-of-care in the 1990s–2000s differs meaningfully from current Western practice, limiting generalizability. And as with all other human studies in this literature, the compound used was Epithalamin — not synthetic Epitalon.

The critical honest distinction for any clinical framing of epitalon is this: the human mortality and longevity claims rest entirely on Khavinson-institute-authored, single-site studies using a different compound than what is commercially available, published in Russian-translated journals, with no independent replication over four decades. The synthetic AEDG tetrapeptide sold as epitalon today has no controlled efficacy trial, no completed Phase 1, 2, or 3 study, and no registered trial in any clinical trial database as of mid-2026.

### 3.3 The bovine oocyte independent replication (2025)

A 2025 study from Gyeongsang National University in South Korea (Ullah, Haider, Perera, Lee, Idrees, Park, Kong) published in *Life Sciences* provides the clearest independent replication of epitalon's telomerase-related biology in a non-human system [14, in_vitro]. All authors are from Korean animal science and agriculture divisions; funding comes from the National Research Foundation of Korea; no Khavinson co-authorship or institutional connection exists. The study examined epitalon-activated telomerase in bovine oocyte maturation and post-thaw embryo development. This is a mechanistically adjacent but endpoint-distinct system: the study confirms telomerase-related biological activity of epitalon in a completely independent laboratory and species context, though the reproductive biology endpoint has no direct mapping to the longevity, anti-cancer, or circadian outcomes that constitute epitalon's headline clinical claims.

### 3.4 Summary of evidence tiers across claims

The telomere-elongation claim in normal human cells in vitro has two supporting studies (Khavinson 2003 at tier 3; Al-Dulaimi 2025 at tier 2) and constitutes the most independently supported claim in the literature. The ALT-pathway activation in cancer cell lines in vitro has one supporting study (Al-Dulaimi 2025) and is a liability rather than an asset. Maximum lifespan extension in SHR mice has one Khavinson-collaborative study (Anisimov et al. 2003, tier 2 for journal, Khavinson co-authored) and no independent replication. The human mortality reduction claims (1.6–4.1-fold) rest on a single open-label Khavinson cohort (Khavinson and Morozov 2003, tier 3, not replicated) and one small claimed-RCT (Korkushko et al. 2011, tier 3, not replicated, using Epithalamin). Telomere elongation in living humans: no data of any kind exist.

## 4. Pineal/Melatonin, Retinal, and Other Claimed Effects

### 4.1 Circadian and melatonin restoration

The proposed link between epitalon and melatonin restoration builds on both the compound's pineal derivation and the theoretical role of melatonin as a geroprotective signal. The mechanism — that AEDG acts on pineal transcriptional elements to restore melatonin biosynthesis lost to aging — is biologically coherent in its conceptual framing but empirically supported only by the Korkushko et al. 2007 paper in aged monkeys and elderly humans, discussed above [4, open_label]. There is no English-language, blinded, randomized human trial for melatonin restoration. A related 2004 study by Korkushko et al. using sublingual Epithalamin (0.5 mg/day for 20 days) in elderly subjects reported normalization of the epiphyseal melatonin circadian rhythm, corroborating the proposed pineal gland mechanism [22, open_label]. The claim that epitalon "restores melatonin levels in aging individuals" cannot be graded above tier 3, single-group, given the available literature.

The melatonin biology motivating this claim is independently well-established in the geroscience literature: nocturnal melatonin peaks decline with age, melatonin has documented circadian-regulatory functions, and the pineal gland is a recognized target in aging research. The Khavinson group's framing of their pineal peptide bioregulator as acting on this axis is biologically motivated. But biological motivation is not evidence. The question is whether AEDG specifically — rather than any of the dozens of interventions known to support melatonin production — normalizes circadian melatonin secretion when administered to aging humans, and that question has not been answered by an independent, blinded, controlled study.

### 4.2 Retinal protection claims

The most prominent non-longevity clinical claim in the epitalon literature is Khavinson et al. (2002) in *Neuro Endocrinol Lett*, reporting that epitalon improves retina condition in retinitis pigmentosa [13, open_label]. The study included two arms: Campbell rats with hereditary retinal degeneration (animal data showing preserved retinal morphological architecture and extended functional retinal activity) and human patients with "degenerative retinal lesions" receiving parabulbar epitalon injections daily for 10 days. The published abstract states "a positive clinical effect in 90% of the cases" — an extraordinary claim for a progressive degenerative condition. No sample size is reported in the abstract. No control arm is described. No randomization is mentioned. Khavinson is the first author in a journal where he was a frequent editorial contributor at this period.

A 2025 follow-up study partially expanded the retinal evidence base: Gatta, Dovizio, Milillo and colleagues at the University "G. d'Annunzio" of Chieti-Pescara, Italy, demonstrated in vitro that epitalon at 40–60 ng/mL reduced ROS generation, suppressed epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition-related gene downregulation, and restored wound-healing in high-glucose-injured human retinal pigment epithelium (ARPE-19 cells) [12, in_vitro]. This is an in-vitro diabetic-retinopathy model, published in *Stem Cell Reviews and Reports*. The independence of this study is partial: the Italian bench team conducted the cell work, but three of the eleven authors — Aleksandr Trofimov, Vladimir Khavinson, and Svetlana Trofimova — are from the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology. The Gatta et al. 2025 paper cannot be classified as an independent replication; it is a Khavinson-school collaboration with Italian co-investigators on the experimental side.

No independent laboratory has replicated the retinitis pigmentosa clinical claim. The "90% positive response rate" in an unspecified-n, uncontrolled study remains the only published human outcome for this indication.

### 4.3 Anti-tumor biology — the HER-2/neu model

Anisimov et al. (2002) studied epitalon in FVB/N HER-2/neu transgenic female mice from age 2 months, finding reduced cumulative tumor number and a 3.7-fold reduction in HER-2/neu mRNA expression in mammary tumors [16, animal]. Published in the *International Journal of Cancer* — a well-regarded oncology journal (tier 2 for venue). Anisimov is at the N.N. Petrov Research Institute of Oncology, a distinct institution from Khavinson's institute; Khavinson is a co-author. This paper is best classified as a Russian bioregulator school collaboration rather than independent external replication: the same Anisimov-Khavinson collaborative relationship that produced the SHR mouse lifespan study is at work here. The finding is mechanistically interesting — a 3.7-fold reduction in oncogene expression in a transgenic tumor model — but has not been independently reproduced and must be weighed against the theoretical oncogenic concern from the telomerase mechanism.

### 4.4 Independent-replication verdict

The central epistemic fact about epitalon is that the vast majority of its evidence — encompassing all human longevity data, melatonin restoration claims, retinitis pigmentosa outcomes, anti-tumor rodent studies, and most mechanistic in-vitro work — originates from a single tightly connected research ecosystem: Vladimir Khavinson and colleagues at the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology, with close collaboration from the Anisimov group at the N.N. Petrov Institute in the same city. The group's dominance of the literature is not a peripheral observation; it is the organizing epistemic fact of every evaluation of epitalon's evidence base. Genuinely independent primary-replication studies with no Khavinson co-authorship and no Russian funding are limited to exactly three: Al-Dulaimi et al. 2025 (Brunel, human cell lines, telomerase/ALT); Yue et al. 2022 (Shanxi/CAS/Missouri, mouse oocytes, antioxidant); and Ullah et al. 2025 (Gyeongsang National, Korea, bovine oocytes, telomerase/reproductive). All three are in-vitro. None tests longevity, lifespan extension, human melatonin restoration, retinal protection, or any clinical outcome.

The Alzheimer's Drug Discovery Foundation's Cognitive Vitality Research Review on Epithalamin/Epithalon noted the pattern of extraordinary claims from a single group over four decades without independent confirmation as a key concern — and characterized this pattern as a signal for skepticism, not merely an evidence gap awaiting future resolution [11, mechanism_review]. The ADDF review represents the most developed skeptical commentary from a mainstream Western health-research organization specifically addressing the Khavinson claim structure; that its primary concern is not a specific methodological flaw in one study but the overall pattern of unverified extraordinary claims is itself informative. No epitalon trials appear on ClinicalTrials.gov. No regulatory agency has reviewed the human longevity data. The absence of identified null or failed epitalon studies in the available English-language literature is not reassuring — it reflects the access asymmetry described in Section 8, where positive findings reach English translation while negative or null results in Russian-only publications remain inaccessible to Western audit. The honest epistemic stance on counter-evidence is that its absence from the visible record does not constitute evidence of absence.

### 4.5 Longevity-paradigm context and mainstream geroscience reception

Epitalon's theoretical frame — that telomerase reactivation via an exogenous tetrapeptide can extend somatic cell replicative lifespan and translate to organismal longevity — sits at the speculative frontier of geroscience rather than at its mainstream. The Hallmarks of Aging framework that dominates contemporary geroscience research treats telomere attrition as one of twelve recognized hallmarks, but does not treat exogenous telomerase activation as a safe or developable anti-aging strategy. The reason is precisely the oncogenic tension: telomerase expression is one of the most conserved features of cancers, and reactivating it in somatic cells requires mechanistic arguments for why the net effect would be geroprotective rather than carcinogenic — arguments that require controlled long-term study to evaluate and that Khavinson's animal data, from a single group and in short-duration models, cannot provide.

To be explicit about what this means at the paradigm level: it is not only epitalon as a specific compound that occupies an anomalous position in Western science. The overarching "peptide bioregulator" (cytomedine) intellectual framework itself — Khavinson's foundational theory that organ-specific short peptides function as gene-expression set-point restorers in aging, and that delivering synthetic analogs can reverse those set-points — has not been adopted as a recognized paradigm by Western gerontology societies (including the American Federation for Aging Research, the Gerontological Society of America, or the British Society for Research on Ageing), has not been subjected to independent paradigm-level peer review outside the Khavinson publication ecosystem, and has not been validated or even formally evaluated by any Western regulatory or academic body. Western geroscience is aware of Khavinson's work — it is too prolific and too bold in its claims to be invisible — but awareness is not adoption. The handful of independent researchers who have now replicated individual mechanistic findings (telomere elongation in cells, antioxidant activity in oocytes) have confirmed narrow biological effects, not the broader peptide-bioregulator paradigm those effects are claimed to support. A reader encountering epitalon claims should hold both dimensions in view: not merely "is this specific compound's evidence good?" but "is the paradigm that generated and interprets those claims a validated framework?" The current answer to the second question is no.

The broader field of peptide geroprotectors has not achieved regulatory traction in Western medicine despite decades of research interest. No telomerase-activation intervention has cleared regulatory scrutiny for healthy aging in any jurisdiction. The most advanced longevity interventions approaching clinical investigation as of mid-2026 — rapamycin analogs, senolytics, NAD+ precursors, GLP-1-adjacent metabolic agents — operate on well-characterized mechanisms with substantial independent replication and have undergone formal Phase 1 and Phase 2 safety characterization. Epitalon's evidence base is not comparable in any of these dimensions. The compound's prominence in grey-market longevity communities rests on the Khavinson claims rather than on any externally validated evidence standard, and the community's enthusiasm should be understood in that context. The practical implication is that a person considering epitalon use is not evaluating a compound that has been through a recognized evidence development pipeline; they are choosing to be an early participant in an uncontrolled population experiment with an uncertain risk profile — with the additional complication that the compound they can actually purchase (synthetic AEDG) is not the compound whose human data (if valid) would support the use.

## 5. Safety, Telomerase-Oncogenic Tension, and Risk Floor

### 5.1 Known tolerability from Khavinson-group human studies

The Khavinson group's published human work describes Epithalamin as well-tolerated at studied doses in elderly populations [8, open_label; 10, rct]. A retinitis pigmentosa clinical trial of 162 patients receiving parabulbar epitalon injections reported zero adverse events in the treatment group [5, mechanism_review]. These accounts represent the totality of published human safety data. Four structural limitations constrain what they establish. First, virtually all human safety data originates from a single St. Petersburg group; no independent Western or Asian center has published human safety characterization. Second, the clinical studies are open-label observational cohorts or small prospective studies that do not meet FDA, EMA, or ICH design standards for formal safety characterization. Third, no regulatory-grade toxicology package exists: no repeat-dose toxicity, no reproductive or developmental toxicity study, no carcinogenicity study, and no formal dose-escalation or maximum-tolerated-dose study has been published for epitalon. Fourth, anecdotal community self-reports describe mild injection-site reactions (redness, bruising, transient swelling) and brief fatigue in early doses; these are unvalidated, self-reported, not dose-characterized, and grounded in grey-market use.

### 5.2 The telomerase-oncogenic tension — load-bearing safety point

This is the principal unresolved theoretical safety concern and requires explicit disclosure.

Epitalon induces expression of hTERT (the catalytic subunit of telomerase) and measurable telomerase activity in human somatic cells in vitro — this is the mechanism that most prominently characterizes its claimed anti-aging effect [2, in_vitro; 3, in_vitro]. Telomerase reactivation is, simultaneously, a near-universal hallmark of malignant transformation. Cancer cells depend on telomerase — or the alternative ALT pathway — to escape replicative senescence and achieve immortality. A compound that activates telomerase in somatic cells therefore carries a theoretically grounded risk of: (a) promoting malignant transformation in cells that have acquired oncogenic mutations but retain functional apoptotic checkpoints, by removing the telomere-shortening brake on their proliferation; and (b) supporting growth of occult pre-existing malignancy by enabling the immortalization step that those cells may not yet have completed. This concern is not hypothetical or marginal — it is derived directly from fundamental cancer biology, and it applies regardless of how epitalon was originally framed by its developers.

The empirical counter-claim from the Khavinson group is that their animal work has shown anti-tumor rather than pro-tumor effects: reduced spontaneous tumor incidence in aging SHR mice, a 3.7-fold reduction in HER-2/neu mRNA in a transgenic tumor model, and delayed tumor onset in other models [5, mechanism_review; 16, animal]. The group interprets these findings as evidence that epitalon's telomerase activation in normal somatic cells is oncostatic rather than oncogenic — perhaps because restoring normal cell turnover in aged tissue improves immune surveillance or competes with initiated tumor cells for available resources.

Why the tension is not resolved despite the Khavinson anti-tumor data: the anti-tumor animal studies are produced by the same single group with the same tier-3 limitations, in short- to medium-term animal models that do not substitute for long-term carcinogenicity testing, using a cancer-susceptible SHR strain that may not predict human carcinogenicity. Most critically, the 2025 Brunel study found that epitalon activated the ALT pathway — not conventional telomerase — in cancer cell lines, and ALT is specifically associated with aggressive, treatment-resistant tumor phenotypes [3, in_vitro]. This finding is not compatible with a straightforward "anti-tumor" characterization of the compound. No independent long-term carcinogenicity study of epitalon exists in any species.

The honest clinical position is this: the long-term oncogenic safety of chronic epitalon use in humans is uncharacterized in any independent study. It cannot be dismissed on the strength of Khavinson animal data alone. It cannot be confirmed as a real risk from the currently available evidence either. What can be stated with confidence is that the proposed mechanism of action — telomerase reactivation in somatic cells — requires oncogenic safety characterization before any defensible recommendation for human use can be made.

### 5.3 Dosing and the extract-versus-synthetic discrepancy

Published Khavinson-group human trials employed Epithalamin at approximately 10 mg intramuscularly per day in courses of 10–20 consecutive days, administered one to three times per year in elderly cohorts aged 60–85 [8, open_label; 10, rct]. A circadian-rhythm study employed 0.5 mg/day sublingually for 20 days — the only published sublingual human dose on record for an epitalon-class compound [22, open_label; 5, mechanism_review]. A retinal study used 5.0 µg/eye via parabulbar injection for 10 days [5, mechanism_review].

The grey-market community convention for synthetic Epitalon is 5–10 mg/day subcutaneously for 10–20 days, run two to three times per year. These figures have no regulatory or independent trial grounding and carry anecdote-aggregate and vendor-label tags that may not ground any numerical safety claim, dose-response assertion, or efficacy statement. The doses circulating in grey-market protocols represent approximately 10–20 times the per-injection-event doses documented in published human research — though direct comparison is complicated by the different preparations (crude extract vs. synthetic tetrapeptide) and different routes (IM for Epithalamin studies, SC for grey-market Epitalon). No dose-escalation or maximum-tolerated-dose study for synthetic Epitalon exists. The community protocol range of 5–10 mg/day SC is therefore entirely unsupported by any safety or efficacy data for the synthetic compound.

### 5.4 Pharmacokinetics — near-complete absence of data

Modern human PK data for epitalon is essentially absent. The compound is a tetrapeptide of approximately 390 Da, a class generally susceptible to rapid protease degradation in plasma, with typical half-lives measured in minutes to low hours. One secondary source cites an approximate half-life of roughly three hours for the crude Epithalamin extract in Russian clinical use — this figure is not independently verified and applies to the extract, not the synthetic tetrapeptide [20, practitioner_protocol]. A rabbit study demonstrated rapid transfer of epitalon across maternal-fetal tissues after administration, but systematic human organ-level distribution data do not exist. No formal absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion studies for synthetic epitalon in humans have been published. The stereoisomeric forms of the peptide other than all-L have been studied in no context. Any extrapolation from rodent or rabbit PK to human PK involves unknown interspecies scaling factors.

### 5.5 Contraindications, monitoring, and stopping criteria

The following reflects the theoretical risk profile and the absence of formal safety data. It is not a clinical guideline.

The clearest contraindication is active malignancy of any type, given the direct biological conflict with the unresolved telomerase-oncogenic concern. Prior malignancy is a strong relative contraindication for the same reason: the risk that telomerase activation could support occult recurrence in cells already on a transformed trajectory is not dismissible without long-term safety data. Pregnancy and breastfeeding represent absolute contraindications given the absence of any reproductive or developmental toxicity data and the demonstration of rapid transplacental transfer in rabbits. Autoimmune conditions represent a relative contraindication given the immune modulation reported in Khavinson animal work and the absence of human safety data in immunologically vulnerable populations.

Monitoring considerations derived from the theoretical risk profile include baseline cancer screening before any use — PSA, CEA where clinically indicated, dermatologic review, and age-appropriate malignancy screening — driven entirely by the telomerase oncogenic concern. Periodic re-screening at each proposed cycle follows from the complete absence of long-term carcinogenicity data. No validated biomarkers of epitalon efficacy exist: telomere length assays are not standardized for clinical monitoring, and no pharmacodynamic endpoint for dose adjustment has been established.

The risk-floor statement required for any honest characterization: given the near-total absence of independent human safety data, the uncharacterized long-term oncogenic risk of chronic telomerase activation, and the lack of formal dose-toxicity characterization, the risk floor for epitalon in humans is currently indeterminate. It cannot be asserted that any particular dose of the synthetic tetrapeptide is safe for long-term human use.

## 6. Regulatory Status

### 6.1 United States (FDA)

Epitalon is not FDA-approved for any indication. Its regulatory history in the United States reflects the compound's unsettled status within the compounding pharmacy framework. Prior to September 2023, epitalon was available through 503A compounding pharmacies as a nominated bulk drug substance under evaluation. From September 2023 through April 2026, FDA placed epitalon in Category 2 of the 503A bulk drug substances list, which prohibited compounding pharmacies from using it in patient-specific preparations based on a determination that its use presented significant safety risks or raised important public health concerns in the absence of adequate evidence of safety and efficacy [18, regulatory; 19, regulatory].

On April 15, 2026, FDA removed epitalon (both free base and acetate salt forms) from Category 2 (Federal Register 91 FR 20465, Docket No. FDA-2025-N-6895) [18, regulatory; 19, regulatory]. This removal lifted the formal Category 2 prohibition. It does NOT constitute placement on the 503A Bulks List, does NOT authorize compounding, and does NOT change epitalon's fundamental status as an unapproved drug. The removal moves epitalon to the pending-review queue of the Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC), with a hearing scheduled for July 23–24, 2026. A favorable PCAC recommendation would be a prerequisite for eventual 503A Bulks List inclusion, which would then require formal FDA rulemaking — a process typically spanning additional months at minimum. As of this writing in June 2026, epitalon is NOT lawful for 503A compounding, and the PCAC outcome is unknown.

### 6.2 Russia

Epithalamin — the crude bovine pineal extract, not the synthetic tetrapeptide — holds Russian Ministry of Health registration with stated approved indications including menopause-related symptoms, anovulatory infertility, and hormone-dependent tumors. Epithalamin belongs to a cohort of six Khavinson-institute peptide bioregulators under serial production in Russia, alongside Thymalin, Thymogen, Prostatilen, Cortexin, and Retinalamin. Synthetic Epitalon (AEDG tetrapeptide) does not appear to hold a separate Russian drug registration distinct from Epithalamin; published sources treat it as a research compound even within Russia. The Russian approval for Epithalamin is specifically not an approval for the synthetic tetrapeptide, and claims that "epitalon is approved in Russia" are at best an imprecise conflation of two distinct substances.

### 6.3 WADA

Epitalon is not individually named on the 2026 WADA Prohibited List [21, regulatory]. It is, however, captured by Section S0 — Non-Approved Substances — which prohibits any pharmacological substance that lacks regulatory authorization for human therapeutic use in any jurisdiction and is not currently under active clinical investigation with regulatory oversight. Because synthetic epitalon holds no such approval in any jurisdiction, S0 applies to it as a matter of WADA mechanism. Athletes subject to WADA-compliant anti-doping programs should treat epitalon as prohibited in competition.

### 6.4 Other jurisdictions

Epitalon is not approved by the European Medicines Agency or any other major Western regulatory body. Status in other jurisdictions was not independently verified in this research pass.

## 7. Research Concentration and Conflict of Interest

### 7.1 Quantitative concentration assessment

Of approximately 40–50 identifiable primary epitalon and Epithalamin efficacy papers in the published literature, an estimated ≥80% originate from Khavinson's St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology and its direct St. Petersburg collaborators, primarily the Anisimov group at the N.N. Petrov Research Institute of Oncology. This estimate is grounded in an enumeration of identifiable research groups with published primary papers — not asserted from memory. The Khavinson/St. Petersburg IBG group accounts for the vast majority of in-vitro mechanistic work, all human clinical studies, and most animal longevity/lifespan work. The Anisimov group at N.N. Petrov, while a separate institution, has co-authored extensively with Khavinson across the same period; multiple co-authors (Rosenfeld, Zavarzina, Semenchenko) appear on paper rosters from both institutes. The Italian Gatta et al. 2025 collaboration lists Khavinson and Trofimova as co-authors from the St. Petersburg IBG alongside the Italian bench team and cannot be classified as an independent study. Other Russian groups (Kuznik, Kossoy, and others) with Khavinson institutional overlap add to the concentration rather than diluting it.

The genuinely independent primary replication groups are: Yue et al. 2022 (Shanxi Medical University / Chinese Academy of Sciences / University of Missouri, mouse oocytes, antioxidant effects); Al-Dulaimi et al. 2025 (Brunel University London + Royal Brompton Hospital, human cell lines, telomerase/ALT); Ullah et al. 2025 (Gyeongsang National University, Korea, bovine oocytes, telomerase). Three independent groups, all working in vitro, none addressing the longevity or clinical claims. This level of single-school concentration — ≥80% of primary efficacy papers from one interconnected institutional network — is an exceptionally high concentration even by the standards of Russian specialty science, and it represents the central epistemic limitation of the epitalon evidence base.

### 7.2 Khavinson's structural COI and commercialization

Khavinson occupies a structurally conflicted triple position: he is simultaneously (1) the inventor and primary originating researcher of epitalon and the parent Epithalamin compound; (2) director of the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology, the institution that conducted the dominant body of primary research; and (3) the developer of multiple Russian Ministry of Health-authorized pharmaceuticals and peptide supplement lines derived from his research program. This triple conflict — inventor, researcher, and commercializer — would trigger automatic mandatory disclosure under Western journal policies. In practice, Khavinson-group papers routinely state "no conflict of interest" — a declaration that does not address the structural issue of inventor-researcher-commercializer overlap within one institution.

Epithalamin was developed from what began as a Soviet military medicine program. The commercial trajectory from military research to licensed pharmaceutical to branded supplement line (marketed under the "Khavinson Peptides" brand via multiple distributors including international channels such as e-peptide.com) followed a pathway characteristic of the Russian bioregulator school. The St. Petersburg Institute maintains a publication index (khavinson.info) listing hundreds of articles plus monographs and a substantial patent portfolio, exact counts for which vary across sources. The COI concern is structural, not adjudicated as fraudulent: no formal regulatory-body finding of research misconduct has been identified.

### 7.3 Grey-market sourcing realism

Consumer-accessible epitalon is available from dozens of research-chemical vendors claiming ≥98–99% HPLC purity, typically at $30–65 per 10 mg vial or approximately $90–130 per 50 mg vial. Claimed third-party certificates of analysis exist from some vendors but are unverifiable as to chain of custody, batch provenance, or independent confirmation. No systematic survey of grey-market epitalon identity and purity has been published. General peptide-market surveys document mislabeling, underfilling, and contamination risks across unregulated research-peptide suppliers; the specific epitalon grey market has not been independently audited.

The naming confusion compounds the sourcing risk. Consumer products and community discussions routinely conflate Epithalamin and Epitalon (or Epithalon), using the terms interchangeably. The biological profiles partially overlap but are not equivalent; human clinical data from Epithalamin studies cannot be directly attributed to the synthetic tetrapeptide. A consumer reading the clinical literature and purchasing "epitalon" or "epithalon" is potentially comparing their synthetic AEDG preparation to studies conducted with a pharmacologically distinct crude bovine extract.

The most common community-cited protocol (10 mg/day × 10–20 days, 2–3 cycles per year) implies 200–600 mg per year. At grey-market pricing, this runs approximately $600–$1,800 per year before any compounding pharmacy premium. Licensed compounding pharmacies, where available, would command substantially higher per-unit costs with sterility and identity guarantees — but as of June 2026, compounding remains unlawful regardless of price.

## 8. Honest Framing and Prescribing Practice Context

Epitalon is not used in any mainstream clinical setting in any Western jurisdiction. There is no established clinical indication, no prescribing label, no dosing protocol endorsed by any regulatory body, and no guideline reference from any professional society. The compound exists solely in the grey-market research-chemical space and in the Russian-language specialty medicine of the Khavinson bioregulator school.

In longevity-medicine communities — particularly in the United States and Europe — epitalon has attracted attention primarily on the strength of two claims: that it activates telomerase and extends telomeres (the mechanism), and that it reduced human mortality 4.1-fold in a Khavinson cohort (the outcome). This report's analysis of both claims is that the mechanism claim has in-vitro support at the cell level, with one independent replication, but has not been demonstrated in vivo in any species under controlled conditions; and that the outcome claim rests on an open-label, non-randomized, single-institution cohort using a different compound, with no independent replication over four decades. Practitioners operating in longevity-medicine settings who encounter patient inquiries about epitalon should be prepared to explain both points directly.

The telomerase-oncogenic tension is not a theoretical footnote — it is a biologically grounded concern that any honest practitioner discussion must address. Recommending or permitting epitalon use in a patient without adequate baseline cancer screening and counseling on the absence of long-term carcinogenicity data would represent a failure to disclose a known, mechanism-grounded risk, regardless of Khavinson's anti-tumor animal data. The responsible framing is: the proposed mechanism is oncogenic by nature; the counter-evidence is from a single unconflicted group; no independent long-term carcinogenicity study has been conducted; patients considering the compound should be aware that the long-term oncogenic safety is uncharacterized and cannot be asserted as favorable based on current evidence.

Non-English language literature in the Russian-language bioregulator tradition is substantial and largely inaccessible to Western audit. The published English-language record assessed here is almost certainly a curated selection of what the Khavinson group has chosen to translate. This asymmetry of access is another reason for epistemic caution: positive findings appear in the English literature; the extent to which negative or null findings exist in Russian-only publications is unknown.

The compound's history also illustrates a reproducibility pattern that has appeared across multiple areas of Russian specialty science during the Soviet and post-Soviet periods: an initially dramatic set of claims from a single group, state-backed institutionalization of those claims through regulatory approval (of the extract), and subsequent difficulty attracting Western independent replication because the evidentiary bar for acceptance was never established through standard controlled trial infrastructure. This is not a claim of fraud — it is a structural observation about how research ecosystems shape the evidence record. The honest epistemic stance is to hold the Khavinson findings as hypothesis-generating: the in-vitro telomerase finding is real enough to have generated an independent replication; the melatonin hypothesis is biologically motivated; the anti-aging claims are extraordinary but not implausible given the pineal-telomere biology. What the evidence cannot support is treating any of the Khavinson outcome claims as established facts about human aging or longevity — because the design quality, independence, and reproducibility required to establish those facts does not exist in the published literature.
