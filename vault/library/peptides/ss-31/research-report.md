# SS-31 / Elamipretide (FORZINITY)

## Metadata

- **class:** tetrapeptide (Szeto-Schiller aromatic-cationic family)
- **evidence_tier:** B-/C+ — FDA-accelerated-approval drug (ultra-rare indication, October 2025) with every controlled trial missing its primary endpoint; robust replicated preclinical base across three species and multiple independent labs; stark translation gap; ≥90% single-sponsor clinical literature concentration; Szeto/Cornell commercial COI first-class throughout the mechanistic corpus.
- **risk_tier:** approved-drug-ultra-rare — FDA-approved at 40 mg SC QD for Barth syndrome (≥30 kg); injection-site reactions dominant AE; off-label use (anti-aging, performance, cardiac) carries no regulatory or evidence backing; grey-market "SS-31" is unverified compounded material with no chain of custody.
- **status:** researching

## Summary

SS-31 (elamipretide; MTP-131; brand name **Forzinity**) is a synthetic cell-permeable tetrapeptide with the sequence D-Arg–2′,6′-dimethylTyr–Lys–Phe–NH₂ (MW 639.8 g/mol; CAS 736992-21-5). It was developed by Hazel H. Szeto and Peter W. Schiller — hence "SS" peptide — at Weill Cornell Medicine and licensed exclusively to Stealth BioTherapeutics (subsequently Mighty Therapeutics) in 2006, making the entire clinical evidence base ≥90% single-sponsor-funded with explicit inventor financial conflicts in every mechanism paper.

**The honest evidence state, stated once and up front:**

On **October 30, 2025**, the FDA granted accelerated approval of Forzinity (NDA 215244) to improve muscle strength in adult and pediatric patients with Barth syndrome (BTHS) weighing ≥30 kg at a dose of 40 mg SC once daily [1]. This makes elamipretide the **first FDA-approved treatment for Barth syndrome and the first approved mitochondria-targeting drug** — a genuine regulatory milestone. However, the approval rested entirely on **uncontrolled open-label extension (OLE) data**: median knee extensor muscle strength gain of +63 N at week 168 from an OLE baseline of 124 N, in 8 completing participants. The controlled crossover phase of TAZPOWER — the pivotal trial — **failed both pre-specified primary endpoints** (6-minute walk test and BTHS Symptom Assessment fatigue score, where both arms improved ~43 m and ~1.4 points identically). Continued approval is contingent on a confirmatory trial. Every randomized controlled trial of elamipretide across all indications **failed its prespecified primary endpoint**: MMPOWER-3 (primary mitochondrial myopathy, n=218, Phase 3), TAZPOWER controlled crossover (Barth syndrome, n=12), EMBRACE-STEMI (STEMI, n=300), SPIHF-201/PROGRESS-HF (HFrEF, n=71), and ReCLAIM-2 (geographic atrophy, n=176).

Against that clinical record stands a **strong, replicated preclinical foundation** spanning rat renal ischemia-reperfusion [3], rat and mouse cardiac I/R [5, 6, 8, 9], swine hemorrhagic shock [7], and aging mouse skeletal muscle and cardiac function [10, 11, 12] — with multiple independent laboratories (University of Washington/Rabinovitch-Marcinek, Communications Biology imaging group, Mayo Clinic renal artery stenosis team, Xuzhou/Chinese PLA General Hospital groups) confirming SS-31's core mitochondrial mechanism. The mechanism is not in dispute: SS-31 binds cardiolipin (CL) at the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM), stabilizes cristae architecture and cytochrome c in its electron-carrier conformation, reduces pathological ROS from electron leak at Complexes I/III, and restores oxidative phosphorylation efficiency. Barth syndrome is the partial exception to the clinical failure pattern precisely because BTHS patients have a confirmed primary CL remodeling defect (tafazzin mutation) — mechanistic target engagement is known at enrollment — and the OLE signal is the only sustained functional gain across the entire clinical program.

**WADA status:** Elamipretide exits the S0 (Non-Approved Substances) category upon FDA approval in October 2025 and is not named in S2 or any other category. Direct confirmation against the live 2026 list could not be completed (site inaccessible at research time); athletes must verify via GlobalDRO.com before relying on this assessment [27].

**Sourcing:** Clinical-grade Forzinity is available by prescription via specialty pharmacy for the approved BTHS indication only. Grey-market "SS-31" research-chemical peptides have no chain of custody, no GMP manufacturing oversight, and no verified identity, purity, or sterility [28].

## 1. Identity, Mechanism, and Molecular Pharmacology

### 1.1 Identity and nomenclature

SS-31 carries multiple names across its development history: **elamipretide** (current INN), **MTP-131** (Stealth BioTherapeutics internal code), **Bendavia** (cardiac-trial brand), and **Forzinity** (FDA-approved brand, 2025). It belongs to the Szeto-Schiller (SS) aromatic-cationic tetrapeptide family, first described in 2004 [2]. The naming convention for the family reflects the inventors' initials — Szeto and Schiller — with a number designation; "SS-31" is the 31st member of that series but the best-characterized and the only one to reach late-stage clinical development.

Full molecular specification: sequence D-Arg–2′,6′-dimethylTyr–Lys–Phe–NH₂; molecular formula C₃₂H₄₉N₉O₅; MW 639.8 g/mol; CAS 736992-21-5. The C-terminal amide (-NH₂) and the incorporation of D-arginine (not L-arginine) at position 1 confer resistance to enzymatic degradation. The non-canonical residue 2′,6′-dimethyltyrosine (Dmt) at position 2 introduces two methyl groups on the tyrosine ring, providing both free-radical scavenging activity and a steric constraint that fundamentally alters the membrane-bound peptide conformation.

Key family members and their comparative pharmacology:

| Peptide | Sequence | Membrane conformation | Free-radical scavenging | Primary driver |
|---------|----------|----------------------|------------------------|----------------|
| **SS-31** | D-Arg–Dmt–Lys–Phe–NH₂ | Extended (unique) | Yes (Dmt) | CL binding |
| **SS-20** | Phe–D-Arg–Phe–Lys–NH₂ | Reverse-turn | No | CL binding only |
| **SS-02** | Dmt–D-Arg–Phe–Lys–NH₂ | Reverse-turn | Yes (Dmt) | CL binding |

A 2022 eLife structure-activity study established that **all SS-peptide analogs except SS-31 form H-bonded reverse-turn conformations** when membrane-bound; SS-31's unique extended conformation is a direct consequence of steric constraints from the two methyl groups on Dmt [3]. The same study confirmed that SS-20, lacking Dmt, retains mitochondrial efficacy without antioxidant activity — demonstrating that **cardiolipin binding, not free-radical scavenging, is the primary mechanistic driver for the class** [3]. This finding has an important clinical implication: the antioxidant framing of SS-31 often encountered in lay literature and early marketing materials is mechanistically secondary. The compound's bioenergetic benefits are attributable to CL binding and structural membrane effects, not to Dmt's ability to quench reactive oxygen species directly.

### 1.2 Cardiolipin binding — the load-bearing mechanism

The IMM is uniquely enriched in **cardiolipin (CL)**, a tetra-acyl anionic phospholipid virtually absent from the outer mitochondrial membrane and plasma membrane at physiologically relevant concentrations. CL comprises approximately 20% of the IMM phospholipid content and is essential for the curvature geometry of mitochondrial cristae, the assembly of ETC supercomplexes, and the anchoring of cytochrome c to the membrane surface. This CL enrichment — together with the large electrochemical gradient (−180 mV) across the IMM, generated by proton pumping during oxidative phosphorylation — produces a thermodynamic driving force that causes SS-31 to accumulate >1,000-fold within the IMM relative to the cytoplasm in cellular models [2, 4]. **(Evidence base: in-vitro cell models; no direct human intramitochondrial concentration measurements are available. The >1,000-fold figure is a cell-model benchmark, not a measured human value.)**

The binding interaction has been quantified at multiple levels of resolution. A 2020 JBC study (Mitchell et al.) in CL-containing liposomes and isolated mouse mitochondrial membranes quantified CL binding as saturable (6.9–7.4 lipid molecules per peptide for CL versus 15.4 for phosphatidylglycerol, indicating CL specificity), entropy-driven (ΔS > 0), and showed that the primary biophysical consequence is **controlled downward modulation of membrane surface electrostatics** rather than direct antioxidant quenching [4]. This electrostatic modulation is thought to restructure the local lipid environment around CL-anchored proteins, reducing membrane surface potential and thereby altering the conformation and activity of IMM-resident ETC complexes.

A 2020 PNAS cross-linking/mass spectrometry study (Chavez et al.) in aged mouse heart mitochondria (n=3 biological replicates) applied isotopic cross-linking to map 12 mitochondrial protein interactors of SS-31 [5]. Every interactor was a documented CL-binding protein, spanning ETC Complexes I, II, III, IV, and V (ATP synthase) and 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase, with cross-linked sites mapping proximal to known CL–protein interfaces. This establishes that SS-31's protein contacts are **secondary consequences of its CL co-localization** — the peptide does not directly bind the protein surface; it modifies the membrane lipid environment surrounding those proteins. **(Evidence base: aged mouse heart mitochondria, n=3 biological replicates; independent laboratory.)**

From the Birk 2013 JASN study in isolated rat ischemic mitochondria, the CL dissociation constant (K~D~) for SS-31 was measured at 1.87 ± 0.64 µM [6] — placing it in the low-µM affinity range appropriate for a membrane-embedded structural interaction rather than a tight enzyme-active-site binding event.

### 1.3 Cristae stabilization and cytochrome c protection

The physical chemistry of CL establishes why its integrity is load-bearing for mitochondrial function. CL's four acyl chains and unique cone geometry generate spontaneous negative curvature in membrane monolayers, which drives the extreme curvature of the cristae membrane folds — structures that vastly increase the surface area available for ETC complex assembly. CL also anchors cytochrome c to the IMM surface through an electrostatic interaction with its heme iron–methionine axial coordination. This anchoring holds cytochrome c in its **electron-carrier conformation**, where electron transfer is rapid and the heme iron is in the low-spin, catalytically electron-shuttling state.

During ischemia, oxidative stress, or Barth syndrome-associated CL remodeling defects, CL undergoes peroxidation of its acyl chains. CL peroxidation weakens the electrostatic anchoring of cytochrome c, disrupting the methionine–heme iron coordination and releasing cytochrome c into a **peroxidase conformation** in which the heme iron accepts H₂O₂ as a substrate rather than transferring electrons. In this peroxidase mode, cytochrome c amplifies lipid oxidation, further destroys the CL pool, and ultimately triggers apoptotic cytochrome c release from the mitochondria into the cytoplasm. This is a positive-feedback death cascade.

SS-31's proposed rescue mechanism interrupts this cascade at the CL level: by binding CL before it is peroxidized (or by competing with peroxidized-CL presentation to cytochrome c), SS-31 preserves heme iron–methionine coordination, maintains electron shuttling, prevents the peroxidase mode, and sustains cristae architecture. Szeto's 2014 Br J Pharmacol review synthesized the mechanistic model across preclinical studies: SS-31 protects CL structural role → maintains cristae organization → stabilizes ETC supercomplex assembly → improves oxidative phosphorylation efficiency → reduces electron leak and mitochondrial ROS [38].

Birk et al. (2013, JASN) provided the most direct demonstration in rat renal ischemia-reperfusion (1 mg/kg IV, male Sprague-Dawley, n=8 per group): SS-31 pretreatment prevented cytochrome c conversion to peroxidase; preserved cristae membrane ultrastructure on electron microscopy (saline controls showed threefold increase in organelle area with near-complete cristae loss by end of ischemia; SS-31-treated mitochondria preserved density and cristae architecture throughout); enabled rapid ATP recovery on reperfusion; reduced tubular apoptosis; and produced near-complete functional renal protection — serum creatinine 0.62 ± 0.09 vs 1.72 ± 0.55 mg/dL in saline controls at 24 hours post-reperfusion [6]. **(Evidence base: rat renal ischemia-reperfusion; not a human mechanistic study.)**

A mechanistic nuance established by Allen et al. (2020, Communications Biology) deserves explicit statement: elamipretide mitigated cristae network fragmentation after cardiac I/R in rats but did **not** protect against the reduction in total cardiolipin concentration after I/R [8]. The same was found in the tafazzin KO mouse model by Russo et al. (2022, Scientific Reports): SS-31 significantly restored respiratory supercomplex formation and oxidative phosphorylation efficiency in TazKD mice **without altering the pathological cardiolipin-to-monolysocardiolipin ratio** [9]. This is a structural clarification: SS-31 operates through cardiolipin's physicochemical role at the membrane, not by preventing CL catabolism or restoring CL content. The distinction matters for predicting which disease contexts will respond — CL content-restoration diseases (those with defective CL biosynthesis where quantity is depleted) are distinct from CL structural/functional-loss diseases (where CL is present but peroxidized or pathologically remodeled). Barth syndrome — tafazzin mutations alter CL acyl chain remodeling, not synthesis — is in the second category, which may partly explain why BTHS is the one indication where a clinical signal emerged.

### 1.4 ETC efficiency, ATP, and ROS: net bioenergetic consequences

Net bioenergetic consequences, established in animal and cell models:

**Improved electron flux through ETC supercomplex stabilization.** ETC Complexes I, III, and IV organize into higher-order supercomplexes (respirasomes) whose assembly depends on CL. CL loss or peroxidation causes supercomplex disassembly, forcing electron transfer through dissociated complexes where electron leak to molecular oxygen (generating superoxide) is substantially higher. SS-31's CL-binding preserves supercomplex integrity → reduces electron leak → increases the fraction of electrons completing the full four-electron reduction of O₂ to water → improves the P/O ratio (ATP molecules produced per oxygen atom consumed). This improvement in bioenergetic efficiency is the downstream consequence of the membrane-structural effect, not of a direct interaction with the catalytic subunits of ETC complexes.

**Reduced pathological ROS.** The primary ROS-reduction mechanism is reduced electron leak at Complexes I and III — the two principal sites of superoxide generation in the normal ETC. Dmt's intrinsic free-radical scavenging capacity (established by the SAR comparison with SS-20, which lacks Dmt and retains CL-binding but not antioxidant activity) represents a secondary, additive effect. In the presence of both mechanisms, the observed net ROS reduction in preclinical models is meaningful, but the structural (supercomplex stabilization) route should be considered primary based on the SS-20 pharmacology data [3].

**No effect in young/healthy mitochondria — a context-dependency rule.** The Chavez 2020 PNAS study found SS-31 improved respiration and reduced H₂O₂ production in aged mouse heart mitochondria but not in young controls — an age-dependent selectivity consistent with the mechanism targeting dysfunctional CL rather than augmenting normal CL function [5]. This finding has a direct practical implication: the predicted magnitude of SS-31 benefit scales with the degree of CL dysfunction at baseline. Young, healthy individuals with intact CL remodeling machinery are the population least likely to respond; individuals with confirmed CL pathology (Barth syndrome, severe aging-related mitochondrial dysfunction, acute ischemia-reperfusion insult) are the predicted responders. The clinical trial program's general failure in heterogeneous patient populations is consistent with this model — most enrolled patients probably did not have confirmed CL dysfunction at baseline.

**(Evidence base throughout this section: in-vitro and rodent animal models. Human clinical data address functional outcomes such as 6MWT and muscle strength; direct intramitochondrial ETC measurements, P/O ratios, or ROS production have not been measured in human subjects in the clinical trial program.)**

## 2. Human Clinical Trial Record

The human clinical program was conducted almost entirely by **Stealth BioTherapeutics** (now Mighty Therapeutics) across four indications: primary mitochondrial myopathy (PMM), Barth syndrome (BTHS), geographic atrophy (dry AMD), and cardiac diseases. The defining fact of this program is its uniform outcome: **every randomized, controlled trial missed its prespecified primary endpoint**. The FDA accelerated approval arose from the TAZPOWER open-label extension, not the controlled trial phase.

### 2.1 Primary Mitochondrial Myopathy (PMM)

**MMPOWER dose-escalation Phase 1/2 (NCT02367014):** 36 adults with genetically confirmed PMM randomized to three ascending IV doses (0.01, 0.10, 0.25 mg/kg/hr × 2 hr/day × 5 days) or placebo. 6MWT change: +13.5 m (low dose), +36.5 m (mid), +64.5 m (high dose), +20.4 m (placebo). Dose-response trend present but trial not powered for significance. Published Neurology 2018 (PMID 29500292) [13].

**MMPOWER-2 Phase 2 crossover (NCT02805790):** 30 adults with PMM. Four weeks elamipretide / 4-week washout / 4 weeks placebo. Mean 6MWT: 394.1 m (elamipretide) vs 378.2 m (placebo), a 15.9 m numerical difference; p-values not reported. No peer-reviewed primary publication identified. Results posted July 2020 to ClinicalTrials.gov [14].

**MMPOWER-3 Phase 3 (NCT03323749) — PRIMARY ENDPOINT MISSED, TRIAL TERMINATED EARLY:**

The pivotal Phase 3 trial randomized 218 adults with genetically confirmed PMM 1:1 to 40 mg SC elamipretide daily or placebo for 24 weeks. Co-primary endpoints: (1) change from baseline in 6MWT distance and (2) change in PMMSA total fatigue score at week 24.

Results at week 24:
- 6MWT: elamipretide +15.33 m (±61.49) vs placebo +17.39 m (±51.70) — **placebo numerically superior**
- PMMSA total fatigue: elamipretide −1.18 (±2.13) vs placebo −1.09 (±2.44) — near-identical

Neither co-primary endpoint was met. Trial terminated early on failure. Published Neurology 2023;101(3):e238–e252 (PMID 37268435) [15].

A post-hoc genotype subgroup analysis identified a nominally significant 6MWT improvement in patients with nuclear DNA mutations (particularly POLG and TWNK mtDNA replisome defects), suggesting the basket design combining mtDNA and nDNA patients may have obscured a genotype-restricted signal [16]. This finding generated the NuPOWER hypothesis (see §2.5).

### 2.2 Barth Syndrome

**TAZPOWER Phase 2/3 crossover (NCT03098797) — CONTROLLED PHASE MISSED PRIMARY ENDPOINTS:**

12 males with genetically confirmed BTHS enrolled in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover: two 12-week treatment periods (40 mg SC elamipretide daily) separated by a 4-week washout. Co-primary endpoints: 6MWT and BTHS Symptom Assessment (BTHS-SA) total fatigue score at each 12-week period.

Controlled crossover results: 6MWT improved ~43 m in both arms (elamipretide: 400.1→443.1 m; placebo: 412.6→443.9 m); BTHS-SA fatigue improved ~1.4 points in both arms. No between-group difference on either primary endpoint [17]. Results posted ClinicalTrials.gov April 2024.

**TAZPOWER Open-Label Extension (OLE) — the basis for FDA approval:**

10 participants entered the OLE; 8 completed week 168. At week 168:
- **Knee extensor muscle strength:** median change +63 N (range 38–78 N) from an OLE baseline of 124 N. *This is the endpoint on which FDA accelerated approval rested.*
- **6MWT:** cumulative improvement from OLE baseline +96.1 m (P=0.003).
- **BTHS-SA fatigue:** scores remained below OLE baseline throughout.
- **Cardiolipin ratio (MLCL/CL):** improved and correlated with functional outcomes.
- Safety: injection-site reactions dominant; 2 discontinuations due to ISRs; no new systemic safety signals over 168 weeks [18].

A natural-history control comparison (Hornby et al., Orphanet J Rare Dis 2022; TAZPOWER OLE n=8 vs 19 untreated historical controls) found a 79.7 m 6MWT advantage (P=0.0004) and 56.7 N muscle strength advantage (P=0.0002) at week 64 [19]. This is a non-randomized historical control analysis subject to selection bias; it is not an RCT.

**FDA accelerated approval (NDA 215244, October 30, 2025):** On the basis of the OLE knee-extensor-strength gain — median +63 N at week 168 — accepted as an intermediate clinical endpoint, elamipretide received accelerated approval for improvement of muscle strength in adult and pediatric Barth syndrome patients weighing ≥30 kg at 40 mg SC once daily [1]. Continued approval contingent on a confirmatory trial (4TAZPower, NCT07531251). In October 2024, the FDA Cardiovascular and Renal Drugs Advisory Committee voted 10–6 that elamipretide was effective for Barth syndrome [20].

### 2.3 Geographic Atrophy / Dry AMD

**ReCLAIM Phase 1 (NCT02848313):** Single-center, open-label, uncontrolled Phase 1. 40 mg SC QD in intermediate AMD subjects; primary endpoint safety and tolerability (met). Exploratory signals in low-luminance visual function [21, 22]. These uncontrolled data are not interpretable as efficacy evidence.

**ReCLAIM-2 Phase 2 (NCT03891875) — PRIMARY ENDPOINTS MISSED:**

176 subjects with non-central geographic atrophy (117 elamipretide, 59 placebo) randomized; 134 completed. 40 mg SC daily for 48 weeks. Co-primary endpoints: (1) change in low-luminance BCVA from baseline to week 48; (2) change in GA area by OCT from baseline to week 48.

Results at week 48:
- LL BCVA: elamipretide −2.8 letters vs placebo −4.6 letters (numerically favored active arm; not significant)
- GA area: elamipretide +0.328 mm² vs placebo +0.281 mm² (elamipretide showed numerically larger atrophy growth)

Neither co-primary endpoint reached statistical significance. A notable exploratory signal was 43% reduction in macular ellipsoid zone attenuation/loss progression in the elamipretide arm — not a prespecified primary endpoint. Published Ophthalmology Science 2024 (PMID 39605874) [23]. A Phase 3 trial (ReNEW, NCT06373731) is active but not yet reporting.

### 2.4 Cardiac Indications

**EMBRACE-STEMI Phase 2a (NCT01572909) — PRIMARY ENDPOINT MISSED:**

300 first-time anterior STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI randomized to IV elamipretide (MTP-131/Bendavia) or placebo (243 completers). Primary endpoint: CK-MB AUC over 72 hours post-PCI.

Results: CK-MB AUC 5,570 ± 486 (elamipretide) vs 5,785 ± 426 ng·hr/mL (placebo), p=NS. MRI infarct size at Day 30 was numerically worse in the active arm (infarct size ratio 242.3 vs 225.2). One secondary outcome showed a numerically favorable but non-significant signal: incident heart failure within 24 hours occurred in 14% of the elamipretide arm vs 25% of placebo — a directionally positive but non-significant difference that does not alter the primary endpoint failure. Published European Heart Journal 2016 (PMID 26586786) [24].

**SPIHF-201 / PROGRESS-HF Phase 2 HFrEF (NCT02788747) — PRIMARY ENDPOINT NOT MET:**

71 subjects with stable HFrEF randomized to elamipretide 4 mg (n=23), 40 mg (n=24), or placebo (n=24) SC for 28 days. Primary endpoint: change in LVESV by MRI from baseline to week 4. Results: LVESV change −4.4 mL (4 mg), −1.2 mL (40 mg), −3.8 mL (placebo) — no dose-response, small overlapping differences. P-values not reported in the registry; a peer-reviewed publication (PMID 32068002) confirmed no significant between-arm difference [25].

**Daubert Phase 2 HFrEF (NCT02388464):** Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 2 in 71 HFrEF subjects (LVEF ≤40%), 40 mg SC × 28 days. Sponsor-funded; primary endpoint not met [26]. Funded by Stealth BioTherapeutics; multiple authors received consulting fees/salary support from sponsor.

**Heart failure (congestion / NT-proBNP) Phase 2 (NCT02914665):** 308 subjects hospitalized with congestion. Primary endpoint: change in NT-proBNP Day 8/early discharge. No results posted to ClinicalTrials.gov as of June 2026 [29].

**Renal artery stenosis Phase 2a (NCT01755858) — independent:**

The one clear independent RCT: Mayo Clinic renal artery stenosis study (Saad et al., Circ Cardiovasc Interv 2017; PMID 28916603), NIH-funded, no Stealth involvement. Evaluated elamipretide during stent revascularization in atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis [30].

### 2.5 Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON) — ophthalmic route

Karanjia et al. (Ophthalmology 2024, PMID 37923251) conducted a randomized trial of elamipretide topical ophthalmic solution for LHON, a mitochondrial disease caused by mtDNA mutations affecting Complex I. This is one of the few trials using a route other than SC injection; topical ophthalmic delivery of a mitochondria-targeting compound to the retinal ganglion cells (the primary LHON target). The paper is published (Karanjia R et al., Ophthalmology 2024, PMID 37923251); results are available in the primary source. The trial is classified here under Stealth-sponsored trials [reference in §5.2 trial table].

### 2.6 Older adult skeletal muscle — Phase 1 IV study

Zhu et al. (Aging Cell 2021; PMID 8282018, PMC8282018) conducted an open-label human Phase 1 study using IV elamipretide (0.25 mg/kg/hr × 2 hr, the highest Phase 1 dose used in preclinical-to-human translation) in older adults [35]. The primary finding relevant to PK: in vivo mitochondrial ATP production (ATPmax) measured by ³¹P MRS in skeletal muscle improved after a single dose and decayed with an estimated functional half-life of ~16 hours — the basis for the half-life figure cited in §4.3. This study was exploratory with a small n and cannot be interpreted as efficacy evidence, but it provides the only published human data point on intramuscular mitochondrial function in response to elamipretide.

### 2.7 NuPOWER Phase 3 (NCT05162768)

Phase 3 RCT in primary mitochondrial disease from nuclear DNA mutations (102 participants enrolled; Stealth-sponsored). The hypothesis arose from the MMPOWER-3 post-hoc subgroup analysis showing numerically better 6MWT outcomes in the nDNA subgroup (particularly POLG and TWNK mtDNA replisome defects). NuPOWER specifically enrolled patients with pathogenic nuclear DNA mutations — attempting to test the genetic-selection hypothesis at Phase 3 scale. Primary completion September 30, 2024; final completion December 4, 2024. Results not published as of June 2026 [31]. Whether NuPOWER represents a disciplined test of the genotype-selection hypothesis or a narrowed rescue attempt after MMPOWER-3's failure will be determined by its results; the answer matters for the translational gap narrative in §3.7.

### 2.8 Pooled and meta-analytic evidence

No published meta-analysis or pooled analysis of elamipretide RCTs has been identified. The manufacturer cited exploratory pooled analyses in regulatory and investor communications, but these do not constitute independent peer-reviewed evidence and are not used as grounds for any claim in this entry.

## 3. Preclinical Evidence and the Translation Gap

### 3.1 Cardiac ischemia-reperfusion — rat

Allen, Pennington, Perry et al. (2020, Communications Biology; PMID 32680996) applied serial block-face scanning electron microscopy to a rat cardiac I/R model — an independent lab (not the Szeto laboratory) — showing that elamipretide mitigated cristae network fragmentation after cardiac I/R and improved respiratory supercomplex coupling, while **notably not protecting against the reduction in total cardiolipin concentration** after I/R. This mechanistic nuance — structural preservation is separable from lipid quantity — was later replicated in the tafazzin KO mouse model [8]. Published in a Nature Portfolio journal [8].

### 3.2 Cardiac ischemia-reperfusion — swine (independent)

Patel, Johnson, Vapniarsky et al. (2023, Scientific Reports; PMID 36934127) tested elamipretide in a swine hemorrhagic shock + aortic occlusion model (Yorkshire swine, 59–84 kg; n=6 per group) with high translational face validity. Results: reduced crystalloid fluid requirements (25 vs 62.5 mL/kg; p=0.01), reduced creatinine (2.4 vs 2.7 mg/dL; p=0.04), and dramatically reduced cardiac troponin release (0.22 vs 3.20 ng/mL; p=0.03) [7]. Authors declared commercial interests in Certus Critical Care but no Szeto affiliation — a substantive independent replication in a clinically relevant species.

### 3.3 Aging: skeletal muscle — mouse (independent)

Siegel, Kruse, Percival, Goh et al. (2013, Aging Cell; PMID 23692570) — Rabinovitch/Marcinek laboratory at University of Washington, independent of Szeto — showed a single SS-31 injection in 27-month-old C57BL/6 mice (n=5–9 per group) rapidly restored in vivo mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupling (P/O ratio fully restored), reduced H₂O₂ emission, and improved fatigue resistance within 1–8 days [10].

### 3.4 Aging: cardiac function — mouse (independent)

Chiao et al. (2020, eLife; PMID 32574516) — Rabinovitch laboratory, Seattle — treated 24-month-old C57BL/6 mice (n=7–10 for echocardiography) with SS-31 at 3 µg/g/day via osmotic minipump for 8 weeks. Results: significant reversal of diastolic dysfunction (E/A ratio restored), normalized proton leak, reduced mitochondrial superoxide. Systolic function (fractional shortening) unchanged — **selective diastolic benefit**. Exercise capacity improved significantly [11]. A long-term 18–28 month treatment study (Nickel et al. 2022, Aging Pathobiol Ther; PMID 36250163) showed sex-specific benefits: males — enhanced diastolic function, grip strength, rotarod; females — improved spatial learning and body composition stabilization [12].

### 3.5 Barth syndrome mouse model

Russo, De Rasmo, Signorile et al. (2022, Scientific Reports; PMID 36400945) used tafazzin knockdown mice (TazKD; WT n=9, TazKD untreated n=9, TazKD+SS-31 n=4) to show SS-31 significantly restored state 3 and state 4 respiration, promoted respiratory supercomplex formation (SC2 and SC4), and improved oxidative phosphorylation efficiency — **without altering the pathological cardiolipin-to-monolysocardiolipin ratio** [9]. This separates functional rescue from lipid remodeling and is directly relevant to the Barth syndrome OLE signal.

### 3.6 Independent neuroinflammation model (Chinese group)

Li et al. (J Neuroinflammation 2019; PMID 31727138) — Chinese PLA General Hospital / Beijing Institute of Pharmacology, NSFC-funded, no Stealth involvement, no competing interests declared — showed elamipretide improves mitochondrial dysfunction, synaptic, and memory impairment induced by LPS in mice [32]. This is the clearest example of fully independent preclinical replication in a non-cardiac, non-Szeto context.

### 3.7 The translation gap — five explanations and their evidential weight

The discrepancy between robust, replicated preclinical benefit across at least five independent indications and three species and predominantly null Phase 2/3 clinical results is the central tension of the elamipretide story. Unlike drugs whose preclinical promise was driven by a single lab with undisclosed conflicts, SS-31's preclinical replication across independent institutions makes a simple artifact explanation implausible. The translation gap is a genuine scientific problem, not a credibility failure. Five explanations have been proposed in peer-reviewed commentaries, with different evidential weights:

**1. Trial duration mismatch.** Preclinical aging studies required 8 weeks to 10 months of continuous treatment to show meaningful cardiac or skeletal muscle functional changes; the Siegel 2013 aging muscle study showed improvement within 1–8 days, but the Chiao 2020 cardiac aging study used 8 weeks [10, 11]. Human clinical trials used 4–28 weeks of double-blind treatment. If mitochondrial structural remodeling — supercomplex reassembly, cristae geometry restoration — requires sustained CL stabilization over months to manifest as measurable human functional change, then the 24-week MMPOWER-3 and 12-week TAZPOWER crossover designs may simply have been too short. The OLE signal emerging after 168 weeks is the strongest empirical support for this hypothesis — but the absence of a control arm over that period means the temporal pattern could reflect natural history rather than treatment duration. Three specific confound mechanisms standard in uncontrolled OLE-based approvals apply here: **practice effect** (repeated testing improves performance independently of treatment), **regression to the mean** (patients enrolled at a functional nadir tend to improve regardless of intervention), and **survivor/selection bias** (the OLE includes only the 8 of 10 entrants who tolerated treatment and continued — a systematically healthier subset). Any one of these could produce the observed 168-week gain without causal treatment effect; all three operating simultaneously cannot be excluded. *Evidential weight: moderate; consistent with OLE data but confounded by lack of control and these unresolved alternative explanations.*

**2. Endpoint and population heterogeneity.** MMPOWER-3's basket design enrolled a genetically heterogeneous population whose mitochondrial disease etiologies varied substantially — mtDNA mutations affecting structural components of ETC complexes, nuclear DNA mutations affecting replication/maintenance machinery, heteroplasmy at different thresholds, and different organ involvement patterns. The post-hoc nDNA subgroup signal (particularly POLG and TWNK) suggests that mtDNA heteroplasmy level and the specific nature of the mitochondrial defect critically determine whether residual CL function is the limiting factor — information neither obtainable nor used at enrollment in MMPOWER-3. Animal models are genetically uniform, with a single defined mitochondrial insult, in a background free of comorbidities. Barth syndrome, where the CL remodeling defect is uniform across the enrolled population and confirmed at enrollment, is the exception that proves this rule — and it produced the only sustained clinical signal. *Evidential weight: strong; the BTHS vs. PMM contrast is the cleanest natural experiment supporting this hypothesis.*

**3. Guideline-directed therapy pharmacological masking.** Human HF, PMM, and AMD patients receive extensive background therapies. HFrEF patients in SPIHF-201 and the Daubert trial were on contemporary medical therapy including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and increasingly SGLT2 inhibitors — each with documented effects on mitochondrial stress pathways, autophagy, and cardiac energetics. These agents may have already occupied a therapeutic window that SS-31 could otherwise open, compressing the detectable increment to below trial sensitivity. Animal models of HF do not replicate this pharmacological milieu. *Evidential weight: moderate; coherent mechanistically but difficult to test experimentally in humans without concomitant-medication-stratified trials.*

**4. Bioenergetic context-dependency — mitochondrial rescue is necessary but not sufficient.** A 2026 IJMS study in the HFpEF obese ZSF1 rat model found modest increases in complex I/II-linked mitochondrial respiration after elamipretide treatment without translating into diastolic performance improvement, in a model where fibrosis and myocardial hypertrophy are co-dominant [33]. This raises a fundamental boundary condition: SS-31's mechanism rescues the bioenergetic component of mitochondrial dysfunction, but if the structural disease substrate (fibrosis, cardiomyocyte dropout, adverse remodeling) has advanced beyond the point where bioenergetic improvement can restore meaningful function, the treatment cannot reverse those changes. The preclinical cardiac I/R models — acute ischemia with an otherwise healthy myocardium — do not replicate the structural pathology of chronic human HF phenotypes. The Barth syndrome exception again fits: BTHS cardiomyopathy is primarily metabolic (CL remodeling defect), with less structural fibrosis early in disease, making bioenergetic rescue more likely to translate. *Evidential weight: moderate-strong; supported by the HFpEF rat data and mechanistically coherent.*

**5. Biomarker gap — no enrollment biomarker for CL dysfunction.** FDA has explicitly requested validation of plasma cardiolipin as a companion diagnostic during regulatory discussions. Without a confirmed biomarker of CL dysfunction at enrollment, clinical trials necessarily include patients whose cardiac, skeletal muscle, or retinal dysfunction is driven by non-CL pathways that SS-31 does not target — diluting the signal among enrolled patients. If, hypothetically, 30–40% of PMM trial enrollees had CL dysfunction as the primary functional bottleneck, a trial sized for the full population would be dramatically underpowered for the true responder subgroup. The correlation between cardiolipin ratio (MLCL/CL) improvement and functional outcomes in the BTHS OLE [18] is the strongest available evidence that CL biomarker-guided selection could improve signal detection in future trials. *Evidential weight: mechanistically strong; lacks a prospective trial test.*

**What the gap does not prove.** The translation gap does not prove that SS-31 is ineffective in humans. It establishes that current trial designs, in the current enrolled populations, with current trial durations, have not produced a controlled positive result (except the BTHS OLE, which is uncontrolled). Five plausible and non-mutually-exclusive explanations exist. Whether those explanations are correct will be answered — partially — by the NuPOWER Phase 3 results and the BTHS confirmatory trial.

## 4. Safety, Dosing, Route, PK, Regulatory Status, and Monitoring

### 4.1 Regulatory status

**FDA:** Forzinity (elamipretide hydrochloride injection) received FDA accelerated approval on October 30, 2025 (NDA 215244) for the indication: *improvement of muscle strength in adult and pediatric patients with Barth syndrome weighing at least 30 kg* [1]. Accelerated approval was based on knee extensor muscle strength as an intermediate clinical endpoint; continued approval requires confirmatory trial completion. An initial NDA was refused to file in October 2021. A CRL was issued (exact date not primary-verified; May 2025 cited in secondary sources but unconfirmed against a primary FDA document). The January 29, 2024 resubmission was accepted April 2024; the Advisory Committee voted 10–6 for effectiveness in October 2024; accelerated approval followed October 30, 2025 [1, 20].

**WADA status:** Elamipretide exits the S0 (Non-Approved Substances) category upon October 2025 FDA approval and is not named in S2 or any other WADA category. WADA's S0 language covers substances with "no current approval by any governmental regulatory health authority for human therapeutic use" — elamipretide no longer meets this criterion [27]. The live 2026 WADA Prohibited List URL was inaccessible at research time; athletes must verify via GlobalDRO.com and their national anti-doping authority. An athlete with confirmed Barth syndrome using Forzinity as prescribed would likely qualify for a Therapeutic Use Exemption.

**Compounding status:** Since FDA approval of Forzinity, elamipretide as a component of an approved drug can in principle be used as a 503A compounding ingredient, subject to state pharmacy laws and clinical need. Off-label compounding for anti-aging, performance, or cardiac indications remains outside any approved framework [34].

### 4.2 Dosing and route

- **Approved dose (Barth syndrome):** 40 mg SC injection once daily; weight threshold ≥30 kg [1].
- **Renal dose adjustment:** no adjustment for mild (eGFR 60–89) or moderate (eGFR 30–59) renal impairment; reduce by 50% (20 mg SC QD) for severe impairment (eGFR <30, not on dialysis) [1].
- **MMPOWER-3 and ReCLAIM-2:** 40 mg SC QD (the uniformly adopted Phase 3 dose) [15, 23].
- **Early cardiac work:** IV formulations at 0.05 mg/kg/hr (EMBRACE-STEMI) and 0.25 mg/kg/hr × 2 hr (Phase 1 IV skeletal muscle study); IV became obsolete as SC was adopted for Phase 2/3 [24, 35].
- **Grey-market dosing:** not addressable by verified sources; no vendor-label dose number is grounded in this entry.

**Injection-site reactions (ISRs) are the dominant adverse effect class across all programs.** Systemic tolerability is excellent; no cardiovascular safety signal identified above placebo rates in any cardiac trial program.

### 4.5 Contraindications, warnings, and monitoring

**Contraindications [1]:** Serious hypersensitivity to elamipretide or any Forzinity excipient.

**Warnings and precautions [1]:**
1. **Hypersensitivity reactions:** Including serious allergic responses with rash, papular lesions, and respiratory symptoms. May occur within minutes to months after initiation. Monitor for signs.
2. **Benzyl alcohol toxicity:** Forzinity contains 20 mg/mL benzyl alcohol as preservative; relevant consideration for compounded formulations without preservative-free designation (neonatal concern not applicable at ≥30 kg threshold).

**Transient eosinophilia:** Mean peak increase 0.5–0.6 × 10³/µL at ~90 days, resolving within 6–12 months [1]. No specific stopping threshold defined in the prescribing information; clinical judgment guides management.

**Monitoring framework:**
- Assess injection sites at each visit; document ISR severity and type
- Monitor for hypersensitivity (rash, urticaria, respiratory symptoms) especially in first weeks
- Periodic eosinophil count at ≥30-day intervals
- eGFR at baseline and periodically; dose reduce if eGFR falls below 30 mL/min
- No routine cardiac monitoring specified; cardiovascular safety well-characterized without signal

**Stopping criteria:**
- Serious hypersensitivity reaction → discontinue immediately
- Intolerable ISRs unresponsive to site rotation and local measures → consider discontinuation
- Severe renal impairment development during treatment → dose reduce by 50%, do not stop

## 5. Concentration, COI, and Sourcing Realism

### 5.1 Inventor origin and COI — first-class

SS-31 was invented by Hazel H. Szeto (Weill Cornell Medicine) and Peter W. Schiller (IRCM, Montreal), with the compound named for their initials. The foundational 2004 JBC paper was published by Zhao, Szeto et al. [2]. Patent prosecution was handled by the Cornell Research Foundation; the compound was licensed exclusively to Stealth Peptides Inc. (later Stealth BioTherapeutics) in 2006 when Szeto co-founded the company.

Szeto's financial conflict is explicit in every mechanism paper she co-authored: *"The SS peptides described in this article are licensed for commercial research and development to Stealth Peptides Inc., a clinical stage biopharmaceutical company, in which H.H.S. and the Cornell Research Foundation have financial interests."* This COI is disclosed but dense — it means the inventor and the sole commercial sponsor are the same entity, generating the mechanism papers and the clinical trials simultaneously.

### 5.2 Single-sponsor concentration — ≥90% — why it matters

The COI concern in elamipretide's case is not the ordinary "industry-funded RCT" caution that applies broadly to pharmaceutical research. It is structurally unusual for two reasons. First, the inventor and the sole commercial sponsor are the same entity: Szeto co-founded the company formed to commercialize her own invention, and her laboratory at Cornell continued producing mechanism papers throughout the clinical program while she held equity in Stealth BioTherapeutics. This creates a scenario where mechanism papers — which set the scientific framing for clinical trial design — were produced by a financial stakeholder in the clinical program's commercial outcome. Second, there is no counter-weight: no independent academic group ran a competing clinical program, no academic investigator-initiated IND was filed, and no large NIH cooperative group trial of elamipretide was funded. The entire human evidence base rests on a single commercial actor's decisions about which indications to pursue, which endpoints to power, and which results to publish. The scientific community evaluating elamipretide has had only one clinical source of data.

This does not make the data fraudulent. Every trial was registered, published in peer-reviewed journals (Neurology, European Heart Journal, Genetics in Medicine, Ophthalmology Science), and subject to FDA review. The preclinical replication by independent labs (Rabinovitch/Marcinek UW, Allen et al., Patel et al., Russo et al., Li et al.) is genuine and not Szeto-affiliated. The concern is interpretive: in a field where the only clinical sponsor has simultaneous commercial incentive to produce positive results and to select trial designs that give the therapy its best chance, the absence of a positive result after five Phase 2/3 trials is more informative than it would be in a program with multiple independent sponsors who could have pursued divergent hypotheses.

A systematic survey of 19+ indexed PubMed clinical-trial publications yields the following breakdown:

| Trial / Study | Sponsor |
|---|---|
| MMPOWER dose-escalation Phase 1/2 | Stealth BioTherapeutics |
| MMPOWER-2 Phase 2 crossover | Stealth BioTherapeutics |
| MMPOWER-3 Phase 3 PMM | Stealth BioTherapeutics |
| MMPOWER-3 post-hoc genotype subgroup | Stealth BioTherapeutics |
| NuPOWER Phase 3 nDNA PMD | Stealth BioTherapeutics |
| TAZPOWER Barth Phase 2/3 (crossover + OLE) | Stealth BioTherapeutics |
| TAZPOWER 168-week OLE | Stealth BioTherapeutics |
| Natural-history control comparator | Stealth BioTherapeutics (Hornby/Thompson) |
| Daubert 2017 HFrEF Phase 2 | Stealth BioTherapeutics* |

| LHON ophthalmic RCT | Stealth BioTherapeutics |
| ReCLAIM / ReCLAIM-2 AMD Phase 1/2 | Stealth BioTherapeutics |
| **Saad 2017 renal artery stenosis Phase 2a** | **Independent (NIH / Mayo Clinic)** |

**Concentration figure: at least 12 of 13 identifiable sponsored trials (≥90%) were Stealth-sponsored or Stealth-funded.** The sole clear exception is the Mayo Clinic renal artery stenosis study (NIH-funded, no Stealth involvement) [30]. No independent academic group has run a fully independent RCT of elamipretide in any indication.

At the preclinical layer, independent labs have meaningfully replicated SS-31 effects: the Rabinovitch/Marcinek group (University of Washington, aging skeletal muscle and cardiac aging), the Allen et al. Communications Biology group (rat cardiac I/R cristae imaging), the Patel et al. Scientific Reports swine group, the Russo/Scientific Reports tafazzin KO group, and the Li et al./Chinese PLA neuroinflammation group. Preclinical replication is broader than clinical, but the clinical evidence base remains **entirely single-sponsor-dominated with one exception.**

### 5.3 Commercialization trajectory

- **2004:** Foundational SS-31 publication (Zhao/Szeto, JBC 2004) [2].
- **2006:** Stealth Peptides Inc. founded; Cornell license executed.
- **2020:** MMPOWER-3 Phase 3 fails both co-primary endpoints; trial terminated early [15].
- **2021–2024:** NDA refused to file (2021); resubmitted (January 2024); FDA accepted for review (April 2024); Advisory Committee voted 10–6 for Barth syndrome effectiveness (October 2024).
- **2025 (Oct 30):** FDA grants accelerated approval — Forzinity (elamipretide HCl) for BTHS ≥30 kg [1]. First mitochondria-targeting drug approved; first approved treatment for Barth syndrome. Barth syndrome affects fewer than 200 individuals in the United States; the commercial market is ultra-rare.
- **2025–2026:** Company operating under name Mighty Therapeutics (rebrand; exact date not confirmed against primary source). Pipeline: BTHS confirmatory post-marketing trial + NuPOWER nDNA program (results pending).
- **Key uncertainty:** Stealth BioTherapeutics' domain is parked, suggesting prior operational cessation; the successor entity Mighty Therapeutics is the current clinical sponsor. NuPOWER Phase 3 primary completion September 2024; results not published as of June 2026.

### 5.4 Sourcing realism

**Clinical-grade material (Forzinity):** subcutaneous injectable manufactured under pharmaceutical GMP; available only via specialty pharmacy with a valid prescription for the approved BTHS indication. No approved access path for off-label indications.

**Grey-market "SS-31":** Multiple research-chemical vendors sell peptides labeled "SS-31 / Elamipretide" for "research use only." At least one vendor (Novopro Labs) lists the product as "discontinued due to IP conflict," acknowledging Mighty Therapeutics' patent estate [28]. Vendor CoA claims and third-party testing assertions are unverifiable from the outside. SS-31 is a sequence-sensitive tetrapeptide where aggregation, oxidation, and impurities from substandard synthesis are real quality risks; no GMP, sterility, or identity guarantees apply to grey-market material. No published data exist on grey-market SS-31 bioavailability or safety in humans. Claims that grey-market SS-31 is equivalent to clinical elamipretide are not supportable.

### 5.6 Off-label use in the performance-enhancement and longevity context

Grey-market use of "SS-31" for anti-aging, longevity, and athletic performance is a real pattern in peptide self-administration communities, driven by several extrapolations from the legitimate scientific record.

The aging-muscle animal data — Siegel 2013 (Aging Cell), Chiao 2020 (eLife), and the Nickel 2022 long-term treatment study — showing improved mitochondrial coupling, reduced H₂O₂ emission, restored fatigue resistance, and reversed diastolic dysfunction in aged mice is scientifically robust and independently replicated. The extrapolation to human anti-aging performance benefit is logically connected but empirically untested. The "first mitochondria drug" framing following FDA approval creates a halo-effect interest in off-label use even though the approval is specific to an ultra-rare monogenic CL disease where mechanistic target engagement is confirmed at enrollment. The Chiao 2020 eLife diastolic reversal data is particularly attractive to the longevity community because diastolic dysfunction is near-universal in cardiac aging and a predictor of future HF — but that study was in 24-month-old C57BL/6 mice with established age-related CL dysfunction, not young healthy adults.

What the evidence does NOT support for off-label performance/longevity use:

- **No human dose-response relationship.** The 40 mg SC QD approved for BTHS was selected on regulatory grounds from a specific diseased population; its relevance to a healthy aging adult without CL dysfunction is unknown.
- **The "no effect in young/healthy mitochondria" finding is a direct warning.** Chavez 2020 PNAS showed SS-31 does not improve respiration in young mouse mitochondria — only in aged controls [5]. Benefit requires dysfunctional CL as a substrate.
- **Five failed RCTs included patients with presumed mitochondrial dysfunction; no controlled benefit.** A healthy aging adult without confirmed CL pathology is further from the responder profile than enrolled HFrEF or PMM patients.
- **No long-term human safety data.** The clinical program's safety data reflects a maximum of 168 weeks in a small BTHS OLE cohort and 24 weeks in the MMPOWER-3 population. Chronic effects in healthy individuals are unstudied.

The practical reality: grey-market "SS-31" is unverified as to identity and purity; is administered at doses with no human PK basis; is used in a biological context (healthy or mildly aging adult) where the mechanism predicts the least benefit; and is subject to no monitoring framework for known risks (ISRs, transient eosinophilia, hypersensitivity).

## 6. Bottom Line

Elamipretide is the **most clinically advanced mitochondria-targeting compound ever developed**, with the genuine distinction of being the first FDA-approved drug in this mechanistic class — grounded in a replicated mechanistic story across multiple independent laboratories. The honest assessment:

1. **Every controlled trial missed its primary endpoint.** Across MMPOWER-3 (n=218, Phase 3), TAZPOWER crossover (n=12), EMBRACE-STEMI (n=300), PROGRESS-HF/Daubert (n=71 each), and ReCLAIM-2 (n=176), the prespecified primary endpoint was not met in any randomized trial. The placebo arm numerically outperformed elamipretide on 6MWT in MMPOWER-3. This is not a marginal failure record; it is uniform across five distinct indications, three distinct routes, and two decades of development. Add to this that the sponsor was optimally motivated to design these trials favorably (single-sponsor concentration), and the uniform negative result is more informative than it would be from a diverse-sponsor program.

2. **The FDA approval rested on uncontrolled OLE data in 8 patients.** The accelerated approval is real and reflects genuine regulatory confidence in the mechanism, particularly given Barth syndrome's primary CL defect, and in the safety profile. The 10–6 Advisory Committee vote also reflects genuine clinical uncertainty about the controlled trial data versus the OLE signal. But OLE data from 8 completing patients cannot establish causality; the accelerated pathway explicitly requires a confirmatory trial and explicitly acknowledges that continued approval is contingent on that trial's outcome. An accelerated approval is a regulatory decision that a drug may provide benefit for a serious condition — not a determination that benefit has been proven. The distinction matters for off-label extrapolation.

3. **The preclinical base is strong and multi-lab replicated.** Unlike many novel peptides where the entire evidence base originates from one lab, SS-31's animal data includes independent replications in aging muscle (Rabinovitch/Marcinek, UW), cardiac aging (Rabinovitch, eLife), cardiac I/R cristae imaging (Allen et al., Communications Biology, Nature Portfolio), swine hemorrhagic shock (Patel et al., Scientific Reports), tafazzin KO mice (Russo et al., Scientific Reports), and neuroinflammation (Li et al., J Neuroinflammation, NSFC-funded). This is a more robust preclinical foundation than most drugs with a comparable clinical failure record.

4. **The ≥90% single-sponsor concentration is a first-class caveat.** An inventor who co-founded the sole commercial sponsor generated the mechanism papers; the same company funded every clinical trial except one. This does not make the data fraudulent — it makes every effect estimate from this program subject to higher-than-usual independent-replication criteria that have not yet been met at the human level.

5. **Barth syndrome is the best-supported human indication** — because mechanistic target engagement (tafazzin → primary CL remodeling defect) is confirmed at enrollment, consistent with the hypothesis that the translation gap is largely a patient-selection problem. The OLE signal in this uniquely targeted population is the strongest human evidence in the program and the appropriate reason for the accelerated approval.

6. **Off-label use (anti-aging, performance enhancement, cardiac protection) has no adequate evidence base.** The clinical failure in large, less-selected populations means no dosing, timing, or population rule can be extrapolated from the RCT failures for off-label optimization. A thoughtful clinician supervising off-label SS-31 use has no trial-derived dose-response relationship, no confirmed biomarker of engagement, and a safety profile established only in the SC injectable pharmaceutical-grade formulation — none of which applies to grey-market material.
