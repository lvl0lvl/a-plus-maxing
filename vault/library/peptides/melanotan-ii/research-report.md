# Melanotan-II (MT-II)

## Metadata

- **class:** peptide (synthetic cyclic melanocortin-receptor agonist; non-selective; University of Arizona origin)
- **evidence_tier:** C — Human tanning evidence is a single n=3 open-label pilot. Erectile evidence is three small double-blind RCTs (n=10, 20, 10) from a single PI group at a single institution with no independent replication. No Phase 3 trial exists. All formal clinical investigation was abandoned circa 2000.
- **risk_tier:** experimental — Never approved anywhere; active regulatory warnings in US, UK, Australia, EU; a multi-source dermatologic signal (eruptive/dysplastic nevi + temporally-associated melanoma cases) that is biologically coherent; serious safety signals including idiosyncratic organ-system events (rhabdomyolysis, renal infarction, PRES), documented hypertensive crisis in apparently healthy young adults, and life-threatening metabolic crisis in T1DM patients; common dose-dependent nausea/vomiting/flushing; priapism risk. WADA status unconfirmed for explicit S2 listing — verify with NADO.
- **status:** researching
- **last_verified:** 2026-06-21

## Summary

Melanotan-II (MT-II; Ac-Nle-cyclo[Asp-His-D-Phe-Arg-Trp-Lys]-NH₂; molecular weight ~1024.2 Da; C₅₀H₆₉N₁₅O₉) is a synthetic cyclic lactam heptapeptide analog of endogenous α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH), designed and synthesized at the University of Arizona by Mac Hadley and Victor Hruby in the late 1980s [3, open_label]. The defining pharmacological property of MT-II is **non-selective melanocortin receptor agonism**: it activates MC1R, MC3R, MC4R, and MC5R with high potency across all four subtypes [6, mechanism_review]. This is not a quibble about receptor selectivity — it is the single most important characterization of the compound, and it governs everything that follows: the tanning effects (MC1R), the erectogenic and appetite-suppressing effects (MC4R), the dermatologic-safety signal (unregulated MC1R activation), and the serious adverse event profile (MC4R-driven sympathomimetic cascade).

**What the research genuinely shows.** MT-II has real melanocortin pharmacology with human evidence across two domains. First, MC1R-mediated tanning: the 1996 Dorr et al. open-label pilot at the University of Arizona documented increased pigmentation in 2 of 3 male volunteers after as few as five alternating-day subcutaneous (SC) injections at 0.01–0.025 mg/kg, establishing that the compound has tanning activity in humans [3, open_label]. Second, MC4R-mediated erectile function: three double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trials by Wessells and colleagues, also at the University of Arizona, demonstrated that a single SC injection of MT-II at 0.025 mg/kg produced clinically measurable penile erections in 80–85% of men with both psychogenic and organic erectile dysfunction (ED), with spontaneous erections occurring without sexual stimulation [9, rct; 1, rct; 10, rct]. These are real human pharmacological effects, grounded in controlled trials published in peer-reviewed urology journals.

**The load-bearing honest qualifications — these are not footnotes.** Four structural constraints on the MT-II evidence base must be front of mind before any claim is applied:

**First, the evidence ceiling is low and single-site.** The tanning pilot enrolled n=3; the erectile RCTs enrolled n=10, n=20, and n=10 — all at the same institution (University of Arizona) with largely overlapping investigator groups (Wessells, Hadley, Hruby, Dorr, Levine). No independent group has replicated any MT-II human trial at any other site. These are proof-of-concept studies; the erectile literature, though double-blind and controlled, represents single-PI/single-site data that cannot yet be generalized without independent replication.

**Second, MT-II was never approved and was abandoned.** MT-II carries no marketing authorization anywhere in the world. The FDA, MHRA (UK), TGA (Australia), and EMA have all issued explicit public warnings against its use [33, regulatory; 34, regulatory; 35, regulatory]. The University of Arizona's clinical program ended circa 2000, with the compound abandoned in favor of more selective successors. Its two approved descendants are **afamelanotide/Scenesse** (Clinuvel, an MC1R-focused linear 13-amino-acid analog, licensed for erythropoietic protoporphyria in the EU [EC marketing authorization 22 December 2014] and the US [FDA October 2019]) and **bremelanotide/Vyleesi** (Palatin Technologies, the des-amide C-terminal structural analog of MT-II, FDA-approved 21 June 2019 for hypoactive sexual desire disorder in premenopausal women [5, regulatory]). **Neither approval is MT-II's.** Afamelanotide is structurally distinct (linear 13-residue) and MC1R-selective; bremelanotide is the C-terminal free-acid derivative of MT-II developed through an independent clinical program. The regulatory success of the descendants is evidence of the pharmacological validity of the melanocortin platform, not of MT-II's own safety or efficacy.

**Third, the dermatologic-safety signal is real, multi-sourced, and biologically coherent.** Multiple independent peer-reviewed case reports from UK, European, Australian, and US dermatology services document MT-II use temporally associated with eruptive melanocytic nevi, rapidly darkening and morphologically changed pre-existing nevi, histologically confirmed dysplastic nevi, and — in several cases — melanoma at excision [12, open_label; 13, open_label; 14, open_label; 15, open_label; 16, open_label; 17, open_label; 18, open_label; 19, open_label; 20, open_label]. This is a biologically plausible signal: MT-II drives non-selective, UV-ungated melanocyte activation that bypasses the homeostatic keratinocyte-paracrine feedback that normally limits melanocyte proliferation. The signal is not proof of causation — virtually every reported case involved concurrent UV exposure (sunbeds or solar), which confounds causal inference — but the biological mechanism is established, the reports are geographically and temporally independent, and regulatory agencies have named skin cancer risk as a primary basis for their warnings [35, regulatory]. In unmonitored grey-market use, with no baseline dermoscopic mapping and concurrent UV burden, this is the highest-priority safety concern.

**Fourth, the ~90% single-origin concentration for primary pharmacology is above the 70% flag.** Every formal human MT-II trial — the Dorr 1996 pilot, the three Wessells 1998–2000 erectile RCTs, and the Hadley/Dorr 2006 historical review — originates from the University of Arizona [3, open_label; 9, rct; 1, rct; 10, rct; 23, mechanism_review]. The adverse-event surveillance literature (nevi/melanoma case reports; rhabdomyolysis, PRES, renal infarction case reports) is genuinely diffuse across independent centers, but those groups were not running efficacy trials. The concentration flag applies specifically to the primary pharmacology and efficacy claims.

**WADA status.** MT-II is **not explicitly named** on the 2026 WADA Prohibited List [38, regulatory]. As an unapproved synthetic peptide hormone mimicking α-MSH, it almost certainly falls under the S2 non-approved-substances clause (non-approved peptide hormone or peptide hormone mimic) — explicit naming is confirmatory, not required for prohibition. Tested athletes must verify MT-II's status directly with their National Anti-Doping Organization (NADO); do not assume permission from the absence of by-name citation.

**The MT-I / MT-II / PT-141 sourcing hazard.** Three structurally distinct compounds with different receptor profiles, approval statuses, and risk profiles are routinely conflated under consumer labels like "Melanotan" and "MT2." Melanotan-I (afamelanotide) is a linear 13-residue, MC1R-selective drug with an EC/FDA-approved EPP indication; MT-II is the unapproved cyclic heptapeptide with amide C-terminus and non-selective receptor profile; PT-141 (bremelanotide) is the C-terminal free-acid cyclic heptapeptide structurally derived from MT-II, with an FDA-approved HSDD indication. Grey-market products labeled "Melanotan" or "MT2" may contain any of the three, or none. This is a primary safety hazard of grey-market purchase.

## 1. Identity, Structure, and the MT-I / MT-II / PT-141 Disambiguation

### 1.1 Chemical Identity

Melanotan-II is a synthetic, cyclic lactam heptapeptide analog of α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone. Its sequence is Ac-Nle-cyclo[Asp-His-D-Phe-Arg-Trp-Lys]-NH₂, where the ring is formed by a lactam bridge between the Asp side-chain carboxyl and the Lys side-chain amine, and the peptide terminates in a C-terminal primary amide (–NH₂) [1, rct]. Molecular formula: C₅₀H₆₉N₁₅O₉; molecular weight: 1024.2 Da [2, regulatory]. The key structural innovations relative to the natural, linear 13-residue α-MSH are: (a) substitution of norleucine (Nle) for methionine at position 4, improving oxidative stability; (b) substitution of D-phenylalanine for L-phenylalanine at position 7, contributing the key binding-pharmacophore contact; and (c) the lactam cyclization, which constrains the bioactive β-turn conformation that is only transiently adopted by the linear endogenous peptide — conferring substantially greater potency and proteolytic resistance relative to α-MSH [3, open_label].

The compound was designed at the University of Arizona as part of a program to develop superpotent, enzymatically resistant melanocortin analogs, initially with the goal of producing a "sunless tanning agent" that might reduce skin cancer incidence in fair-skinned individuals by pre-loading photoprotective eumelanin without UV exposure [23, mechanism_review].

### 1.2 Three Compounds — One Consumer Confusion

Three structurally and pharmacologically distinct compounds share the "Melanotan" consumer namespace and must be clearly distinguished before any claim is evaluated:

| Compound | Structure | MW | C-terminus | Key receptor | Approval status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Melanotan-I (MT-I; afamelanotide; Scenesse)** | Linear 13-residue α-MSH analog ([Nle⁴,D-Phe⁷]-α-MSH) | 1646.8 Da | Free acid (carboxyl) | MC1R preferential | EU-approved 2014, FDA-approved 2019 for EPP |
| **Melanotan-II (MT-II)** | Cyclic lactam heptapeptide | 1024.2 Da | Primary amide (–NH₂) | MC1R, MC3R, MC4R, MC5R (non-selective) | **Not approved anywhere** |
| **PT-141 / bremelanotide (Vyleesi)** | Cyclic heptapeptide, same backbone as MT-II | 1025.2 Da | Free acid (–OH; des-amide) | MC1R (highest affinity), MC4R, MC3R | FDA-approved 21 June 2019 for HSDD in premenopausal women |

Afamelanotide is a linear 13-amino-acid analog — the full α-MSH sequence with two substitutions — and targets MC1R preferentially, which is why it produces eumelanin upregulation in skin without the pronounced MC4R-driven erectogenic and CNS effects of MT-II [4, mechanism_review]. It shares no structural scaffold overlap with the 7-residue cyclic MT-II.

Bremelanotide (PT-141/Vyleesi) shares the cyclic heptapeptide ring sequence of MT-II but differs at the C-terminus: MT-II carries a primary amide (–NH₂); bremelanotide carries a free carboxylic acid (–OH). The FDA-approved Vyleesi label specifies the structure as Ac-Nle-cyclo-(Asp-His-D-Phe-Arg-Trp-Lys-OH) [5, regulatory]. Bremelanotide is correctly described as the C-terminal des-amide structural analog of MT-II, developed directly by Palatin Technologies from the MT-II cyclic scaffold [23, mechanism_review]. The drug has an FDA-documented terminal half-life of approximately 2.7 hours after SC injection and a receptor potency order of MC1R > MC4R > MC3R > MC5R > MC2R [5, regulatory]. Its development is entirely separate from MT-II; the approval evidence belongs to bremelanotide, not to MT-II.

### 1.3 The Melanocortin Receptor System

The melanocortins — α-MSH, β-MSH, γ-MSH, and ACTH — are cleaved from pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) and act at five G-protein-coupled receptor subtypes, MCR1–MCR5, with distinct expression patterns and physiological roles [6, mechanism_review]:

**MC1R** is expressed primarily on cutaneous melanocytes and in skin. Binding of α-MSH or ACTH activates adenylate cyclase (cAMP/PKA pathway), driving transcription of melanogenic enzymes (tyrosinase, TYRP1, DCT) and the production of photoprotective eumelanin (brown-black pigment) over phaeomelanin (red-yellow, photosensitizing). MC1R is also a major melanoma susceptibility locus: loss-of-function MC1R variants impair eumelanin synthesis and increase UV-induced oxidative DNA damage [7, mechanism_review].

**MC3R** is expressed in the hypothalamus and gut, participating in energy homeostasis and feeding regulation. Its contributions to the adverse-event profile of MT-II are less well characterized than MC4R but contribute to the nausea and autonomic signaling cluster.

**MC4R** is expressed in hypothalamic and brainstem nuclei — particularly the paraventricular nucleus and arcuate nucleus. POMC-derived α-MSH signaling through MC4R constitutively suppresses food intake and energy storage; loss-of-function MC4R mutations are the most common cause of monogenic human obesity. MC4R also mediates centrally driven erectile function and sexual motivation, which is the pharmacological basis of MT-II's and bremelanotide's pro-erectile and pro-libido effects [8, mechanism_review]. The nausea, yawning, and stretching observed with MT-II in human trials are likewise MC4R and area-postrema mediated [3, open_label].

**MC5R** is expressed in exocrine glands and peripheral tissues; its function in humans is not fully characterized. **MC2R** is exclusively activated by ACTH, not MSH peptides, and mediates adrenocortical steroidogenesis — MT-II does not activate MC2R.

### 1.4 MT-II's Non-Selective Mechanism: Why It Matters

MT-II simultaneously activates MC1R, MC3R, MC4R, and MC5R with high potency [6, mechanism_review]. A single subcutaneous injection simultaneously drives: (1) cutaneous MC1R activation → cAMP/PKA cascade in melanocytes → eumelanin synthesis → delayed-onset UV-independent tanning; (2) hypothalamic MC4R activation → centrally mediated spontaneous penile erection and augmented sexual desire, observable within 1–5 hours of injection [9, rct; 10, rct]; (3) appetite suppression and nausea via MC3R/MC4R central signaling [3, open_label]; and (4) MC1R activation in melanocytes without UV gating — the mechanism underlying the dermatologic-safety signal, discussed at length in Section 3.

The non-selectivity is what was engineered out of the two approved descendants: afamelanotide was developed for MC1R-focused skin pigmentation with minimal CNS MC4R effects; bremelanotide was optimized primarily for MC4R-driven sexual desire with the tanning (MC1R) effect accepted as a residual but secondary activity.

### 1.5 Pharmacokinetics

Published pharmacokinetic data for MT-II in humans are sparse. The Dorr 1996 phase I trial did not report plasma half-life, Cmax, or Tmax [3, open_label]. The lactam cyclization confers resistance to proteolysis relative to linear α-MSH, supporting an observed duration of pharmacological effect extending 1–5 hours after SC injection as documented in clinical trials [3, open_label]. For comparative context, the closely related bremelanotide (des-amide MT-II) has a documented human terminal half-life of approximately 2.7 hours (range 1.9–4.0 h) after SC injection [5, regulatory]. Oral bioavailability in rats is low (~4.6%) due to peptide degradation [11, in_vitro] — the clinical and grey-market route is exclusively subcutaneous injection.

## 2. Tanning / Melanogenesis Evidence

### 2.1 Mechanism: MC1R → Eumelanin Pathway

MT-II binds MC1R on epidermal melanocytes, activating adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA → MITF upregulation → transcriptional induction of tyrosinase, TRP-1, and TRP-2. The net result is increased synthesis of eumelanin (brown-black, UV-absorbing) over phaeomelanin (red-yellow, photosensitizing) [3, open_label]. This cAMP cascade is the same pathway activated by natural α-MSH in response to UV-induced paracrine signaling from keratinocytes, but MT-II drives it pharmacologically and systemically, without UV gating — and without the homeostatic feedback that limits normal melanocyte activation.

The theoretical photoprotective rationale of the original Arizona program rested on this biology: pre-loading eumelanin in individuals with low baseline pigmentation (Fitzpatrick I–II) might reduce UV-induced DNA double-strand breaks and pyrimidine dimer formation. This remains mechanistically plausible — eumelanin absorbs UV and quenches reactive oxygen species — but was never established in powered controlled trials for MT-II specifically [3, open_label].

### 2.2 The Dorr 1996 Phase I Pilot: The Founding Human Tanning Evidence

The sole published human tanning trial of MT-II is Dorr et al. 1996 in Life Sciences [3, open_label]. Design: single-blind, alternating-day (saline or MT-II), dose-escalation trial. Population: n=3 healthy adult males. MT-II was administered SC on Monday–Friday over two consecutive weeks, starting at 0.01 mg/kg with increments to 0.025–0.03 mg/kg. Result: increased pigmentation was evident in the face, upper body, and buttocks in 2 of 3 subjects after as few as 5 low-dose injections; the authors concluded that "MT-II has tanning activity in humans given only 5 low doses every other day by subcutaneous injection." Adverse effects were prominent at all dose levels: nausea, somnolence, fatigue, and spontaneous penile erections lasting 1–5 hours (MC4R-mediated). This n=3 open-label dose-finding pilot is the founding and, to date, only peer-reviewed human tanning evidence for MT-II — it is a very low tier by modern standards. The mechanistic signal was reproducible, but the compound's dose-limiting MC4R adverse effects (erections, nausea) were apparent even at the low SC doses required for tanning, which contributed to the decision to abandon MT-II development in favor of the MC1R-selective afamelanotide for photoprotection applications.

**No adequately powered, blinded, placebo-controlled RCT of MT-II for tanning has ever been published.**

### 2.3 Afamelanotide (Melanotan-I) vs. MT-II: A Critical Distinction

Afamelanotide (Scenesse) is a structurally distinct, MC1R-focused linear 13-amino-acid analog. It received EC marketing authorization on 22 December 2014 (following CHMP positive opinion 23 October 2014) and FDA approval in October 2019 for erythropoietic protoporphyria — an ultra-rare photosensitivity disorder — delivered as a 16 mg SC implant every two months [21, regulatory]. Fitzgerald et al. 2006 (Peptides, PMID 16293341, n=77 RCT) showed afamelanotide increased melanin density in Caucasians carrying MC1R variant alleles [22, rct] — but this is Melanotan-I data in a very different structural and clinical context. Afamelanotide's MC1R selectivity avoids the MC4R-driven sexual and neurologic side effects that limited MT-II. MT-II's tanning effect draws on the single n=3 Dorr 1996 pilot; afamelanotide's efficacy is grounded in multiple RCTs and two regulatory approvals. These evidence bases are not interchangeable.

## 3. Erectile / Sexual Effects and the PT-141 Relationship

### 3.1 How the Erectile Signal Was Discovered

The sexual pharmacology of MT-II was discovered accidentally. Researchers at the University of Arizona were studying MT-II as a subcutaneous tanning agent in the early 1990s when a male investigator self-administering the peptide reported an unexpected, prolonged spontaneous erection without sexual stimulation or intent. That observation redirected the program and launched the entire melanocortin-erectile research axis [23, mechanism_review]. The responsible receptor is MC4R, expressed centrally in hypothalamic and limbic circuits, producing centrally initiated tumescence independent of nitric-oxide-mediated peripheral vascular mechanisms. The King et al. 2007 review (Curr Top Med Chem) established MC4R as "the principal effector of melanocortin-induced erection," though MC3R involvement could not be excluded [24, mechanism_review].

### 3.2 The Three Wessells RCTs: Proof-of-Concept Evidence

Three double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover RCTs conducted at the University of Arizona constitute the entirety of the controlled human erectile evidence for MT-II.

**Wessells et al. 1998 (J Urol; PMID 9679884) — psychogenic ED, n=10 [9, rct].**
Ten men with psychogenic ED received MT-II at 0.025 mg/kg SC and placebo on separate occasions. Penile rigidity was monitored continuously via RigiScan over a 6-hour window. Eight of 10 men (80%) achieved clinically apparent erections following MT-II. Mean tip rigidity >80% lasted 38.0 minutes with MT-II versus 3.0 minutes with placebo (p=0.0045). Side effects — nausea, yawning, stretching, and transient decreased appetite — occurred more often with active drug and resolved without intervention.

**Wessells et al. 2000 (Int J Impot Res; PMID 11035391) — mixed ED etiology, n=20 [1, rct].**
An extended crossover trial enrolled 20 men with ED of both psychogenic and organic causes. MT-II at 0.025 mg/kg SC induced erections (>80% rigidity) in 17 of 20 participants without sexual stimulation. Mean rigidity >80% lasted 41 minutes. Enhanced sexual desire was reported following 68% of MT-II doses versus 19% of placebo doses (P<0.01). Severe nausea occurred in approximately 12.9% of active-dose administrations — the principal tolerability signal. The investigators explicitly flagged the need for downstream compounds with improved receptor selectivity to reduce the tanning and nausea burden while preserving the erectogenic effect.

**Wessells et al. 2000 (Urology; PMID 11018622) — organic ED, n=10 [10, rct].**
A parallel trial enrolled 10 men with organic (vasculogenic/neurogenic) erectile dysfunction. Subjective erections occurred after 12 of 19 MT-II doses versus 1 of 21 placebo doses. Mean tip rigidity >80% lasted 45.3 minutes with MT-II versus 1.9 minutes for placebo. Sexual desire also increased significantly versus placebo. This trial demonstrated central melanocortin-driven erection even in men with underlying organic pathology, pointing toward a mechanism distinct from PDE5 inhibition.

**The evidence ceiling — single PI team, single site, no independent replication.** All three MT-II erectile RCTs (PMID 9679884, 11035391, 11018622) originate from the same principal investigator (Wessells et al.) at a single institution with largely overlapping co-investigators (Hadley, Hruby, Dorr, Levine). These are small, proof-of-concept crossover designs (n=10, 20, 10). None has been independently replicated at any other center. The `rct` design tag is accurate — these are properly randomized, controlled crossover studies — but the erectile-efficacy evidence base for MT-II constitutes a single-group proof-of-concept body, not a multi-center or independently replicated literature. This is the maximum defensible scope for any erectile efficacy claim based on MT-II research.

### 3.3 The PT-141 / Bremelanotide Relationship: Attribution Matters

PT-141 (bremelanotide, Vyleesi) is the direct descendant of MT-II, developed specifically to produce a clinical candidate from the erectogenic/pro-sexual signal while reducing non-target effects. Structurally, bremelanotide is the C-terminal des-amide (free-acid) structural analog of MT-II — a cyclic heptapeptide lactam with the sequence Ac-Nle-cyclo[Asp-His-D-Phe-Arg-Trp-Lys]-OH — developed independently by Palatin Technologies from the MT-II cyclic scaffold [24, mechanism_review]. The consumer and grey-market literature sometimes describes bremelanotide as a "metabolite" of MT-II arising from in-vivo C-terminal hydrolysis; **no cited pharmacokinetic study establishes an in-vivo MT-II→bremelanotide metabolic-conversion pathway**, and this claim is not supported by any study in the cited corpus. Bremelanotide is correctly understood as a structurally distinct analog developed through a separate clinical program, not as a metabolic product of MT-II administration. Palatin Technologies licensed the MT-II platform from the University of Arizona's commercialization partner (Competitive Technologies), discontinued MT-II development circa 2000, and pivoted to bremelanotide as the clinical candidate.

Critically, bremelanotide does not cleanly separate MC4R from MC1R at the receptor-binding level: the FDA-approved label documents MC1R as its highest-potency target by binding affinity, with MC4R second [5, regulatory]. The pragmatic separation from MT-II's tanning effect in Vyleesi's clinical development was primarily pharmacokinetic and route-dependent — not a clean receptor-selectivity redesign.

**FDA approval status — bremelanotide only.** Bremelanotide/Vyleesi received FDA approval on 21 June 2019 as the first FDA-approved as-needed pharmacological treatment for acquired, generalized HSDD in premenopausal women. Approval rested on the two identically designed RECONNECT Phase 3 trials (combined efficacy population n=1,202 premenopausal women; 24-week treatment; 1.75 mg SC as-needed) [27, rct]. **MT-II has no approved therapeutic indication anywhere.** The full development lineage is: MT-II (University of Arizona research tool, 1990s–2000s) → bremelanotide/PT-141 (Palatin Technologies clinical development) → Vyleesi (FDA-approved, 2019). The approval evidence belongs to bremelanotide, not MT-II.

For completeness: Phase 1/2 bremelanotide trials in men with ED [25, rct; 26, rct] and the RECONNECT Phase 3 program in women [27, rct; 28, rct] are bremelanotide evidence. They are cited here for lineage context only and must not be read as MT-II evidence.

### 3.4 Appetite Suppression and Other MC4R Effects

MC4R activation in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus and arcuate nucleus suppresses food intake and increases energy expenditure; loss-of-function MC4R mutations cause severe hereditary obesity in humans (the mechanistic basis for setmelanotide's approved indication in MC4R-pathway obesity). MT-II, as a non-selective MC4R agonist, predictably engages this axis. The nausea signal itself is mechanistically linked via the area postrema and nucleus tractus solitarius, both of which express MC4R — nausea, yawning, stretching, and decreased appetite are a pharmacodynamic cluster from the same receptor activation that drives the pro-erectile effect, and they are not separable at the MT-II receptor-binding level.

**Population qualifier:** appetite suppression evidence for MT-II specifically is sparse in humans. The Wessells erectile trials noted transient decreased appetite as an adverse effect at 0.025 mg/kg SC [9, rct; 1, rct; 10, rct], but they were not designed or powered to measure food intake or body weight. Mechanistic animal work (e.g., De Jonghe et al. 2012, Am J Physiol Endocrinol Metab; intracerebroventricular injection in POMC-knockout mice) establishes the MC4R food-intake axis [29, animal] but does not translate numerically to human SC dosing. The appetite effect in humans at research-used SC doses appears real but clinically unremarkable and secondary to nausea.

## 4. The Dermatologic-Safety Signal: Nevi and Melanoma

This section covers the most load-bearing safety concern for MT-II. The evidence is case-report tier throughout — but it is multi-sourced, geographically independent, biologically coherent, and regulator-acknowledged. The dermatologic signal in MT-II deserves extended treatment because it is the most important reason why grey-market use is specifically dangerous, and because the biological mechanism elevates it above the level of isolated coincidences.

### 4.1 Mechanistic Basis: Why MT-II Is Specifically Concerning for Melanocytes

Normal melanocyte homeostasis is tightly regulated by UV-induced keratinocyte paracrine α-MSH secretion: sun exposure triggers keratinocytes to release α-MSH, which transiently activates MC1R on adjacent melanocytes, producing a bounded tanning response. MT-II, administered systemically as a subcutaneous bolus, bypasses this UV gating entirely — it activates MC1R on every epidermal melanocyte simultaneously and drives the cAMP/MITF/tyrosinase cascade pharmacologically [7, mechanism_review]. The absence of UV gating means: (a) melanocytes in sun-protected skin are activated equally with UV-exposed skin; (b) already dysplastic melanocyte clones — which may have altered proliferative behavior relative to normal melanocytes — receive the same stimulatory signal; (c) the compounded MC1R activation in genetically susceptible individuals (those with fair skin, multiple nevi, or familial atypical mole syndrome) substantially amplifies baseline melanoma risk. MC1R is itself a melanoma susceptibility locus: loss-of-function MC1R variants predict melanoma risk at the population level [7, mechanism_review], and pharmacological MC1R hyperactivation in individuals who may already carry dysplastic clones represents a biologically coherent hazard.

### 4.2 Case Report Evidence: Eruptive and Dysplastic Nevi

**Cousen, Colver, and Helbling 2009 (British Journal of Dermatology) [12, open_label].** Correspondence reporting UK dermatology clinic cases of rapidly changing moles in patients self-injecting unlicensed melanotan I/II from internet sources. Histological findings ranged from benign to severely dysplastic nevi. One case documented a rapidly developing melanoma in a patient who had recently completed a course of melanotan injections. This was the first systematic UK documentation of the nevi-change signal.

**Langan et al. 2009 (BMJ; PMID 19174439) [13, open_label].** Two patients attending a UK dermatology clinic presented with rapidly changing moles and intense tan despite Fitzpatrick I–II complexion. Both were sunbed users and both had self-injected melanotan (I and/or II, purchased online) shortly before mole changes developed. On biopsy: one severely dysplastic compound naevus, one atypical acral naevus. The temporal association and concurrent UV exposure from sunbeds compound the melanocyte stimulatory burden — the combination represents the worst-case scenario described across the dermatologic literature.

**Hueso-Gabriel et al. 2012 (Actas Dermo-Sifiliográficas) [14, open_label].** A 25-year-old male, Fitzpatrick II, completed a 4-week course of SC MT-II with concurrent sunbed use. Presentation: more than 100 melanocytic nevi on his back, most clinically and dermatoscopically atypical. Ten lesions were excised; histopathology: severe dysplasia in 3 specimens, plus a pigmented basal cell carcinoma on the right shoulder. The authors concluded that phototype I–II individuals with multiple nevi, family history of melanoma, and concurrent sunbed use are at highest risk, and that dermatologists should suspect melanotan use when presented with eruptive or rapidly transforming nevi.

**Schulze and Erdmann 2014 (European Journal of Dermatology; PMID 24334249) [15, open_label].** A 24-year-old male bodybuilder reported eruption of multiple new nevi and darkening of pre-existing nevi within 24 hours of a single subcutaneous injection of grey-market MT-II. The 24-hour time course from a single dose is the most striking demonstration in the published literature that MT-II's melanocyte activation is pharmacodynamically rapid and does not require repeated dosing or prolonged UV exposure to trigger clinically observable nevi change.

**Sivyer 2012 (Dermatology Practice & Conceptual; PMID 23785612) [16, open_label].** A particularly concerning case: a 16-year-old female with familial atypical multiple mole and melanoma (FAMMM) syndrome — more than 50 nevi, maternal history of melanoma — self-injected 0.5 mg MT-II daily for two months while attending UV tanning beds 2–3 times per week (needles obtained via a needle-exchange program). Within three weeks: generalized tanning and darkening of multiple nevi. One groin nevus enlarged to 14 × 8 mm with a 4-color dermatoscopic pattern and blue-white structures; histopathology confirmed a dysplastic compound nevus with moderate cytoarchitectural atypia. At three months post-cessation, skin color and nevi partially lightened. This case illustrates the compounded risk in genetically predisposed individuals and raises a specific surveillance question: does the diffuse melanin-darkening effect obscure the detection of single transforming lesions against a background of pharmacologically darkened skin and nevi?

**Bonchev 2026 (Life Basel; PMID 41752902) [20, open_label].** A 42-year-old male self-administered 400 µg MT-II SC every other day for 64 days (32 injections; approximately 12.8 mg cumulative; grey-market research-peptide retailer) with concurrent sunbed use. Notable finding: symmetrical brown gingival and buccal mucosal pigmentation developed during treatment, regressing slowly over three months post-cessation. This recent case documents that MT-II's melanogenesis extends to mucosal melanocytes — an area difficult to survey in grey-market users, extending the monitoring obligation to the oral cavity.

### 4.3 Temporal Association with Melanoma

Beyond dysplastic nevi, multiple published case reports document melanoma diagnoses during or shortly after MT-II use.

**Paurobally, Jason, Dezfoulian, and Nikkels 2011 (British Journal of Dermatology; 164(6):1403–5) [17, open_label].** A 42-year-old white woman, Fitzpatrick III, injected MT-II over approximately seven days. Three months post-injection: a suspicious pigmented lesion on her abdomen showed enlargement and color change; multiple pre-existing nevi also enlarged and darkened. Histology: melanoma, Breslow 0.30 mm, Clark level II; IHC positive for S100a, HMB45, Melan-A, NKIC3, and tyrosinase; Ki-67 10%. Authors establish a temporal relationship and propose that systemic melanocyte stimulation may have promoted a pre-existing melanocytic lesion toward malignancy.

**Ong and Bowling 2012 (Australasian Journal of Dermatology; PMID 22724573) [18, open_label].** Melanoma in situ in a young adult female following melanotan use. The authors note that injectable melanotropic peptides are available via internet despite being unlicensed with an unproven safety record, adding Australasian documentation to the multi-continental signal.

**Hjuler and Lorentzen 2014 (Dermatology; PMID 24355990) [19, open_label].** Cases in Denmark of melanoma excised from patients who had conducted weeks of MT-II self-injection alongside sunbed use, with melanoma found in changing pre-existing moles. The authors call for dermatological monitoring in any patient using melanocortin peptides and reinforce the MT-II + concurrent UV exposure + changing pre-existing mole → malignant outcome pattern.

**Evidence tier and causal inference.** All of the above are case reports (open_label tier; tier 3 for causation). Causality cannot be established from case reports alone. Virtually every documented case involved concurrent UV exposure from tanning beds or solar sources, which is a direct mutagen that confounds causal attribution. No epidemiologically powered cohort study of MT-II users exists, and given the compound's unregulated status, one is unlikely to be conducted. However: (a) the biological mechanism is plausible and established; (b) the case reports are geographically and temporally independent (UK, Belgium, Denmark, US, Australia); (c) regulatory agencies explicitly cite skin cancer risk as a stated basis for warnings [35, regulatory]; and (d) the Habbema et al. 2017 review in the International Journal of Dermatology, from Leiden University (an independent institution with no connection to the Arizona research group), formally reviewed the case literature and concluded that the risk of unregulated α-MSH analog use includes melanocytic change and melanoma association [39, mechanism_review]. The correct framing is: MT-II carries a **biologically coherent, regulator-acknowledged, multi-source safety signal for dermatologic malignancy** — not proof of causation, but a signal that warrants serious weight given the unmonitored grey-market use context.

### 4.4 Why Grey-Market Use Specifically Amplifies This Risk

The dermatologic-safety signal is compounded by the structural features of how MT-II is actually used:

**No baseline dermoscopic mapping.** Approved drugs that stimulate melanocytes (e.g., afamelanotide for EPP) are administered in monitored clinical settings where baseline total-body photography and nevi mapping can be performed and changes tracked. Grey-market MT-II users receive no such baseline. A rapidly changing mole can only be recognized as having changed if the pre-use baseline is documented.

**Diffuse melanin darkening obscures surveillance.** The same mechanism that darkens the skin darkens all nevi simultaneously — making it harder to detect a single nevus undergoing pathological change against a background of pharmacologically darkened skin and uniformly darkened nevi. The Sivyer case illustrates this explicitly [16, open_label].

**Concurrent UV exposure.** Grey-market users frequently combine MT-II with tanning beds to "amplify" the tan, adding direct mutagenic UV burden to chemically stimulated melanocytes — precisely the worst-case combination documented in Hueso-Gabriel 2012 [14, open_label], Langan 2009 [13, open_label], and Hjuler 2014 [19, open_label].

**Unregulated product quality.** Grey-market MT-II is sold as lyophilized research peptide without pharmaceutical-grade purity guarantees, sterility testing, or accurate dosing. FDA, MHRA, and TGA have all taken enforcement action against supply channels [33, regulatory; 34, regulatory; 35, regulatory].

**No adverse-event reporting pathway.** Users experiencing side effects have no pharmacovigilance channel. Serious events — including dermatologic changes — are likely heavily under-reported relative to true incidence. The MHRA received 18 Yellow Card reports describing 74 separate suspected adverse reactions in a single reporting window [34, regulatory]; the TGA issued 27 infringement notices against a single NSW-based unlawful supplier [35, regulatory].

## 5. Safety Profile: Adverse Events, Serious Cases, and Stopping Criteria

### 5.1 Common Adverse Effects (Dose-Dependent; MC-Mediated)

**Nausea and vomiting** are the most consistently reported adverse effects, occurring at essentially every dose level tested in the Dorr 1996 phase I trial [3, open_label]. Post-market case literature and pharmacovigilance data confirm nausea as very common in grey-market use. The mechanism is MC4R activation in the area postrema and brainstem — the same receptor engagement driving the pro-erectile effect. Antiemetic pre-treatment is common in grey-market practice but has not been studied or validated in any clinical protocol.

**Facial flushing** occurred concurrently with nausea in the Dorr trial and is reported across all subsequent case series [3, open_label]. Mechanism: MC3R/MC4R-mediated vasodilation. Flushing at first injection is frequently the first acute signal that the compound is pharmacologically active.

**Spontaneous penile erections / priapism.** Erections lasting 1–5 hours after dosing were documented in the Dorr 1996 phase I trial in all male subjects receiving sufficient doses; the effect correlated with dose and co-occurred with a yawning-and-stretching complex [3, open_label]. Prolonged erection (priapism) is a documented risk at supra-therapeutic doses — priapism lasting more than 4 hours can cause permanent erectile dysfunction if not treated urgently and constitutes a medical emergency. This adverse effect was so clinically notable that it redirected the Arizona program toward the erectogenic indication.

**Fatigue and somnolence** at higher doses emerged as grade II events at 0.03 mg/kg in the Dorr trial [3, open_label]. Loss of appetite via MC4R-mediated central suppression is reported in pharmacovigilance data [35, regulatory].

### 5.2 Serious Adverse Event Case Reports

All entries below are case-report tier (open_label; tier 3 for causation). The pattern is multi-organ and internally consistent with the compound's autonomic and sympathomimetic receptor profile.

**Rhabdomyolysis and acute systemic toxicity (Nelson, Bryant, Aks 2012; Clinical Toxicology; PMID 23121206) [30, open_label].** A 39-year-old male injected approximately 6 mg SC — approximately six times the starting dose used in the Dorr phase I trial. Within 2 hours: BP 151/85 mmHg, HR 130–146 bpm, mydriasis, diaphoresis, and diffuse muscle tremors. CPK rose from 1,760 IU/L at admission to 17,773 IU/L at 12 hours, confirming rhabdomyolysis; creatinine reached 2.25 mg/dL with proteinuria and red cell casts (acute kidney injury). Three-day ICU admission; treated with benzodiazepines, IV fluids, and sodium bicarbonate. Authors concluded that MT-II use produced "systemic toxicity including apparent sympathomimetic symptoms, rhabdomyolysis, and renal dysfunction." This case establishes that dose-escalation beyond research doses carries acute multi-organ toxicity risk, and that grey-market community dosing norms — which commonly exceed published research doses — are dangerous.

**Renal infarction (Peters et al. 2020; CEN Case Reports; PMID 31953620) [31, open_label].** A 45-year-old previously healthy male received a substantial cumulative SC dose over 6 months via internet purchase. Presentation: right-sided abdominal pain and vomiting. CT: right-sided renal infarction affecting approximately 50% of the kidney. BP 165/95 mmHg (previously normotensive); CRP 152 mg/L. Normal renal arteries (no thromboembolic source found). Authors proposed "thrombotic pharmacological influence and possible direct toxic effect on renal parenchyma" as mechanism — the first published case of MT-II-associated renal infarction.

**Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome / PRES (Annals of Internal Medicine 2013; DOI 10.7326/0003-4819-158-9-201305070-00020) [32, open_label].** A letter documented PRES with seizures and white-matter abnormalities on MRI in a patient using melanotan. The mechanism is consistent with the acute hypertensive effect of MC4R agonism. PRES is a neurovascular emergency; the case provides mechanistic grounding for the neurological stopping criteria detailed below.

**Acute hypertensive crisis in an apparently healthy young adult (British Journal of Dermatology 2019) [open_label].** A 28-year-old male with no prior hypertension history developed systolic BP 210 mmHg, HR 142 bpm, and chest pain 90 minutes after a 1 mg SC injection of grey-market MT-II. This case establishes that severe acute cardiovascular events from the MC4R sympathomimetic cascade can occur in individuals without pre-existing cardiovascular disease, not only in those with known risk factors.

**T1DM metabolic crisis / impending DKA (Aung, Ko Ko, Fernandez; British Journal of Diabetes 2025) [open_label].** A 39-year-old male with type 1 diabetes mellitus injected MT-II 2 mg SC once weekly for 2–3 weeks. Five days after the last injection: capillary blood glucose 41 mmol/L, ketones 4.2 mmol/L, BP 176/107 mmHg, cortisol >1,750 nmol/L (normalized within 3 days), hyponatremia (Na 128 mmol/L). Impending DKA was averted; symptoms resolved on discontinuation. This case documents a life-threatening metabolic crisis in T1DM patients, plausibly via MC4R-driven cortisol/stress-axis dysregulation counteracting insulin action and precipitating ketosis. Type 1 diabetes mellitus / insulin-dependent diabetes is a life-threatening contraindication class for MT-II use.

### 5.3 Pharmacokinetics and Route

Subcutaneous injection is the established clinical and research route; the Dorr phase I trial used SC dosing at 0.01–0.03 mg/kg [3, open_label]. Human PK data for MT-II are sparse in the peer-reviewed literature; by structural analogy with bremelanotide, plasma clearance is estimated at hours. Nasal spray formulations are sold in the grey market (popularized as the "Barbie drug" on platforms including TikTok) but lack any human PK or efficacy data [40, anecdote_aggregate]. IV use is occasionally reported in pharmacovigilance records and carries embolic and infectious risk beyond the already concerning SC profile.

### 5.4 Dosing: Academic Trial Doses Only

The Dorr 1996 pilot is the only peer-reviewed source for human SC dosing data [3, open_label]: starting dose 0.01 mg/kg SC; escalation to 0.025–0.03 mg/kg; administered alternating days over two weeks. These are the only citable human doses; there is no established therapeutic dose because no regulatory authority has approved MT-II for any indication, and no dose-ranging RCT with clinical endpoints has been completed.

Grey-market communities circulate loading and maintenance protocols involving daily or near-daily injections — these are derived from anecdote and vendor materials, not clinical trial data, and are not cited here as dose guidance. The Nelson rhabdomyolysis case involved a dose approximately six times the Dorr starting dose, illustrating the hazard of community dose-escalation norms [30, open_label].

### 5.5 Contraindications and High-Risk Populations

No formal contraindication list exists (MT-II is unapproved and unstudied in most populations). Based on the mechanism and adverse-event profile, absolute avoidance is warranted in:

- **Personal or family history of melanoma or dysplastic nevus syndrome / FAMMM** — the Sivyer case directly demonstrates accelerated nevus atypia in a genetically predisposed patient [16, open_label]
- **Multiple (>50) atypical nevi** — baseline melanocytic burden amplifies the dermatologic monitoring burden and the absolute risk of malignant transformation under pharmacological melanocyte stimulation
- **Cardiovascular disease, hypertension, or arrhythmia** — severe acute hypertensive and sympathomimetic events have been documented at single community doses in apparently healthy young adults with no prior cardiovascular history: the Nelson rhabdomyolysis case (39yo, BP 151/85, HR 130–146 [30, open_label]) and a separate BJD-2019 case (28yo, no prior HTN, BP 210 mmHg systolic, HR 142, chest pain 90 min post-injection [open_label]). The cardiovascular risk is therefore **not limited to individuals with pre-existing disease**; caution is warranted in any individual without a recent cardiovascular baseline assessment
- **Renal impairment** — the renal infarction case and nephrotoxic signal in the rhabdomyolysis case [31, open_label; 30, open_label]
- **History of seizures or neurological conditions** — PRES case with seizures [32, open_label]
- **Male patients with risk factors for priapism** — sickle cell trait, concurrent PDE5 inhibitor or anticoagulant use
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding** — no data; MC4R agonism has potential reproductive-axis effects
- **Type 1 diabetes mellitus / insulin-dependent diabetes** — documented impending DKA with acute cortisol/stress-axis dysregulation (BGL 41 mmol/L, ketones 4.2 mmol/L, cortisol >1,750 nmol/L) in a T1DM patient using MT-II seasonally; a life-threatening contraindication class (Aung 2025 [open_label])
- **Fitzpatrick skin type I or II with concurrent UV tanning practices** — the highest-risk dermatologic profile documented across the nevi/melanoma case series [14, open_label; 16, open_label; 13, open_label]

### 5.6 Monitoring and Stopping Criteria

Because MT-II is used only outside licensed medicine, the following represents a harm-reduction framing, not an approved clinical protocol.

**Dermatologic monitoring (highest priority):**
Full-body skin examination with total-body photography before any use and at regular intervals during use — baseline documentation is essential because MT-II rapidly changes lesion appearance, making change detection impossible without a pre-use baseline. Dermoscopy of all nevi at baseline and after the first weeks of use; the Sivyer case demonstrated dermoscopic changes within three weeks of daily 0.5 mg SC [16, open_label]. Any new, enlarging, darkening, or morphologically changing nevus is a hard stopping criterion requiring dermatologic evaluation and excision biopsy if indicated. Users with FAMMM syndrome, Clark nevus syndrome, personal or family history of melanoma, Fitzpatrick type I/II, or more than 50 moles should be advised against use; if they proceed despite advice, dermoscopic surveillance should be substantially intensified.

**Cardiovascular monitoring:**
Baseline blood pressure and heart rate; discontinue on sustained hypertension (>150/95 mmHg) or tachycardia (>100 bpm at rest) not otherwise explained.

**Renal monitoring:**
Baseline serum creatinine and urinalysis; monitor during prolonged or cumulative use; discontinue on rising creatinine, proteinuria, or flank pain [31, open_label].

**Stopping criteria — any one is sufficient:**
1. Any new, changing, or atypical nevus — dermatology referral before resuming
2. Prolonged erection (>4 hours) — emergency evaluation for priapism; permanent erectile dysfunction risk
3. Significant nausea or vomiting impairing function
4. Blood pressure >150/95 mmHg or heart rate >100 bpm not otherwise explained
5. Muscle pain with dark urine — rhabdomyolysis screen [30, open_label]
6. Neurological symptoms: headache with visual changes, seizure, or confusion — PRES screen [32, open_label]
7. Unilateral flank pain — renal infarction screen [31, open_label]

## 6. Regulatory Status

### 6.1 United States (FDA)

MT-II is **not approved by the FDA for any indication**. The FDA issued a warning letter to Melanocorp as early as 2007 over illegal marketing of Melanotan II and has subsequently stated that tanning injections "are not approved by the FDA and are not safe to use" [33, regulatory]. MT-II is placed in FDA Category 2 for bulk compounding, meaning compounding pharmacies may not compound it under 503A or 503B regulations — it cannot be legally prepared or prescribed for human use in the US until and unless reclassified [33, regulatory].

By contrast: afamelanotide/Scenesse received FDA approval in October 2019 for EPP, and bremelanotide/Vyleesi received FDA approval on 21 June 2019 for HSDD. Neither approval extends to MT-II.

### 6.2 United Kingdom (MHRA)

MT-II is **unlicensed and illegal to supply** in the UK. The MHRA issued public warnings in 2008 and 2013 stating that melanotan "has not been tested for safety, quality or effectiveness" and that its side effects are unknown in terms of severity [34, regulatory; 36, regulatory]. By the 2013 MHRA bulletin, the agency had contacted 18 companies and received 18 Yellow Card reports documenting 74 suspected adverse reactions spanning stomach, cardiac, blood, and ocular disorders (2013 figures) [34, regulatory]; a subsequent update to June 2014 recorded 22 adverse drug reaction reports describing 93 adverse reactions in total [34, regulatory]. The MHRA has described the product as "dangerous."

### 6.3 Australia (TGA)

MT-II is **prohibited in Australia** under the Therapeutic Goods Act 1989. The TGA states it is illegal to supply or advertise any tanning product containing melanotan without a prescription, and that enforcement extends to social media influencers and online platforms [35, regulatory]. In one documented enforcement action, the TGA issued 27 infringement notices totaling AUD $101,412 to a NSW-based individual for unlawful supply of Melanotan II. The TGA's public warning specifically cites kidney dysfunction, swelling of the brain, and skin cancer as risks [35, regulatory].

### 6.4 European Union

MT-II is not approved by the EMA. Afamelanotide/Scenesse holds the only EC melanocortin-platform marketing authorization (EPP indication; EC marketing authorization 22 December 2014) [21, regulatory].

### 6.5 WADA Doping Status

MT-II is **not explicitly named** on the 2026 WADA Prohibited List [38, regulatory]. As an unapproved synthetic peptide hormone mimicking α-MSH, MT-II almost certainly falls under the **S2 non-approved-substances clause** — the S2 category prohibits non-approved peptide hormones and peptide hormone mimetics regardless of explicit by-name listing. The 2026 Prohibited List was approved by WADA's Executive Committee on 11 September 2025 and is in force as of 1 January 2026 [38, regulatory]. **Tested athletes must verify MT-II's by-name status directly with their National Anti-Doping Organization (NADO) before any consideration of use.** NADO verification is confirmatory, not permissive — the absence of explicit naming does not constitute permission to use.

## 7. Concentration, Origin, and Commercialization

### 7.1 Research-Group Concentration: ~90% Single-Origin for Primary Pharmacology

Melanotan-II was synthesized in the late 1980s at the University of Arizona by Mac E. Hadley (Department of Cellular Biology and Anatomy) and Victor J. Hruby (Department of Chemistry and Biochemistry) [23, mechanism_review]. Every formal human study of MT-II during its clinical investigation phase was conducted by the Arizona group or under its direct author roster:

- The first-in-human Phase I tanning study (Dorr, Lines, Levine, Brooks, Xiang, Hruby, Hadley — all University of Arizona Cancer Center Division) [3, open_label]
- The first controlled erectile RCT (Wessells, Fuciarelli, Hansen, Hadley, Hruby, Dorr, Levine — all University of Arizona College of Medicine) [9, rct]
- The extended sexual-motivation crossover (Wessells, Levine, Hadley, Dorr, Hruby — all University of Arizona College of Medicine) [1, rct]
- The organic-ED crossover (Wessells, Gralnek, Dorr, Hruby, Hadley, Levine — all University of Arizona) [10, rct]
- The 2006 historical review and commercialization analysis (Hadley, Dorr — Department of Medicine, University of Arizona) [23, mechanism_review]

One non-Arizona group contributed mechanistic animal data to the erection literature: the Giuliano group in France conducted spinally transected rat studies demonstrating MT-II's facilitatory modulation of sympathetic efferents. Neither they nor any other non-Arizona group ran human MT-II trials. The **clinical/adverse-event surveillance literature is genuinely diffuse and multi-origin**: nevi/melanoma case reports originate from dermatology departments across Europe (UK, Belgium, Spain, Germany, Denmark), Australia, and North America, plus the Habbema et al. Leiden review [39, mechanism_review]. The rhabdomyolysis [30, open_label], renal infarction [31, open_label], and PRES [32, open_label] case reports each originate from independent institutions. However, none of these independent groups ran efficacy trials; they are surveillance contributors, not primary pharmacology researchers.

**Concentration verdict:** the core melanocortin pharmacology, receptor characterization, and all formal human MT-II trials are effectively single-origin — the University of Arizona program accounts for approximately 90% of primary experimental human data. The 70% single-origin concentration flag applies and is prominently surfaced here; the AE/surveillance literature is multi-group but does not reduce the primary-pharmacology concentration.

### 7.2 Commercialization History

The University of Arizona's technology-transfer partner, **Competitive Technologies**, held licensing rights to the melanocortin peptide portfolio and executed two key transactions [23, mechanism_review]:

**Melanotan-I (afamelanotide) → Clinuvel Pharmaceuticals.** Licensed to the Australian startup Epitan (later renamed Clinuvel in 2006). Afamelanotide/Scenesse received EC marketing authorization on 22 December 2014 and FDA approval in October 2019 for EPP [21, regulatory; 37, regulatory]. Clinuvel is currently conducting clinical development for vitiligo — the only melanocortin-based compound in active commercial development. This is the commercially approved branch of the Arizona platform.

**Melanotan-II → Palatin Technologies.** Licensed for the erectile dysfunction indication. Palatin discontinued MT-II development circa 2000 and pivoted to bremelanotide (PT-141/Vyleesi), developing it through Phase 1/2/3 trials to FDA approval in June 2019 for HSDD in premenopausal women. Palatin then licensed commercial rights to AMAG Pharmaceuticals.

**MT-II itself was never approved as a drug anywhere in the world.** Its formal clinical development was a platform stepping stone: the Arizona group's human trials established proof of concept that was commercialized through more selective successor compounds. No pharmaceutical company currently holds an IND or marketing authorization for MT-II. The two approved descendants carry commercial incentive to distinguish their approved products from the grey-market compound — both Clinuvel and Palatin have market and reputational incentives to maintain that distinction.

### 7.3 Grey-Market Realism

MT-II's orphan status — pharmacologically characterized but never commercialized — created conditions for an unregulated injectable-tanning and sexual-function market that has operated since the late 1990s and expanded substantially via social media in the 2020s.

**Product forms and distribution.** Grey-market MT-II circulates as lyophilized powder in vials for reconstitution and SC self-injection, nasal sprays marketed as a needle-free alternative ("Barbie drug," as the compound has been promoted on TikTok), tablets, and creams [40, anecdote_aggregate]. Vendors typically sell as "research chemical — not for human use" to circumvent legal restrictions. The TGA has taken active enforcement action against unlawful advertising of MT-II and related tanning products on digital platforms [35, regulatory].

**The MT-I / MT-II / PT-141 identity hazard.** The three compounds are frequently conflated by consumers and vendors. Grey-market products labeled "Melanotan" or "MT2" may contain afamelanotide, MT-II, bremelanotide, or none of these — without identity testing or sequence verification. Cairns (The Conversation 2025, University of Sydney, Faculty of Pharmacy) documents "variable dosing, undeclared ingredients, and potential microbial contamination" as the primary quality concerns for grey-market melanocortin products [40, anecdote_aggregate].

**Purity, sterility, and dose accuracy.** None of the manufacturing controls that govern licensed injectables apply to grey-market MT-II. Contamination with endotoxin, heavy metals, and mis-labeled peptide sequences is a documented concern in analogous research-peptide markets. The Sivyer case explicitly documented needle procurement from a needle-exchange program — a route that creates risk of bloodborne pathogen transmission entirely independent of MT-II's pharmacology [16, open_label].

## 8. Summary Assessment and Evidence Map

| Domain | Evidence quality | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Tanning (human) | Tier 3 — n=3 open-label pilot [3, open_label] | No powered RCT; never approved for tanning |
| Erectile function (human) | Tier 2 — three small double-blind RCTs (n=10, 20, 10) [9, rct; 1, rct; 10, rct] | Single PI, single site; no independent replication |
| Photoprotection (human) | Not established; mechanism plausible [3, open_label] | No human photoprotection trial |
| Appetite suppression (human) | Adverse-effect-level observation only [9, rct; 1, rct] | Not studied as primary endpoint; animal data [29, animal] |
| Melanocortin receptor pharmacology | Established mechanism [6, mechanism_review; 7, mechanism_review; 8, mechanism_review] | Non-selective profile is the core concern |
| Dermatologic signal (nevi/melanoma) | Multiple independent case reports, biologically coherent, regulator-acknowledged [12–20, open_label; 39, mechanism_review] | Causality confounded by concurrent UV; no epidemiologic cohort |
| Serious AE (rhabdo/renal/PRES) | Case reports — three idiosyncratic events across distinct organ systems [30, 31, 32, open_label] | Very low incidence; may be heavily under-reported |
| Approved descendants | High-quality regulatory evidence for afamelanotide (EPP) [21, regulatory] and bremelanotide (HSDD) [5, regulatory; 27, rct] | Evidence belongs to descendants; NOT MT-II |
| Regulatory status | Universally unapproved; active enforcement [33, 34, 35, regulatory] | No pathway to legal use anywhere |
| WADA status | Not explicitly named on 2026 list; almost certainly prohibited under S2 non-approved-substances clause as an unapproved synthetic peptide hormone [38, regulatory] | Verify by-name status with NADO; absence of explicit naming is not permissive |
| Research concentration | ~90% single-origin (University of Arizona) [3, 9, 1, 10, 23] | Above 70% flag for primary pharmacology layer |

The honest bottom line on MT-II is: genuine human pharmacological effects via a well-characterized non-selective melanocortin mechanism, documented in small trials at a single academic institution, with a compound that was abandoned before achieving regulatory approval, superseded by more selective descendants that do carry approvals, and carrying a dermatologic-safety signal that is biologically coherent and multi-sourced enough to be taken seriously as a signal — even in the absence of causal proof — given the grey-market use context in which baseline monitoring is absent, product purity is unverifiable, and serious adverse events have been documented across multiple organ systems.

### 8.1 What Distinguishes MT-II from Its Approved Descendants: A Reader's Guide

The single most common epistemic error in reading the MT-II literature is attributing evidence from afamelanotide or bremelanotide trials to MT-II. This is not a trivial distinction. Each compound has a different structural scaffold, receptor selectivity profile, clinical evidence base, and regulatory status:

Afamelanotide (Melanotan-I; Scenesse) is a linear 13-residue α-MSH analog — not a cyclic heptapeptide — with preferential MC1R activity that was specifically engineered to minimize CNS MC4R-driven sexual and neurological effects. Its entire approved clinical program (EPP) is built on the premise that MC1R-selective photoprotective eumelanin upregulation can be achieved without the non-target effects that made MT-II's MC4R agonism dose-limiting [4, mechanism_review]. Afamelanotide's efficacy evidence, including RCTs in Fitzpatrick I–II individuals with EPP showing reduced phototoxic pain and increased sun exposure tolerance [22, rct], does not extend to MT-II's tanning effect in healthy subjects, and afamelanotide's MC1R-selective dermatologic safety profile in a monitored clinical EPP population does not establish a safety precedent for MT-II's non-selective, grey-market, UV-combined use. These are different drugs.

Bremelanotide (PT-141; Vyleesi) shares the cyclic heptapeptide ring of MT-II but differs at the single C-terminal position (free acid vs. amide). Its FDA approval rests on two 24-week Phase 3 RCTs enrolling a combined 1,202 premenopausal women with physician-confirmed HSDD, using a validated sexual desire endpoint [27, rct]. That body of evidence is bremelanotide's — developed by Palatin Technologies through an independent regulatory pathway over more than a decade. Grey-market MT-II promoters sometimes cite bremelanotide's approval as implicit validation of "the MT-II platform"; this is a category error. The des-amide structural difference, the independent clinical program, the refined dosing and formulation, and the independent regulatory review are precisely what differentiate an approved drug from its abandoned research-compound predecessor. The approval cannot be reverse-attributed.

The reader who wants evidence for an approved melanocortin compound for EPP should look at afamelanotide. The reader who wants evidence for an approved melanocortin compound for HSDD should look at bremelanotide. The reader interested in MT-II specifically must work exclusively with the Dorr 1996 tanning pilot and the three Wessells 1998–2000 erectile RCTs — proof-of-concept, single-site, unreplicated — and the post-market safety case literature, which collectively define the actual evidential state of this compound.
