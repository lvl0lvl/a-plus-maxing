## Summary

Dihexa (chemical name: N-hexanoyl-Tyr-Ile-(6)-aminohexanoic acid amide; also designated PNB-0408; PubChem CID 129010512; molecular formula C₂₇H₄₄N₄O₅; MW 504.7 g/mol) is a small-molecule peptidomimetic developed at Washington State University (WSU) by Joseph W. Harding and John W. Wright as an orally active, blood-brain barrier-permeable analog of angiotensin IV (AngIV). It is marketed in the grey-market nootropic space principally on the claim that it is "seven orders of magnitude more potent than BDNF" in promoting synaptogenesis — a claim that traces to the now-retracted primary mechanism paper and cannot be treated as established.

**This is the most integrity-compromised entry in the peptide library sweep.** Six load-bearing facts define its status:

**One: The foundational mechanism papers are retracted.** The paper establishing HGF/c-Met-dependent synaptogenesis as Dihexa's mechanism — Benoist CC et al. (JPET 2014, PMID 25187433 [7, animal]) — was formally retracted in April 2025 (retraction notice PMID 40312093) following a WSU investigation that found figures 1B and 2A/C contained falsified or fabricated elements. A companion mechanism paper — Kawas LH et al. (JPET 2012, PMID 22129598 [10, animal]) — was retracted simultaneously (retraction notice PMID 40312092). The primary oral dosing-anchor paper — McCoy AT et al. (JPET 2013, PMID 23055539 [1, animal]) — received an Expression of Concern in September 2021 (PMID 34551989) and has not been retracted but remains under integrity scrutiny as of June 2026. The BDNF-potency comparison figure cited in virtually all secondary sources and grey-market marketing appeared in the now-retracted Benoist 2014 paper and cannot be presented as an established fact.

**Two: The proposed mechanism has zero non-retracted, non-WSU support.** Every primary experimental paper establishing the HGF/c-Met → dendritic spine → synaptogenesis mechanism chain traces to the Harding/Wright/Kawas group at WSU. No peer-reviewed paper from an independent laboratory has replicated this specific mechanism in any species. The sole credible independent replication of cognitive rescue (Sun et al. 2021 [5], China Pharmaceutical University, APP/PS1 mice) identified PI3K/AKT signaling — explicitly not HGF/c-Met — as the operative pathway; the Sun paper contains no HGF or c-Met data. The mechanistic basis of Dihexa's proposed action is therefore currently unsupported in the non-retracted literature from any independent group.

**Three: The c-Met oncogenic tension is load-bearing and unstudied.** The HGF/c-Met receptor tyrosine kinase axis is a primary oncogenic driver across hepatocellular carcinoma, non-small-cell lung cancer, gastric cancer, breast cancer, glioblastoma, and multiple other malignancies. The FDA approved capmatinib (2020) and tepotinib (2021) as selective c-Met kinase inhibitors specifically because blocking this pathway suppresses tumor growth. Dihexa's proposed therapeutic mechanism is the pharmacological inverse: potentiation of HGF-driven c-Met phosphorylation. No carcinogenicity study — rodent long-term or otherwise — has been conducted for Dihexa. Absence of a carcinogenicity study is the normal state for an academic compound that never reached IND development; it does not imply the risk was assessed and found absent. The theoretical tumor-promotion concern is genuine, grounded in established cancer biology, and completely unresolved by any existing Dihexa-specific data. Concurrent use with approved c-Met inhibitors (capmatinib, tepotinib) constitutes a direct pharmacodynamic opposition and is contraindicated.

**Four: Zero human data exist.** No clinical trial of Dihexa has been registered or published. ClinicalTrials.gov returns zero results for any Dihexa study [2, regulatory]. No human pharmacokinetic, dose-escalation, safety, or efficacy data of any kind exist. The related compound fosgonimeton (ATH-1017; Athira Pharma, subsequently LeonaBio) is a structurally distinct, subcutaneously administered HGF/c-Met modulator from the same mechanistic program — not Dihexa — and its human data do not transfer to Dihexa. Fosgonimeton failed all its trials: the LIFT-AD Phase 2/3 (n=554 enrolled; primary Global Statistical Test endpoint missed, results posted April 2025) [17, regulatory], ACT-AD Phase 2, and the SHAPE Parkinson's disease trial was halted early. These clinical failures from the only human program in this mechanistic class are important translational context.

**Five: The literature is ~67% single-group, and that group's load-bearing evidence is retracted.** A complete enumeration of Dihexa-relevant PubMed-indexed studies identifies 9 papers; 6 of 9 (~67%) are primarily WSU/Harding-Wright in origin (including M3 Biotechnology, the WSU spinout with dual-affiliation authorship). The three independent papers (Ho & Nation 2018 systematic review, Sun 2021 APP/PS1 mouse, Wells 2024 Huntington's rat) provide partial corroboration but no full independent replication of the original procognitive claims in the same model. The Wells 2024 paper [8, animal] — the most recent published in-vivo Dihexa study — found a null result. Critically, the load-bearing mechanism evidence within the WSU-group papers is not merely concentrated but retracted.

**Six: The Kawas integrity story has compounding layers.** Leen Kawas was a WSU doctoral student, co-author on all the flagged papers, and subsequently CEO of Athira Pharma. The WSU investigation found she altered images in her 2011 doctoral dissertation and at least four co-authored publications from 2011–2014. Athira Pharma (subsequently rebranded as LeonaBio [20, vendor_label]) settled a $4 million U.S. Department of Justice claim in January 2025 [18, anecdote_aggregate] over NIH grant applications supported by manipulated figures from the WSU papers. A separate ~$10 million securities class action was also filed. Kawas resigned as Athira CEO in October 2021.

The grey-market sells Dihexa in oral, topical, and injectable formulations on the strength of the retracted potency claim. Buyers frequently conflate grey-market Dihexa with the clinical fosgonimeton program; these are different compounds, and the clinical program failed. The IRAP/AT4 binding hypothesis (Albiston et al. 2001 [6, in_vitro] — independent group, no integrity concerns) is a parallel mechanistic candidate for the broader AngIV/AT4 procognitive axis that does not establish the synaptogenic claims specific to Dihexa and does not rescue the retracted HGF/c-Met evidence.

## 1. Identity, Structure, and the Angiotensin IV Lineage

### 1.1 Chemical Identity

Dihexa belongs to the lineage of AngIV-derived peptidomimetics developed by the Harding/Wright group at WSU beginning in the 1990s. Angiotensin IV (Val-Tyr-Ile-His-Pro-Phe) is a hexapeptide metabolite of the renin-angiotensin system generated by successive aminopeptidase cleavage of angiotensin II. The parent AngIV hexapeptide is rapidly degraded in plasma and does not efficiently cross the blood-brain barrier, limiting its therapeutic utility.

The peptidomimetic design strategy for Dihexa made two deliberate terminal modifications to an AngIV-derived core sequence. The N-terminus received a hexanoyl acyl cap (replacing the free amine), improving lipophilicity and protease resistance against aminopeptidase attack. The C-terminus received a 6-aminohexanoic acid amide (replacing the free carboxylate), reducing hydrogen-bond donor character and impeding carboxypeptidase recognition. Together these changes were designed to confer oral bioavailability and BBB permeability while retaining the procognitive activity ascribed to the Tyr-Ile dipeptide core [1, animal; 3, mechanism_review].

Verified structural data from PubChem CID 129010512:

- **Molecular formula:** C₂₇H₄₄N₄O₅
- **Molecular weight:** 504.7 g/mol
- **IUPAC name:** (2S,3S)-N-(6-amino-6-oxohexyl)-2-[[(2S)-2-(hexanoylamino)-3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)propanoyl]amino]-3-methylpentanamide
- **Isomeric SMILES:** CCCCCC(=O)N[C@@H](CC1=CC=C(C=C1)O)C(=O)N[C@@H]([C@@H](C)CC)C(=O)NCCCCCC(=O)N
- **InChIKey:** XEUVNVNAVKZSPT-JTJYXVOQSA-N

Dihexa was developed as a further metabolic stabilization of C-terminally truncated Nle¹-AngIV analogs characterized by Benoist et al. (2011) [9, animal] in spatial-memory and hippocampal-dendritic-spine studies. That 2011 paper is not formally retracted as of June 2026, but it originates from the same Harding/Wright/Kawas authorship cluster and the same 2011–2014 publication period as the papers that drew integrity findings [18, anecdote_aggregate]; its figures have not been individually cleared, and it should be weighted accordingly.

### 1.2 The AT4 Receptor and the IRAP Hypothesis

Beginning in the mid-1990s, the Harding/Wright group characterized a distinct brain receptor subtype — the AT4 receptor — with selective affinity for AngIV and its analogs, distinct from the AT1 and AT2 receptors that bind angiotensin II [4, mechanism_review]. The AT4 receptor is heavily expressed in hippocampal CA1–CA3 fields, parahippocampal regions, and other memory-related structures. In rat spatial-learning paradigms (circular water maze), intracerebroventricular AT4 agonists facilitated acquisition while the AT4 antagonist Divalinal-AngIV impaired it, establishing the AngIV/AT4 system as a procognitive axis [12, animal].

A separate, fully independent line of research identified the molecular identity of the AT4 receptor. Albiston et al. (2001), using protein purification and peptide sequencing from bovine adrenal cortex, reported that the AT4 receptor is insulin-regulated aminopeptidase (IRAP) — a type II transmembrane zinc metalloprotease [6, in_vitro]. Functional characterisation showed that AT4 ligands dose-dependently inhibit IRAP's catalytic activity, suggesting that prolonged IRAP inhibition extends the half-lives of neuropeptide substrates in synaptic clefts as a putative memory mechanism [6, in_vitro; 13, mechanism_review]. This IRAP hypothesis was corroborated in a follow-up mechanistic review [13, mechanism_review] and constitutes the dominant mechanistic framework from an independent-group perspective. It carries no integrity concerns.

A parallel European-based medicinal chemistry program — centered at Uppsala University and reviewed by Hallberg and Larhed (2020) [14, mechanism_review] — developed macrocyclic peptidomimetics and small-molecule scaffolds as IRAP inhibitors, entirely separate from the WSU HGF/c-Met account. This program treats AngIV analogs as IRAP inhibitors without reference to HGF or c-Met, and its mechanistic papers carry no integrity flags.

The IRAP hypothesis does not establish the synaptogenic potency claims specific to Dihexa. It does, however, represent the most evidence-solid mechanistic account for the broader AngIV/AT4 procognitive axis — one that remains plausible in the post-retraction literature.

### 1.3 The HGF/c-Met Hypothesis — and Its Retracted Foundation

Starting approximately 2012, the Harding/Wright group proposed an alternative target for AngIV analogs: the hepatocyte growth factor (HGF)/c-Met receptor tyrosine kinase system. The proposed mechanism was that Dihexa and related compounds function as potentiators of HGF at sub-threshold concentrations, augmenting c-Met phosphorylation and driving downstream PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK signaling to produce dendritic spine formation and de novo synaptogenesis. This mechanism, if real, would be the basis for all downstream synaptogenic and procognitive claims that differentiate Dihexa from simpler IRAP inhibitors.

The foundational experimental paper for this claim was Kawas LH et al. (JPET 2012, PMID 22129598 [10, animal]) — which first proposed AngIV analogs as HGF/c-Met modifiers — followed by Benoist CC et al. (JPET 2014, PMID 25187433 [7, animal]) — which established that Dihexa's procognitive and synaptogenic effects are mechanistically dependent on HGF/c-Met activation, using Morris water maze behavioral data plus in-vitro synaptogenesis assays in dissociated rat hippocampal neurons and organotypic hippocampal slice cultures.

Both papers were formally retracted in April 2025 (retraction notices PMID 40312092 [10, animal] and PMID 40312093 [7, animal] respectively). The retraction of Kawas 2012 was for falsification/fabrication in figures 3A and 4. The retraction of Benoist 2014 was for falsification/fabrication in figures 1B and 2A/C, specifically the Western blot data supporting c-Met phosphorylation as the operative mechanism. The HGF/c-Met mechanism of dihexa must therefore be treated as unconfirmed by any non-retracted primary literature.

### 1.4 The "Seven Orders of Magnitude More Potent than BDNF" Claim

The widely cited potency comparison — that Dihexa is "100 million times" or "7 orders of magnitude" more potent than BDNF in promoting synaptogenesis — originates from the Harding/Wright originating group and was disseminated via a WSU press release (October 2012) summarizing unpublished organotypic hippocampal slice data. The quantitative figure appears in the body of the now-retracted Benoist 2014 paper, in an in-vitro hippocampal slice synaptogenesis assay comparing effective concentrations of Dihexa versus BDNF.

This claim requires multiple layers of qualification, all of which are disqualifying for any presentation of it as an established fact: (1) in-vitro only — the comparison was from one assay system, not an animal cognition study or human trial; (2) originating-group single source — the data were produced and reported by the lab that developed and patented Dihexa, with no independent replication of this specific potency comparison; (3) retracted primary source — the paper containing this comparison was formally retracted due to figure manipulation; (4) non-comparable mechanistic endpoint — BDNF acts via TrkB, while Dihexa is proposed to act via HGF/c-Met potentiation, making "potency" in one assay not a general cognitive potency comparison.

The claim is documented here for transparency — it dominates secondary literature and drives virtually all grey-market marketing — but it must not be presented as an established biological fact.

Understanding why this comparison became so culturally sticky in the nootropics community helps explain the broader grey-market dynamics around Dihexa. BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) is widely recognized as the canonical synaptogenic and neuroprotective neurotrophin — the molecule most popularly associated with the concept of "growing new neurons" and "building synapses." Positioning Dihexa as something vastly more potent than BDNF in its synaptogenic effects, while also being orally bioavailable, positioned it in grey-market discourse as the most powerful cognitive-enhancement compound ever described. The claim spread through nootropics forums, podcasts, and grey-market vendor marketing in the years 2012–2015 in a way that insulated it from scrutiny. The underlying paper was written in the language of rigorous science; it was published in a respected American pharmacology journal (JPET, published by the American Society for Pharmacology and Experimental Therapeutics); and it was from a named institution (WSU). The retraction did not occur until April 2025 — more than a decade after the claim began circulating — by which point it had become so entrenched in the secondary literature that most users and vendors who cite it have no awareness the originating paper no longer stands.

### 1.5 Pharmacology: Oral Activity, BBB Penetration, and the Limits of Existing PK Data

The central pharmacological claim for Dihexa — beyond its proposed mechanism — is that it is orally bioavailable and brain-penetrant, overcoming the pharmacokinetic failures of the natural AngIV hexapeptide, which is rapidly degraded in plasma by aminopeptidases and carboxypeptidases and does not efficiently traverse the blood-brain barrier. The design rationale for the N-hexanoyl cap and C-terminal amide modifications is chemically coherent: the N-hexanoyl group blocks the free α-amine that aminopeptidases require for substrate recognition, and the terminal amide blocks carboxypeptidase access while also reducing the molecule's hydrogen-bond donor count and overall polarity, improving passive transcellular diffusion across lipid bilayer barriers including the blood-brain barrier [1, animal; 3, mechanism_review].

The McCoy 2013 paper (under Expression of Concern, PMID 23055539 [1, animal]) reported several pharmacological characterizations relevant to these claims. Brain penetration was assessed via radiolabeled Dihexa in young Sprague-Dawley rats; distribution to hippocampus, frontal cortex, and other brain regions was reported to exceed blood-contamination levels, consistent with active BBB crossing. Oral activity was demonstrated in the Morris water maze scopolamine-reversal model at doses of 1.25–2.0 mg/kg by gavage. These findings come from the originating group and carry the same integrity flag as all McCoy 2013 data.

The critical gap is that no independent pharmacokinetic characterisation of Dihexa exists in the peer-reviewed literature as of June 2026. No published study — from any group — has reported absolute oral bioavailability (F%), plasma half-life (t₁/₂), maximum concentration (C_max), time to maximum concentration (T_max), volume of distribution (Vd), clearance, or brain-to-plasma ratio for Dihexa in any species. The compound's pharmacokinetic profile in humans is entirely unknown. Vendor claims about Dihexa's duration of action, bioavailability percentage, and brain penetration efficiency are not grounded in any published primary source; they are extrapolations from the McCoy 2013 EoC paper or are simply invented.

Wright JW and Harding JW's 2019 review [22, mechanism_review] provides broader renin-angiotensin/cognition context without independent pharmacokinetic data for Dihexa specifically. The Wright 2015 Progress in Neurobiology review [3, mechanism_review] discusses oral activity and BBB penetration in the context of the broader AngIV-analog program but again relies on the originating group's unpublished or EoC-flagged data.

The pharmacokinetic uncertainty has direct practical implications for grey-market dosing. Without an established oral bioavailability fraction in humans, the relationship between a given oral dose (in milligrams) and the brain exposure achieved is entirely speculative. If Dihexa's oral bioavailability in humans is substantially lower than in the rodent models used to establish efficacy doses, the rodent-to-human dose extrapolation fails in a direction that could either result in sub-therapeutic exposure or — if a user attempts to compensate by increasing dose — potentially produce greater systemic c-Met potentiation than the rodent models suggested. Neither failure mode has any published data to characterize it.

## 2. Cognitive and Neuroprotective Efficacy Evidence

### 2.1 Evidence Tier Declaration

**This section carries an evidence-tier D ceiling.** The primary mechanistic evidence papers (Kawas 2012 [10, animal], Benoist 2014 [7, animal]) are retracted for data fabrication. The primary oral dosing-anchor paper (McCoy 2013 [1, animal]) is under Expression of Concern. The HGF/c-Met mechanism has zero non-retracted, non-WSU experimental support. One independent animal study found cognitive effects via a different mechanism (Sun 2021 [5, animal]); one independent animal study found null results (Wells 2024 [8, animal]). Zero human data exist. These are the intrinsic ceiling conditions of this literature, not gaps addressable by more careful analysis.

### 2.2 Foundational Behavioral Work — Scopolamine and Aged-Rat Models

The primary behavioral evidence derives from McCoy et al. (2013) [1, animal], which introduced Dihexa as an orally bioavailable, metabolically stable peptidomimetic. In male Sprague-Dawley rats (n=8 per group), oral Dihexa at 2.0 mg/kg/day reversed scopolamine (1.0 mg/kg i.p.)-induced deficits in the Morris water maze, restoring escape latency to levels statistically indistinguishable from unimpaired controls (p<0.001 by day 7). Doses of 1.25 mg/kg were also effective; 0.05 mg/kg i.p. was not. In a separate cohort of aged (24-month-old) mixed-sex Sprague-Dawley rats (n=6 per group), oral Dihexa at 2.0 mg/kg/day significantly improved MWM performance on most training days versus vehicle (p<0.05, Mann-Whitney U).

**This paper received an Expression of Concern in September 2021 (PMID 34551989 [1, animal]) for the same image-manipulation concerns that eventually drove the Benoist 2014 retraction.** It has not been retracted as of June 2026 but must be treated as under integrity cloud. All animal dose figures drawn from this paper carry that caveat. The behavioral MWM data in the McCoy paper were produced by the same group whose other papers were retracted; the behavioral outcome data themselves were not specifically implicated in the integrity notices, but the overall weight of the paper's evidence is substantially diminished.

### 2.3 HGF/c-Met Mechanism and Synaptogenesis — the Retracted Paper

The Benoist et al. (2014) paper [7, animal] was the mechanistic cornerstone of Dihexa's cognitive efficacy narrative before its retraction. It reported — in male Sprague-Dawley rats (n=8–10 per group) in the MWM scopolamine model — that Dihexa's cognitive rescue was blocked by the HGF antagonist "Hinge," establishing HGF/c-Met as the obligate pathway. In dissociated hippocampal neuron cultures (n≥50 neurons per condition), it reported that Dihexa at 10⁻¹² M increased dendritic spine density from approximately 17 to approximately 26 spines per 50 µm dendrite, with miniature excitatory postsynaptic current (mEPSC) frequency doubling — supporting functional synaptogenesis.

**This paper was formally retracted in April 2025 (retraction notice PMID 40312093 [7, animal]) after the WSU investigation found figures 1B and 2A/C contained falsified data.** The figure-manipulation findings were specifically in the Western blot data supporting c-Met phosphorylation as the mechanism. The synaptogenic and behavioral data in separate figures of the same paper were not directly implicated in the retraction finding, but their evidentiary weight is substantially diminished by the overall retraction context. The HGF/c-Met mechanism of action for Dihexa should be treated as scientifically plausible (given the independent HGF neuroscience literature) but specifically unconfirmed by any non-retracted primary evidence.

### 2.4 The Systematic Review Context

Ho and Nation (2018) [11, meta_analysis] reviewed 32 experimental studies of AngIV and Ang-(1-7) and cognition. Of 9 cognitive-deficit model studies, 8 found AngIV or analogs improved spatial working memory or passive avoidance tasks, with Dihexa grouped among the effective compounds. This review was published before the 2025 retractions and does not adjudicate the IRAP versus HGF/c-Met mechanistic dispute. All included studies are animal data. The review provides context for the broader AngIV-analog procognitive signal without resolving whether Dihexa's specific HGF/c-Met mechanism is real.

### 2.5 Independent Replication in APP/PS1 Alzheimer's Mouse Model

Sun et al. (2021) [5, animal] provided the only substantial independent replication of Dihexa's cognitive effects outside the Harding/Wright lab. Using APP/PS1 double-transgenic mice (6-month-old males; n=12 per group for MWM, n=6 for molecular analyses), intragastric Dihexa at 1.44 or 2.88 mg/kg/day for 3 months reduced escape latency and increased platform crossings in probe trials versus untreated APP/PS1 controls. PI3K/AKT pathway activation was confirmed: wortmannin (PI3K inhibitor) reversed the behavioral improvement, and p-AKT expression was elevated with Dihexa treatment. Neuroinflammatory markers (IL-1β, TNF-α) were reduced; IL-10 was increased; Nissl-positive neuron density and synaptophysin expression were preserved.

**The mechanistic finding here — PI3K/AKT, not HGF/c-Met — deserves particular weight.** This is a Chinese-institution group (China Pharmaceutical University, Nanjing Medical University) with no WSU affiliation, using a different species (mouse versus rat), a different AD model (amyloid transgenic versus scopolamine pharmacological), and a fully independent authorship. The paper contains no HGF or c-Met discussion. This independent replication: (a) does validate a cognitive rescue signal in a disease-relevant model; (b) explicitly does NOT validate the HGF/c-Met mechanism; and (c) identifies a different mechanistic pathway — PI3K/AKT — as the operative explanation. Published in Brain Sciences (MDPI, open-access; no EoC or retraction). This is a tier-2 study in a lower-trust open-access outlet, but it is the most credible confirmation in the post-retraction literature that some cognitive effect signal may exist for Dihexa, even if the mechanism remains unclear.

### 2.6 The Huntington's Disease Null Result

Wells et al. (2024) [8, animal] tested PNB-0408 (Dihexa) in male Wistar rats (n≈13–14 per group) with 3-nitropropionic acid (3-NP)-induced Huntington's disease-like neurochemistry and motor/cognitive deficits. Dihexa failed to protect rats from the spatial learning and memory deficits induced by 3-NP neurotoxicity; no cognitive or motor benefit was detected. This is the most recent published in-vivo Dihexa behavioral study and the only one to find a clear null result. Published in the Journal of Huntington's Disease; no integrity issues. The null finding does not mean Dihexa cannot work in any model — HD/3-NP involves excitotoxic mechanisms distinct from the amyloid or scopolamine-cholinergic models where modest effects were reported — but it substantively limits generalization of the efficacy signal across disease contexts.

### 2.7 Parkinson's Disease — Narrative Claims Without Published In-Vivo Dihexa Data

Wright, Kawas, and Harding (2015) [3, mechanism_review] describe the rationale for AngIV analog use in Parkinson's disease, noting HGF/c-Met activation's potential to protect dopaminergic neurons. No published primary in-vivo study of Dihexa in a dopaminergic lesion model (6-OHDA or MPTP) was identified in the literature search. PD claims in the secondary literature are extrapolated from general HGF neuroprotection literature, the SHAPE trial rationale used by Athira for fosgonimeton (which was halted early), and the narrative review by the originating group itself. These are not independent experimental data for Dihexa in PD.

### 2.8 Human Clinical Evidence — Absent

**No human clinical trial of Dihexa has been published or registered on ClinicalTrials.gov [2, regulatory].** No human pharmacokinetic data, safety data, or efficacy signal of any kind exists for Dihexa. The cognitive performance claims circulating in the grey-market nootropic community are wholly unsupported by human data of any kind.

The closest human evidence comes from fosgonimeton (ATH-1017), developed by Athira Pharma from the same HGF/c-Met program as Dihexa. Fosgonimeton is a distinct compound — structurally different, administered subcutaneously rather than orally, with its own pharmacokinetics — and its data cannot be transposed to grey-market Dihexa. Fosgonimeton failed its primary endpoint in the LIFT-AD Phase 2/3 trial (n=554 enrolled; primary Global Statistical Test endpoint missed; results posted April 2025 [17, regulatory]). It also failed ACT-AD Phase 2 (n=77, primary ERP endpoint not met), and the SHAPE Parkinson's disease dementia trial was halted early. These failures are important context: the HGF/c-Met activation strategy derived from the same rodent pharmacology as Dihexa failed to translate to human cognitive benefit in every completed human trial.

## 3. The HGF/c-Met Oncogenic Tension

### 3.1 HGF/c-Met as an Oncogenic Axis

The oncogenic potential of HGF/c-Met signaling is not a peripheral concern — it is central to how modern cancer biology and oncology pharmacology treat this pathway. HGF/c-Met is one of the most extensively characterized driver pathways in cancer, with aberrant activation documented across a broad malignancy spectrum: hepatocellular carcinoma, non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, glioblastoma, ovarian, pancreatic, and prostate carcinomas [15, mechanism_review; 21, mechanism_review]. When dysregulated — through MET amplification, activating point mutations, exon-skipping alterations, or autocrine HGF loops — the pathway drives tumor cell proliferation, survival, invasion, angiogenesis, and metastasis through downstream PI3K/AKT, Ras/MAPK, JAK/STAT3, SRC, and Wnt/β-catenin cascades [15, mechanism_review].

The clinical pharmacological operationalization of this concern is concrete: capmatinib (Tabrecta; FDA approved May 2020; NDA 213591) and tepotinib (Tepmetko; FDA approved February 2021; NDA 214096) are selective MET kinase inhibitors approved for metastatic NSCLC harboring MET exon 14 skipping mutations, based on objective response rates of 68% and 43% respectively in pivotal trials [16, regulatory]. The FDA would not have approved targeted inhibitors of this pathway in the absence of demonstrated oncogenic dependency. The existence of these drugs is pharmacological proof that chronic c-Met activation drives clinically significant cancer growth.

### 3.2 The Agonist/Antagonist Inversion Problem

Dihexa's purported therapeutic mechanism is the pharmacological inverse of capmatinib and tepotinib: where those drugs block c-Met to slow tumor growth, Dihexa is proposed to potentiate HGF-induced c-Met phosphorylation [1, animal; 10, animal — RETRACTED]. This creates a direct mechanistic tension that requires explicit framing.

**The concern, stated precisely:** If HGF/c-Met signaling potentiation drives procognitive synaptogenesis at low HGF concentrations in healthy neurons (the proposed therapeutic mechanism), the same potentiation could theoretically amplify signaling in cells where HGF/c-Met is already dysregulated or upregulated — including pre-neoplastic or neoplastic cells. In oncology, even modest augmentation of an already-active oncogenic signal can accelerate tumor progression in ways that manifest over years, not weeks. This concern applies with particular force in three scenarios: (1) individuals with subclinical or undetected early malignancy; (2) individuals with germline or somatic MET alterations; (3) chronic grey-market use without oncologic surveillance.

**What has not been studied:** No carcinogenicity studies (rodent long-term or otherwise) have been conducted on Dihexa. No in-vivo tumor-promotion or co-carcinogenesis assays have been published. The entire available preclinical dataset is restricted to cognitive-behavioral outcomes in rodent models assessed over 4–12 weeks — durations structurally incapable of detecting tumor promotion, which typically requires latency of months to years. This is not evidence of safety. It is an unstudied risk domain. Absence of a carcinogenicity study is the normal state for an academic research compound that never entered formal IND development; it does not imply the signal was sought and found absent.

**Fosgonimeton as incomplete translational evidence:** The LIFT-AD trial of fosgonimeton ran 26 weeks in 554 Alzheimer's patients without a reported oncologic safety signal in the trial period [17, regulatory]. However, the trial was not designed or powered to detect oncologic endpoints that typically manifest over years; it failed its primary cognitive endpoint; and fosgonimeton is a chemically distinct compound from Dihexa with different pharmacokinetics. A 26-week human trial of a different molecule is insufficient to bound the tumor-promotion risk for chronic grey-market use of Dihexa.

The theoretical oncogenic concern for a systemic HGF/c-Met potentiator is genuine, grounded in well-established cancer biology, and unresolved by any existing Dihexa-specific data. It should be treated as an open safety question — neither a demonstrated risk nor a dismissed one — with the understanding that the information needed to resolve it does not yet exist.

### 3.2a The Mechanistic Plausibility Without the Retracted Evidence

It is worth separating two distinct questions to be precise about what the retraction of Benoist 2014 [7, animal] and Kawas 2012 [10, animal] actually destroys and what it leaves untouched.

**What the retractions destroy:** The specific claim that Dihexa potentiates HGF at c-Met to drive dendritic spine formation via the c-Met phosphorylation cascade — as demonstrated by the now-invalidated Western blot data in figures 1B and 2A/C of Benoist 2014 — has no non-retracted primary evidence supporting it. The dose-response relationship for c-Met phosphorylation that the Benoist paper described (10⁻¹⁰–10⁻¹² M range), the specific c-Met-dependence shown by the HGF antagonist "Hinge" blocking experiment, and the in-vitro synaptogenesis quantification in the retracted paper are all from a paper whose data integrity has been found compromised. These findings should be treated as if they do not exist in the evidence base.

**What the retractions leave uncertain, not definitively disproven:** The HGF/c-Met pathway is a genuine neuroscience target with documented roles in neuronal survival, axon growth, dendrite branching, and synaptic plasticity independent of the Kawas papers. Multiple studies from entirely independent groups — in developmental neuroscience, neurotrophic factor biology, and oncology — document HGF/c-Met signaling in neuronal contexts [15, mechanism_review]. The question of whether Dihexa, as a peptidomimetic with some structural similarity to known AngIV-binding motifs, has any interaction with the HGF/c-Met system at all is not definitively answered by the retractions — it is left without reliable primary evidence. The retractions do not prove Dihexa cannot bind HGF; they prove the specific evidence claiming it does was produced under conditions where data manipulation occurred, removing that evidence from the reliable corpus.

This distinction matters for understanding the Sun 2021 independent replication [5, animal]. Sun et al. found cognitive rescue and identified PI3K/AKT activation as the mechanism, with PI3K inhibition reversing the cognitive benefit. The Sun paper contains no HGF or c-Met data: it does not measure c-Met phosphorylation and does not test HGF inhibitors. The affirmative finding is that a pathway other than HGF/c-Met — PI3K/AKT — was operative. Speculating that c-Met "could still be upstream" of the PI3K/AKT signal Sun identified is exactly the kind of unfalsifiable rescue move that the retraction should make us resist: it preserves the discredited mechanism by inference from data that say nothing about it. The honest reading is that the sole independent replication found a different pathway and left the HGF/c-Met account without any corroboration, not that it left room to rehabilitate it.

The intellectually honest position post-2025 is: the proposed HGF/c-Met mechanism is unconfirmed by any non-retracted primary evidence; the only independent replication affirmatively identified a different mechanism; and no non-retracted evidence supports HGF/c-Met as the operative pathway for Dihexa's effects.

### 3.3 Literature Concentration and the Single-Group Problem

A candid audit of the Dihexa efficacy and mechanism literature reveals extreme dependence on a single originating research group. A complete enumeration of primary research papers indexed on PubMed with direct mechanistic or efficacy relevance to Dihexa yields 9 papers; 6 of 9 (~67%) are primarily WSU/Harding-Wright in origin (including M3 Biotechnology, a WSU spinout where Wright, Kawas, and Harding all listed dual institutional affiliations on their mid-2010s publications [3, mechanism_review]).

More specifically: the 5 papers that are purely WSU-originating (PMIDs 23055539, 25187433, 25455861, 25649658, 25674052) include both retracted papers, the paper under Expression of Concern, and the 2015 Progress in Neurobiology review co-authored by Kawas. One additional paper (PMID 34703584) is a multi-institutional study with Harding as a co-author. The three genuinely independent papers are: Ho & Nation (2018) systematic review at USC; Sun et al. (2021) at China Pharmaceutical University and Nanjing Medical University; and Wells et al. (2024) at Whitworth University, OHSU, and the University of Washington.

**The concentration figure (~67%) understates the integrity situation.** The load-bearing mechanism evidence within the WSU-group papers is not merely concentrated — it is retracted. The effective evidentiary situation is that the HGF/c-Met mechanism claim rests on: (a) retracted papers (Benoist 2014 [7, animal], Kawas 2012 [10, animal]); (b) a paper under Expression of Concern (McCoy 2013 [1, animal]); and (c) narrative reviews from Kawas co-authors (Wright & Harding 2015 [3, mechanism_review]; Wright & Harding 2019 [22, mechanism_review]). The independent replication (Sun 2021 [5, animal]) explicitly found a different mechanism (PI3K/AKT), and the most recent independent behavioral study (Wells 2024 [8, animal]) found a null result.

### 3.4 The AngIV/AT4 Class Context

The AngIV-analog field bifurcated mechanistically into two competing accounts — IRAP inhibition (Albiston, European groups) and HGF/c-Met potentiation (Harding/Wright) — without resolution. The IRAP/AT4 account has the cleaner integrity record: the foundational Albiston 2001 identification paper [6, in_vitro] and the European IRAP medicinal chemistry program [14, mechanism_review] carry no integrity concerns and were produced by entirely independent groups. The HGF/c-Met account — the mechanistic basis for all Dihexa-specific synaptogenesis and potency claims — is now the account whose foundational papers are retracted.

From a drug-class perspective: the AngIV-analog / IRAP research program is an active academic investigation area without any approved drugs, without any completed human trials, and without regulatory recognition as a drug class. The specific HGF/c-Met branch of this field has been substantially compromised by the 2025 retractions.

## 4. Safety, Dosing, Route, Regulatory Status, and Monitoring

### 4.1 The Honest Safety Picture

**Dihexa has no published human safety data of any kind.** There is no Phase 1 dose-escalation study, no human pharmacokinetic profile, no characterised adverse-effect spectrum in people, and no chronic toxicity, reproductive toxicity, genotoxicity, or carcinogenicity dataset in any species [18, anecdote_aggregate; 2, regulatory]. The compound has never been evaluated in a registered human trial.

The preclinical record is limited to behavioral outcome studies in rodents, conducted primarily by the originating WSU group over durations of 4–12 weeks. No systematic organ-level histopathology or oncogenicity evaluation was reported in any published experiment. The one independent disease-model study (Wells et al. 2024 [8, animal], 5-week 3-NP Huntington's-like rat) reported no adverse findings but was not designed as a toxicology study.

The integrity timeline begins before the 2025 retractions. In September 2021, JPET published Expressions of Concern for both McCoy 2013 (EoC notice PMID 34551989) [1, animal] and Kawas 2012 (EoC notice PMID 34551990) [10, animal] — flagging the primary dosing-anchor and the first HGF/c-Met mechanism paper years before either was retracted. This means the animal dosing record underpinning all preclinical dose extrapolation was formally integrity-flagged in 2021, three years before the 2025 retractions confirmed the underlying manipulation.

### 4.2 The Load-Bearing Theoretical Safety Risk: HGF/c-Met Potentiation and Oncogenesis

This is the most significant safety concern associated with Dihexa, and it is the one with the greatest clinical stakes for long-term use. The full mechanistic case was laid out in Section 3. The safety translation is:

Any person or clinician weighing self-administration of a systemically bioavailable, brain-penetrant c-Met potentiator must treat the theoretical oncogenic concern as a mechanistically grounded open risk with no empirical safety data to bound it. This is not a remote worst-case scenario — it is the direct predictable consequence of the proposed mechanism operating in cells other than the specifically healthy neurons the mechanism is theorized to benefit. The therapeutic rationale for Dihexa requires persistent c-Met agonism, and chronic c-Met agonism is exactly the mechanism that approved cancer drugs are designed to block.

**Drug interaction — absolute contraindication with c-Met inhibitors:** Concurrent use of capmatinib (Tabrecta), tepotinib (Tepmetko), or any investigational c-Met/MET inhibitor creates a direct pharmacodynamic opposition: one agent potentiates c-Met phosphorylation while the other suppresses it. The net effect is unknown and the combination is mechanistically irrational. Patients receiving approved or investigational c-Met inhibitors for any indication have an absolute contraindication to any c-Met potentiator.

### 4.3 Grey-Market-Specific Safety Risks

Beyond the theoretical oncogenic concern, grey-market Dihexa introduces practical hazards absent from the preclinical literature:

- **Chemical identity:** No mandatory identity verification exists. Vendor-labelled "Dihexa" may be a structurally similar analog, a degradation product, or an entirely unrelated compound.
- **Purity:** No pharmacopoeial standard exists. Common contaminants include residual synthesis reagents and process impurities.
- **Sterility:** Injectable forms (subcutaneous) sold through grey-market channels carry direct infection and endotoxin risk; no sterility testing is required or verified by any regulatory body.
- **Dose accuracy:** Vendor-stated concentrations are unverified and not subject to regulatory audit.

### 4.4 Dosing and Route: Preclinical Data Only

**No human dose-response relationship exists.** Extrapolation from rodent doses to humans is speculative; allometric scaling has not been validated for this compound. Every number below is from animal studies with the integrity caveats described throughout.

The McCoy 2013 paper (under EoC, PMID 34551989 [1, animal]) reported oral administration at approximately 1–2 mg/kg/day in rats as the dose range for reversing scopolamine-induced cognitive deficits in Morris water maze tasks [1, animal]. The Sun 2021 APP/PS1 mouse study (independent group) used intragastric administration at 1.44 or 2.88 mg/kg/day for 3 months [5, animal]. Intracerebroventricular (ICV) doses in the range of 0.1–1 nmol and intraperitoneal doses of 0.05–0.5 mg/kg appear across the earlier WSU rodent literature [1, animal]. The 2024 HD-model study used subcutaneous administration over 5 weeks with no cognitive benefit [8, animal].

Grey-market vendors sell Dihexa in oral, topical, and subcutaneous injectable formulations. Because these are anecdote-sourced conventions with no clinical grounding, no specific grey-market doses, frequencies, or cycle lengths are cited in this entry; those conventions cannot ground any dosing decision.

The Harding/Wright group designed Dihexa specifically to be orally bioavailable and BBB-permeable via the N-hexanoyl and C-terminal amide modifications [1, animal; 3, mechanism_review]. The McCoy 2013 paper (under EoC) reported brain penetration via radiolabeling exceeding blood-contamination levels in hippocampus and other regions, and described oral activity in young Sprague-Dawley rats. No independent pharmacokinetic characterisation of Dihexa exists in the peer-reviewed literature. No absolute oral bioavailability fraction, T_max, C_max, plasma half-life, or brain penetration coefficient has been formally determined in any published study.

### 4.5 Regulatory Status

**United States (FDA):** Dihexa is not approved for any human indication. No Investigational New Drug (IND) application has been filed for Dihexa itself, and no New Drug Application (NDA) exists [18, anecdote_aggregate]. Community sources report that in April 2026 the FDA removed Dihexa acetate from its Category 2 restricted-peptide list as part of a 12-peptide reclassification action — however, this claim is sourced only from anecdote-aggregate and vendor-authored material; no primary FDA administrative record confirming this action was located at research access. This claim should not be read as a confirmed FDA administrative action. If confirmed by a primary FDA record, removal from Category 2 would not constitute approval, would not create a prescription or compounding pathway, and would not indicate the agency assessed the compound as safe for human use; it would mean only that the active safety-concern nomination is no longer maintained under that administrative category. Dihexa is not on any FDA-approved drug list and has not been evaluated under the 503A/503B bulk-drug-substance programs governing compounding pharmacy access. It is not a compoundable substance under the current FDA framework.

**European and other jurisdictions:** Dihexa is not approved by EMA, MHRA, TGA, or any other regulatory authority. It lacks any authorised therapeutic use everywhere in the world.

**WADA status:** Dihexa is not explicitly named on the 2026 WADA Prohibited List. However, the Prohibited List's **S0 (Non-Approved Substances)** category functions as a categorical ban on "any pharmacological substance which is not addressed by any of the subsequent sections of the List and with no current approval by any governmental regulatory health authority for human therapeutic use" — including drugs under pre-clinical or clinical development, discontinued drugs, designer drugs, and veterinary drugs [19, regulatory]. As a substance not approved for human therapeutic use by any governmental regulatory authority, Dihexa falls under S0 by this definition, making it prohibited in sport at all times (in and out of competition) for athletes subject to WADA-code-compliant anti-doping organisations. WADA has not published a formal classification ruling for Dihexa specifically. Athletes should seek NADO guidance or consult GlobalDRO before drawing conclusions from this general-rule analysis.

### 4.6 Contraindications, Monitoring, and Stopping Criteria

No validated contraindication list, monitoring protocol, or stopping criteria exist for Dihexa, because the compound has never been evaluated in humans. The following reflects the mechanistic and regulatory picture.

**Strongest precautionary contraindications:**

- **Personal or family cancer history (especially c-Met-implicated cancers):** Given the HGF/c-Met-potentiation mechanism and the complete absence of carcinogenicity data, any personal history of cancer — particularly cancers with documented c-Met involvement (lung, breast, hepatocellular, glioblastoma, gastric, pancreatic) — or a first-degree family history of those cancers constitutes a serious precautionary contraindication. A clinician presented with a patient requesting information about Dihexa should treat this as a hard stop absent carcinogenicity data.
- **Concurrent approved or investigational c-Met/MET inhibitor use (absolute):** Direct mechanistic opposition at the same receptor target; combination is irrational and contraindicated.
- **Immunocompromised individuals:** HGF/c-Met has documented roles in immune cell migration; the net effect of potentiating that pathway in immunocompromised states is unknown.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** No safety data exist; contraindicated by default for any unregistered investigational compound.

**Monitoring posture (no Dihexa-specific biomarker is validated):**

No biomarker validated as a Dihexa-specific safety signal exists. There is no established therapeutic window, no validated pharmacodynamic marker for on-target effect in humans, and no surrogate endpoint for monitoring c-Met potentiation in a clinical context. Given the mechanistic concern, the most defensible surveillance posture for any individual who has used this compound includes:

- Routine cancer surveillance appropriate to age and family history, with heightened attention to any unexplained mass, lymphadenopathy, systemic symptoms, or laboratory changes that might prompt earlier oncological evaluation
- Hepatic function monitoring (LFTs at baseline and periodically) given c-Met's central role in hepatocyte biology and the hepatotoxicity that can accompany c-Met inhibitor therapy as a class effect in the oncology setting
- Avoidance of concomitant agents that further stimulate HGF/c-Met signaling
- **[[biomarkers/hs-crp]] (hs-CRP):** While not a validated Dihexa safety biomarker, hs-CRP provides an objective inflammatory surveillance marker useful in the context of chronic use of a compound whose mechanism involves receptor tyrosine kinase potentiation and whose carcinogenicity is unstudied. A rising hs-CRP trend during use — absent an intercurrent infection or other identifiable cause — warrants clinical evaluation.
- **Liver function tests (ALT, AST, bilirubin):** Baseline and periodic monitoring given c-Met's role in hepatocyte biology; not because hepatotoxicity has been reported for Dihexa, but because the mechanistic class warrants the precaution in the absence of any formal safety characterization.

**Stopping criteria:**

Any signal of unexplained proliferative, haematological, or hepatic change that emerges temporally with use should prompt cessation pending evaluation. The compound should not be resumed without oncological clearance. For individuals subject to anti-doping rules where WADA S0 prohibition applies: immediate cessation and NADO consultation.

### 5.1 The Concentration Audit

### 5.2 The Kawas Integrity Story

The integrity history of this literature is worth understanding in its full scope, because it is not a simple case of one paper with a figure error.

Leen Kawas completed her PhD at WSU under Harding and Wright, co-authoring multiple papers in the Dihexa/AngIV-HGF program from 2011–2014. She subsequently co-founded M3 Biotechnology (the WSU spinout) and eventually became CEO of Athira Pharma — the NASDAQ-listed company that raised approximately $250 million on the strength of this research program and the fosgonimeton clinical candidate [20, vendor_label].

In 2021, JPET simultaneously issued Expressions of Concern against four WSU papers from the Harding/Wright/Kawas group [18, anecdote_aggregate]. A special committee investigation convened by Athira's board of directors concluded that Kawas had systematically altered images in her 2011 doctoral dissertation and in at least four co-authored publications from the 2011–2014 period. WSU revoked Kawas's PhD. She resigned as Athira CEO in October 2021 pending the investigation outcome. In January 2025, Athira Pharma (by then renamed LeonaBio) agreed to pay $4 million to settle Department of Justice allegations of data falsification in NIH grant applications supported by the manipulated WSU figures [18, anecdote_aggregate]. A separate securities class action seeking approximately $10 million was also filed.

The April 2025 retractions of Kawas 2012 [10, animal] and Benoist 2014 [7, animal] followed the full WSU investigation and confirmed the specific figure-manipulation findings. The McCoy 2013 EoC [1, animal] remains unresolved as of June 2026 — that paper's integrity status is thus still formally open.

This institutional history is directly load-bearing for how the literature should be read. The academic-to-commercial pipeline (WSU lab → M3 Biotechnology / Athira Pharma → NASDAQ IPO) created structural publication incentives that are now confirmed to have coincided with data manipulation in the foundational papers. The magnitude of the commercial enterprise built on this research — hundreds of millions of investor dollars, thousands of patients enrolled in fosgonimeton trials — does not confirm or disconfirm whether the underlying biology is real. But it establishes why independent replication was essential and why the absence of such replication for the HGF/c-Met mechanism is a critical gap, not a minor footnote.

### 5.3 The Athira/LeonaBio to Dihexa Distinction

The commercialization pathway for this research ran: WSU laboratory → M3 Biotechnology (spinout, 2012) → Athira Pharma (renamed, 2019) → NASDAQ IPO (2020) → LeonaBio (renamed again, after clinical program failures). The clinical candidate throughout this pathway was fosgonimeton (ATH-1017) — a distinct, patented, subcutaneously administered small-molecule HGF/c-Met modulator, not the Dihexa peptide itself.

Dihexa (the grey-market compound) has never entered clinical trials. The buyers of grey-market Dihexa are purchasing a research chemical with no clinical trial history, supported by a now-retracted foundational mechanism literature, from unregulated vendors, and conflating their purchase with the clinical fosgonimeton program. The conflation is wrong in two directions: (a) fosgonimeton is a different compound; (b) the fosgonimeton program failed every clinical efficacy endpoint it attempted. LeonaBio has since pivoted to lasofoxifene (breast cancer) and ATH-1105 (ALS) as its current lead programs [20, vendor_label].

### 5.4 The Fosgonimeton Clinical Failures and What They Do and Do Not Mean

The fosgonimeton (ATH-1017) clinical program is frequently cited in grey-market Dihexa discourse in two opposing and mutually inconsistent ways. Some users cite the existence of the Phase 2/3 LIFT-AD trial as validation that the HGF/c-Met mechanism in Alzheimer's is real and clinically pursued. Others, post-2024, cite the fosgonimeton failures as proof that the entire mechanistic class is a dead end. Both framings overstate what the clinical data can support.

**What fosgonimeton's clinical failures actually establish:** Fosgonimeton (a distinct compound, administered subcutaneously, with its own pharmacokinetics and receptor engagement profile) did not improve cognition in mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's patients over 26 weeks in the LIFT-AD Phase 2/3 trial (n=554; primary Global Statistical Test endpoint missed [17, regulatory]). This is a genuine failure of a human trial testing the HGF/c-Met cognitive enhancement hypothesis in the population and duration specified. It provides useful translational information: the best-resourced human test of this mechanistic strategy — conducted by a company with professional trial infrastructure, a specific clinical candidate optimized for human pharmacokinetics, and n=554 enrolled — did not produce a statistically significant cognitive benefit in its target population. The Phase 2 ACT-AD trial also missed its primary ERP endpoint in a smaller earlier study. The SHAPE trial in Parkinson's disease dementia was halted early, meaning it never provided a completed efficacy readout.

**What these failures do not establish:** They do not prove that HGF/c-Met potentiation cannot work in any population, at any dose, with any compound. Clinical trial failure can reflect a multitude of factors beyond mechanistic incorrectness: wrong patient population, insufficient dose or duration, wrong biomarker endpoint, heterogeneous patient sample, or — as is increasingly recognized in Alzheimer's disease trials — the fundamental difficulty of treating established neurodegeneration rather than preventing it. These caveats are standard in clinical pharmacology and do not make the fosgonimeton failures less real, but they do prevent interpreting a single failed trial as proof of mechanistic impossibility.

**The critical distinction that neither framing acknowledges:** Fosgonimeton is not Dihexa. The two compounds share a mechanistic rationale derived from the same academic research program, but they are structurally different molecules with different pharmacokinetics, different routes of administration, different receptor engagement profiles, and — crucially — different evidence bases. Fosgonimeton has human safety and pharmacokinetic data (Phase 1 studies in 88 participants found dose-proportional PK and no serious adverse events); Dihexa has none. Fosgonimeton was specifically engineered for improved pharmacokinetics relative to the peptide precursors; Dihexa is those peptide precursors. Conflating fosgonimeton's human data (in either direction — to validate Dihexa or to condemn it) commits the same category error in both directions. The only accurate statement is: the only human program derived from this mechanistic lineage that was actually tested in humans failed its efficacy endpoints, and the compound tested was not Dihexa.

### 5.5 The Academic-to-Commercial Pipeline and Structural Conflict of Interest

The Dihexa story is also a case study in how academic-to-commercial translation can create structural publication incentives that, in the worst cases, coincide with data manipulation. Understanding this pipeline helps contextualize why independent replication matters so much for this particular compound class.

The research program that produced Dihexa ran inside a university laboratory (WSU, Harding/Wright) simultaneously with the formation of a commercial spinout (M3 Biotechnology, subsequently Athira Pharma). All three co-founders — Wright, Kawas, and Harding — listed dual WSU/M3 Biotechnology institutional affiliations on their mid-2010s publications [3, mechanism_review]. The publications served multiple simultaneous functions: scientific literature, patent support, investor communications basis, and regulatory submission background. Athira's 2020 NASDAQ IPO raised approximately $250 million; the company's market capitalization at peak was estimated in the hundreds of millions. Under these conditions, the publication record was not purely scientific output — it was also a financial asset. This does not imply that every finding in the Dihexa/AngIV-HGF literature was manufactured. It does imply that the structural pressures for positive results in this research program were substantially greater than those in a purely academic context, and that the value of independent replication — which would have been difficult to incentivize in the same period — was correspondingly higher.

The WSU investigation's finding that Kawas manipulated figures in her doctoral dissertation (which preceded the M3 Biotechnology commercialization) and in at least four subsequent co-authored papers (which occurred during the commercialization period) is consistent with a researcher operating under multiple forms of pressure simultaneously. WSU revoked her PhD. The extent to which other authors on the multi-authored WSU papers were aware of the manipulations is not publicly documented beyond the institutional findings. What is documented is the outcome: two retracted papers, one paper under Expression of Concern, a $4M DOJ settlement, a ~$10M securities class action, a CEO resignation, and an academic career ending in degree revocation [18, anecdote_aggregate].

### 5.6 Grey-Market Realism

Dihexa circulates on research-chemical and nootropic vendor markets in oral, topical, and injectable formulations, typically marketed on the "100 million times more potent than BDNF" claim — which, as documented throughout this report, derives from the now-retracted Benoist 2014 paper and an in-vitro assay produced by the same group whose figures were subsequently found to contain fabricated elements [7, animal; 21, anecdote_aggregate].

Several structural hazards attend the grey-market supply:

First, the entire efficacy and safety claim rests on rodent animal studies — primarily intracerebroventricular, subcutaneous, or oral dosing in transgenic mice or scopolamine-impaired Sprague-Dawley rats — produced mostly by the originating group and subject to the integrity concerns documented above. The single independent animal replication found a different mechanism (Sun 2021 [5, animal]); the most recent independent animal study found a null result (Wells 2024 [8, animal]). No human data exist.

Second, the c-Met oncogenic concern is not theoretical in the sense of being remote or speculative. It is the direct pharmacological consequence of the proposed mechanism operating in a biological context beyond healthy neurons. The grey-market user has no oncologic surveillance, no carcinogenicity-study basis for risk assessment, and no medical monitoring framework.

Third, purity, identity, and sterility are unverifiable in grey-market supply chains. Injectable preparations carry direct infectious and endotoxin risk. Claims of oral bioavailability for the intact peptide in humans are not supported by any published human pharmacokinetic data.

Fourth, buyers frequently read the existence of Athira's clinical program as validation of Dihexa's safety and efficacy. The clinical program: (a) used a different compound; (b) failed its primary endpoints in every trial; and (c) operated through a company now confirmed to have been built on papers containing falsified figures.

## 6. Honest Bottom Line

Dihexa occupies a singular position in the peptide research landscape: it is the compound most aggressively marketed in grey-market nootropic circles as the most potent procognitive agent ever studied, and it is simultaneously the compound whose foundational mechanism papers have been retracted for data fabrication, whose primary dosing-anchor paper is under Expression of Concern, whose proposed HGF/c-Met mechanism has zero non-retracted non-WSU experimental support, whose one independent cognitive-rescue replication identified a different mechanism, whose most recent behavioral study found a null result, and which has zero human data of any kind while being sold to users who believe they are taking something validated by a clinical pharmaceutical program — a program that actually used a different compound, and failed.

**The retraction of Benoist 2014 [7, animal] and Kawas 2012 [10, animal] means the following things are currently without primary-literature support:**
- That Dihexa acts via HGF/c-Met potentiation (the retracted mechanism papers are the entire evidentiary basis for this claim from any laboratory)
- That Dihexa is "seven orders of magnitude more potent than BDNF" in synaptogenesis (this comparison appeared in the retracted Benoist paper, from an in-vitro assay by the originating group)
- That Dihexa produces dendritic spine density increases via HGF/c-Met signaling specifically (the spine-density data in the retracted paper were from cultures in the figures found to contain manipulated data)

**The following things remain plausibly suggested by the non-retracted literature:**
- That the broader AngIV/AT4/IRAP procognitive axis is real (Albiston 2001 [6, in_vitro], Wright et al. 1999 [12, animal], Ho & Nation 2018 systematic review [11, meta_analysis] — all unretracted, some independent)
- That some cognitive-rescue effect of Dihexa specifically may exist in disease models (Sun 2021 [5], independent group, PI3K/AKT mechanism, APP/PS1 mice — though tier-2 open-access journal)
- That the McCoy 2013 oral behavioral data [1] might reflect a real rodent cognitive effect, even though the paper is under EoC and the mechanism it proposes (HGF/c-Met) is unsupported

**What this means in practice:** The compound being purchased on the grey market is a research chemical whose most specific and dramatic claims — the BDNF potency comparison, the HGF/c-Met synaptogenesis mechanism — rest on retracted papers containing fabricated figures. Its c-Met potentiation mechanism, if real, carries a theoretical oncogenic risk that has never been assessed in any carcinogenicity study. Its dosing conventions extrapolate from rodent data in an integrity-flagged paper. No human being has been systematically studied under this compound. The clinical pharmaceutical program whose existence grey-market buyers often cite as reassurance used a different compound and failed.

For any individual, clinician, or athlete considering Dihexa: the honest evidence tier is D, the oncogenic risk is unresolved, the mechanism is unconfirmed, and the marketing claims trace to retracted science.
