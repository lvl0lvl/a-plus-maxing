# MOTS-c (Mitochondrial Open Reading Frame of the 12S rRNA Type-C)

## Metadata

- **class:** peptide (mitochondrial-derived peptide, MDP)
- **evidence_tier:** C — The metabolic/exercise efficacy evidence is almost entirely from mouse models receiving pharmacological IP doses; human data is exclusively observational (endogenous circulating peptide as biomarker), not therapeutic. ## Summary

MOTS-c is a 16-amino-acid mitochondrial-derived peptide (sequence: MRWQEMGYIFYPRKLR; MW ~2174.6 Da) encoded by a short open-reading frame (sORF) within MT-RNR1, the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene. It was identified by Lee, Cohen, and colleagues at USC in a landmark 2015 Cell Metabolism paper [1]. The peptide is translated in the cytoplasm from a mitochondria-exported transcript and acts primarily on skeletal muscle by inhibiting the folate one-carbon cycle, driving AICAR accumulation and AMPK activation. Under metabolic stress, it also translocates to the nucleus to regulate antioxidant-response-element genes [2].

**The honest evidence state, stated once and up front:**

The case for MOTS-c as a metabolic or performance-enhancing therapeutic rests almost entirely on mouse studies — dietary-obese inbred male mice receiving IP injections at pharmacological doses (0.5–15 mg/kg/day), with group sizes of n=6–10. Every major mechanistic and efficacy finding through 2021 originates from the Cohen/Lee USC lab, whose principals (Pinchas Cohen and Changhan Lee) were simultaneous co-founders and shareholders of CohBar Inc., the company formed to commercialize the intellectual property. No human trial of exogenous native MOTS-c efficacy has ever been published or completed; one Phase 2a trial (NCT07505745, Hudson Biotech / Peking University Shenzhen Hospital, China; subcutaneous MOTS-c, 12 weeks, adults with prediabetes and overweight/obesity, n=120, primary endpoint: OGTT-derived insulin sensitivity index; recruiting as of February 2026, estimated primary completion February 2027) is underway with no results available. The only human clinical data in the entire MDP/MOTS-c class is for **CB4211**, an engineered and patent-protected analog — not the native peptide — whose Phase 1b formulation was deemed unsuitable for further development; the CB4211 program ended when CohBar commenced dissolution in late 2023. Human evidence for the native MOTS-c peptide is exclusively observational: endogenous circulating MOTS-c correlates with metabolic states (lower in T2DM, variable in obesity), but these associations are not causal and do not license inference about what happens when you inject the peptide exogenously. The "exercise mimetic" label is a consumer/media extrapolation not supported by any human interventional data. MOTS-c is WADA-prohibited under Section S4 (Metabolic Modulators) and not currently compoundable under FDA 503A rules.

**evidence_tier C / risk_tier experimental.** This entry's value is its honesty about a heavily hyped, single-lab, commercially-failed peptide.

## 1. Identity, Discovery, and Molecular Mechanism

### 1.1 Identity and amino acid sequence

MOTS-c (mitochondrial open reading frame of the 12S rRNA type-c) is a 16-amino-acid mitochondrial-derived peptide with the sequence MRWQEMGYIFYPRKLR [1, animal]. It is encoded by a 51-base-pair sORF embedded within MT-RNR1, the mitochondrial gene encoding the 12S ribosomal RNA subunit [1, animal]. Molecular weight: ~2174.6 Da; formula C₁₀₁H₁₅₂N₂₈O₂₂S₂. The sequence is conserved across mammalian species, consistent with functional constraint.

MOTS-c is distinct from humanin, the best-studied MDP: humanin is encoded by MT-RNR2 (the 16S rRNA gene) and acts primarily through known cell-surface receptors on hypothalamic and islet cells, while MOTS-c targets skeletal muscle as its primary site of action and, uniquely among MDPs, can undergo intracellular nuclear translocation under metabolic stress [1, animal]. Humanin also has a more advanced human PK and clinical characterization than MOTS-c — its pharmacokinetics have been described in human volunteers and it has reached early-phase human studies; MOTS-c's human data remains exclusively observational (endogenous biomarker), with no pharmacokinetic profile from exogenous administration in any human study as of mid-2026. This gap sharpens the "MOTS-c human data is thin" context relative to even the broader MDP class.

### 1.2 Translation: cytoplasmic, not mitochondrial

MOTS-c presents an unusual translation paradox. Using the standard mitochondrial genetic code, the sORF would produce tandem start/stop codons and be untranslatable within the mitochondrial matrix. Translation therefore occurs obligatorily in the cytoplasm using the standard genetic code, implying that the polyadenylated MOTS-c transcript is exported from the mitochondria before being translated on cytoplasmic ribosomes [1, animal]. The export mechanism remains uncharacterized; selective depletion of mitochondrial RNA causes loss of cytoplasmic MOTS-c protein over time, confirming the mitochondrial origin of the transcript. This cytoplasmic-translation-from-a-mitochondrial-transcript model is the current consensus, not yet challenged by an experimentally confirmed alternative.

### 1.3 Discovery: Lee et al. 2015

MOTS-c was identified by Lee, Cohen, and colleagues in a 2015 Cell Metabolism paper [1, animal]. The authors performed an in-silico scan of the mitochondrial genome for sORFs with strong Kozak consensus sequences, identified the 51-bp sORF within MT-RNR1, synthesized the peptide, and characterized it in multiple cell lines and in mouse metabolic models. Key in-vitro models: HEK293 (human embryonic kidney), HeLa and HeLa-ρ⁰ cells (mitochondrial-DNA-depleted), L6 rat myoblasts differentiated into myotubes. Key in-vivo models: CD-1 outbred mice (n=10 per group, high-fat diet); C57BL/6 mice (n=6–8, glucose clamp studies). The founding paper established MOTS-c as circulating and active in plasma, with primary metabolic action on skeletal muscle.

### 1.4 Molecular mechanism

#### 1.4.1 Folate–methionine cycle inhibition and AICAR accumulation

The primary mechanistic axis proposed by Lee et al. 2015 operates as follows in cell lines and mouse skeletal muscle [1, animal]:

1. MOTS-c depletes intracellular 5-methyl-tetrahydrofolate (5Me-THF), disrupting the folate one-carbon pool.
2. Depletion of 5Me-THF blocks de-novo purine biosynthesis, causing accumulation of AICAR (5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide) — measured at >20-fold increase in MOTS-c-treated cells vs. controls.
3. AICAR is a well-characterized endogenous AMPK activator; the resulting AMPK phosphorylation occurs independently of changes in the canonical AMP/ATP ratio.

This mechanistic chain was established in cell lines (HEK293, L6 myotubes) and mouse skeletal muscle. It has **not** been demonstrated in humans. A 2016 review by Lee, Kim, and Cohen summarizes this pathway and proposes MOTS-c's role in regulating muscle and fat metabolism [18, mechanism_review], but adds no new primary data.

The folate–AICAR pathway is mechanistically distinctive because it bypasses the canonical energetic trigger for AMPK (a rising AMP/ATP ratio, as occurs during exercise or energy deprivation) in favor of a nucleotide biosynthesis intermediary. This matters for predicting how exogenous MOTS-c would behave at varying doses: unlike direct AMPK agonists such as metformin or salicylate, MOTS-c's route through folate depletion means its potency is partly contingent on the intracellular folate pool size — a variable that differs with nutritional status, MTHFR genotype, and folate supplementation. Whether this confers dose-response advantages or unpredictability in humans is unknown, because the AICAR-accumulation pathway has never been directly measured in human skeletal muscle after exogenous MOTS-c administration. Every claim about the folate/AICAR route in humans is an extrapolation from cell lines and mice.

#### 1.4.2 AMPK activation

#### 1.4.2 AMPK activation

AMPK activation downstream of AICAR accumulation was confirmed by Western blot for phospho-AMPK in vitro, and by metabolic phenotyping (improved glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity) in mouse models [1, animal]. The AMPK dependence was further confirmed in the 2018 Kim et al. study, where AMPK inhibition (compound C) blocked MOTS-c nuclear translocation [2, in_vitro].

#### 1.4.3 Mitochondrial-to-nuclear retrograde signaling

A second mechanistic layer was described by Kim, Son, Benayoun & Lee (2018, Cell Metabolism) [2, in_vitro]. Under metabolic stress stimuli — glucose restriction, serum deprivation, or oxidative stress — MOTS-c translocates from cytoplasm to nucleus within 30 minutes (demonstrated in HEK293 and C2C12 cell lines). Once nuclear, MOTS-c directly regulates antioxidant response element (ARE)-containing genes, acting in concert with NRF2/Nrf2-linked stress-response pathways to modulate oxidative defense gene expression [2, in_vitro]. Nuclear translocation is AMPK-dependent: pharmacological AMPK inhibition prevents nuclear entry [2, in_vitro]. This positions MOTS-c as a retrograde mitochondrial-to-nuclear signaling molecule — a role not described for any prior MDP and one that is mechanistically unusual: signaling information flows from the mitochondria (encoded in the peptide's synthesis) to the nucleus (where the peptide directly contacts chromatin-associated transcription factors).

The retrograde signaling model carries an important structural implication. If nuclear MOTS-c activity requires prior AMPK activation (which in turn requires AICAR accumulation from folate depletion), then the nucleus-regulatory function is downstream of the metabolic sensing function — a two-step cascade in which the metabolic stimulus (MOTS-c → folate depletion → AICAR → AMPK → MOTS-c nuclear entry) gates the transcriptional response. This integrated model is intellectually compelling but was constructed entirely from cell-culture experiments using pharmacological concentrations of exogenous MOTS-c. The concentrations required to drive nuclear translocation in cell lines (reported in the Kim 2018 Cell Metabolism paper) have not been compared to physiological circulating concentrations — a gap that matters for any inference about what endogenous or supplemental MOTS-c does in a living animal or human.

**Evidence classification:** all mechanistic evidence is from cell culture (in vitro). Nuclear translocation under in-vivo metabolic stress conditions in animal or human tissue has not been directly imaged.

#### 1.4.4 Exercise induction

Reynolds et al. (2021, Nature Communications) showed that MOTS-c is induced by exercise in human skeletal muscle and circulation, and that exogenous MOTS-c administration improved physical capacity and muscle homeostasis in young (2-month), middle-aged (12-month), and aged (22-month) mice [3, animal]. Human exercise data (circulating MOTS-c levels pre/post exercise) were reported in a cross-sectional cohort of 10 healthy young men, but mechanistic human data remain absent. This paper did not establish the folate/AICAR/AMPK chain in humans — it showed the upstream induction signal (exercise) and downstream functional outputs (physical capacity) in mouse models.

#### 1.4.5 MOTS-c within the broader mitokine biology

MOTS-c belongs to a growing class of peptides encoded by mitochondrial non-coding RNA regions — a class that also includes humanin (HN), the small humanin-like peptides (SHLPs), and SHMOOSE (another recently identified 12S rRNA-encoded peptide). This is a young field: the first MDP (humanin) was discovered in 2001, and as of 2026 the number of confirmed MDPs with characterised biology is still in single digits [17, mechanism_review]. MOTS-c is the most-studied of the non-humanin MDPs, but "most-studied" here means approximately a dozen primary papers, all in the same mechanistic framework, mostly from the same group.

What distinguishes MOTS-c from humanin at the mechanistic level is the primary site of action. Humanin acts largely through extracellular receptors (CNTFR/IL6ST and FPRL1) and protects neuronal and pancreatic beta cells; MOTS-c's principal target is the intracellular folate metabolism of skeletal muscle cells. This distinction matters clinically: humanin's receptor targets are well-defined and could in principle be engaged pharmacologically by receptor agonists; MOTS-c's intracellular folate-pathway action requires cellular internalization and is pharmacologically more complex to replicate. CohBar's rationale for engineering CB4211 was partly to improve uptake and stability, but the internalization mechanism of the native peptide in human skeletal muscle cells has not been established in a primary publication.

### 1.5 The m.1382A>C SNP (Lys14Gln variant)

Fuku et al. (2015, Aging Cell) identified the m.1382A>C polymorphism (rs111033358), which lies within the MOTS-c-coding region of MT-RNR1 [4, mechanism_review]. This variant causes a Lys14Gln amino acid substitution (K14Q) in position 14 of the 16-amino-acid peptide. The SNP is part of the mtDNA D4b2 haplogroup, enriched in Northeast Asian (particularly Japanese) populations. Fuku et al. proposed — in a hypothesis correspondence paper, not a primary cohort study — that m.1382A>C may contribute to exceptional longevity in Japanese centenarians [4, mechanism_review].

A subsequent large-scale analysis (Zempo et al. 2021, Aging [Albany NY]; n=27,527 across three Japanese/Japanese-American cohorts: J-MICC n=11,852, MEC n=3,387, TMM n=12,288; centenarian sub-cohort n=736) found the m.1382A>C C-allele was associated with a **65% higher prevalence of type 2 diabetes in sedentary males** but not females [5, cohort]. Importantly, this pro-diabetogenic association was sedentary-specific: the T2D-risk signal was detected in inactive males and did not generalize across the physical-activity spectrum, implying that the metabolic cost of carrying the K14Q variant may be offset or masked by habitual exercise — a nuance directly relevant to the exercise/longevity discussion in §4 (see §4.4). The centenarian analysis found **no significant difference** in m.1382A>C allele frequency between centenarians and controls — directly refuting the Fuku longevity hypothesis. In-vitro and mouse (CD-1) experiments confirmed that the K14Q-MOTS-c variant has reduced positive charge and increased hydrophobicity (PROVEAN score –4.0, deleterious), and fails to produce insulin-sensitizing effects at doses effective for wild-type MOTS-c [5, cohort].

The sex-specificity of the pro-diabetogenic association (males only, not females) in Zempo 2021 is unexplained and may reflect differences in MOTS-c receptor expression, sex-hormone interactions with AMPK signaling pathways, or differential muscle mass between sexes altering the metabolic consequence of a partially non-functional MOTS-c peptide. The Fuku and Zempo findings are not necessarily flatly contradictory: the original Fuku longevity association was drawn from an earlier, much smaller centenarian cohort where statistical power to detect modest effects was low, and the D4b2 haplogroup context (which includes many other mitochondrial variants beyond m.1382A>C) complicates single-SNP attribution. However, the Zempo 2021 study is by far the larger and better-powered analysis, and its centenarian arm shows no signal; the most defensible reading of the current literature is that the longevity-genetics claim for MOTS-c is unestablished.

**Status of the longevity-genetics claim:** not supported by the best available evidence. The Zempo 2021 large-cohort refutation (n=27,527; n=736 centenarians) is the current state of the literature. MOTS-c's genetic contribution to exceptional human longevity is unestablished. The SNP finding does establish, however, that variation at position 14 of MOTS-c has measurable metabolic consequences in large human populations — consistent with the peptide's AMPK-mediated mechanism being functionally relevant in humans, even if the translational implications for exogenous administration remain unestablished.

## 2. Literature Concentration and Conflict of Interest — A First-Class Caveat

**This section appears before the indication-specific evidence sections because the concentration finding fundamentally determines how that evidence should be read.**

### 2.1 Single-lab concentration: the Cohen/Lee USC group

Every landmark efficacy and mechanistic characterization paper from MOTS-c's identification through 2021 originates from the Pinchas Cohen / Changhan David Lee group at the USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology:

| Paper | Year | Journal | First Author | Group |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lee et al. — MOTS-c promotes metabolic homeostasis | 2015 | Cell Metabolism | Lee C | Cohen/Lee |
| Lee, Kim, Cohen — regulating muscle and fat | 2016 | Free Radic Biol Med | Lee C | Cohen/Lee |
| Kim et al. — nuclear translocation under metabolic stress | 2018 | Cell Metabolism | Kim KH | Cohen/Lee |
| Reynolds et al. — exercise-induced, aging, muscle homeostasis | 2021 | Nature Communications | Reynolds JC | Cohen/Lee |

Among the four landmark efficacy/mechanism papers through 2021, Cohen/Lee USC holds a **100% share**. When the broader literature is counted across all 16 distinct primary studies in the corpus (including independent observational human cohort studies from Qatar, China, Turkey, and New Zealand, as well as the post-2023 independent animal work from Chinese and New Zealand groups), the Cohen/Lee cluster accounts for approximately 9 of 16 primaries (~56%) because co-authorship by Cohen or Lee appears on several of the human cohort papers (Qin 2018, Sequeira 2021, Yoon 2026, Dieli-Conwright 2021, Zempo 2021). Dieli-Conwright 2021 [15, open_label] (USC/Cohen-affiliated) is included in this COI count: Cohen P and co-authors Wan J and Kim SJ are USC group members, and Cohen is disclosed as a co-author on the paper. The foundational therapeutic efficacy claim has **no independent primary replication**.

Independent primary research appeared only from 2023 onward, all in animal models (Tang et al. 2023 [19, animal]; Yang et al. 2024 [20, animal]; Pham et al. 2025 [21, animal]), following the mechanistic scaffold established by Cohen/Lee. Three independent groups replicating AMPK-mediated metabolic benefit in rodents — from institutions in China (two separate groups) and New Zealand — does constitute meaningful corroboration of the mechanism: these are not CohBar-affiliated labs, were not conducting coordinated studies, and each arrived at AMPK-mediated benefit via different disease models. This is not nothing. What this independent replication does not yet provide is: a disciplined dose-response replication of the specific Lee et al. 2015 insulin-sensitization paradigm (same dose, same strain, same route), or any human mechanistic data. The mechanism in rodents has corroboration from three independent labs; the translational gap to humans remains fully open.

This is among the highest single-group concentrations in the foundational peptide literature. Any effect estimate from MOTS-c preclinical research must carry this caveat: there is no independent animal-model replication of the core insulin-sensitization claim as it was originally published (specific dose, strain, route, outcome).

### 2.2 Conflict of interest: the USC–CohBar nexus

**CohBar Inc.** is a biotechnology company co-founded by Pinchas Cohen, incorporated around the intellectual property of mitochondrial-derived peptides [24, regulatory]. The foundational MOTS-c intellectual property was developed at **USC (University of Southern California)** — a private institution; Cohen and Lee's laboratory is at the USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology. CohBar entered into an exclusive license agreement dated August 6, 2013 covering these MDP assets [24, regulatory].

- Cohen was a co-founder, stockholder, and board member of CohBar.
- Lee is disclosed as a consultant and shareholder of CohBar in both the 2018 Cell Metabolism paper [2, in_vitro] and the 2021 Nature Communications paper [3, animal] — in the papers' own COI statements.
- U.S. Patent 11,111,271 (granted September 2021) covers CB4211 compositions and methods of treatment for NASH; CohBar is the assignee [24, regulatory].

The two scientists whose laboratory produced all foundational primary evidence for MOTS-c were simultaneously financial stakeholders in the principal commercial vehicle attempting to monetize it. This COI is disclosed in the relevant publications and constitutes a first-class caveat when interpreting the literature's strength.

To be precise about what this COI concern does and does not imply: disclosed financial conflicts do not by themselves render data fraudulent. The individual findings from Lee et al. 2015 and Reynolds et al. 2021 — glucose clamp results, metabolomic changes, running performance in aged mice — are reported in Cell Metabolism and Nature Communications, journals with peer review. The concern is not that the data were fabricated; it is that: (a) the interpretation of ambiguous results, the selection of outcome measures, and the framing of effect sizes are all susceptible to motivated reasoning that peer review may not catch when the reviewers do not have access to raw data; (b) independent replication — the standard mechanism for catching artifacts — is entirely absent for the core metabolic efficacy claims because no independent lab published first-author primary MOTS-c efficacy data before 2023; and (c) the commercial failure of CohBar/CB4211 in 2023 did not trigger a re-evaluation of the preclinical claims in the way that a failed Phase 3 clinical trial sometimes prompts reexamination of animal-model predictions. The net effect is that the preclinical MOTS-c literature has never been subjected to the external stress-test that commercial or independent replication would provide.

## 3. Metabolic and Insulin-Sensitivity Evidence

**Key framing:** All evidence for exogenous MOTS-c as a therapeutic derives from rodent (mouse) studies. No published peer-reviewed RCT or open-label trial of exogenous MOTS-c administration in humans exists as of mid-2026 (one Phase 2a trial, NCT07505745 [Hudson Biotech / Peking University Shenzhen Hospital], is recruiting). Human evidence is exclusively observational/cohort, treating endogenous circulating MOTS-c as a biomarker; associations do not establish causation and do not license inference about the effects of exogenous administration.

### 3.1 Rodent metabolic evidence (exogenous MOTS-c as therapeutic)

The foundational rodent characterization was published by Lee et al. 2015 [1, animal] in Cell Metabolism, across six distinct mouse experiments:

**Experiment 1 (glucose tolerance).** Male C57BL/6 mice (n=7/group) received intraperitoneal MOTS-c 5 mg/kg/day for 7 days. Glucose clearance was significantly enhanced relative to vehicle-injected controls.

**Experiment 2 (hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp).** Male C57BL/6 mice on a 60%-fat diet (n=6–8/group) received MOTS-c 5 mg/kg/day IP for 7 days. The exogenous glucose infusion rate required to maintain euglycemia increased ~30%, driven by enhanced insulin-stimulated glucose disposal in skeletal muscle (elevated Akt phosphorylation, increased GLUT4 expression). Hepatic glucose production was not significantly altered, indicating primary action in skeletal muscle, not liver.

**Experiment 3 (obesity prevention).** Male CD-1 mice, 8 weeks old, fed a 60%-calorie-fat diet (n=10/group) received MOTS-c 0.5 mg/kg/day IP for 8 weeks. Treated mice showed significantly lower body weight and fat mass versus controls with identical caloric intake. Hepatic lipid accumulation was dramatically reduced. AMPK was activated in skeletal muscle; hyperinsulinemia was prevented.

**Experiment 4 (age-related insulin resistance).** Male C57BL/6 mice (young: 3 months; middle-aged: 12 months; n=6/group) received MOTS-c 5 mg/kg/day IP for 7 days. Insulin-stimulated glucose uptake in aged animals was restored to levels comparable to young controls.

A follow-up metabolomics study by Kim et al. 2019 [6, animal] in male C57BL/6J mice on a high-fat diet (n=7/group; 2.5 mg/kg twice daily IP for three days) found that MOTS-c treatment reduced three metabolic pathways upregulated in obesity and T2DM models: sphingolipid metabolism, monoacylglycerol metabolism, and dicarboxylate metabolism. Blood glucose was significantly lower after three days of treatment.

**Summary of exogenous-therapeutic evidence:** all statistically significant metabolic effects of MOTS-c administration are from male mice (CD-1 and C57BL/6 strains); the largest experiments used n=10. No female rodent data, no primate data, and no published human therapeutic trial.

### 3.2 Human circulating-MOTS-c evidence (endogenous peptide as biomarker)

#### Type 2 diabetes

Ramanjaneya et al. 2019 [7, cohort] (n=225 total Qatari adults: 68 controls, 33 prediabetes, 31 well-controlled T2DM, 93 poorly-controlled T2DM) found serum MOTS-c significantly lower in T2DM versus healthy controls (p=0.007): 235 ± 182 pg/mL in controls vs. 158 ± 137 pg/mL in poorly-controlled T2DM (−27%). MOTS-c negatively correlated with HbA1c (r=−0.233, p<0.001). Association with insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) did not survive multivariate adjustment. Reverse causation (lower MOTS-c as a consequence of metabolic dysfunction) cannot be excluded.

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis by Zhou et al. [8, meta_analysis] (7 studies, 11 comparison groups, 602 participants) found MOTS-c was significantly reduced in T2DM vs. controls (SMD=−0.89; 95% CI −1.12 to −0.65). In obesity, the direction depended on BMI threshold: at BMI >28 kg/m², MOTS-c was significantly elevated (SMD=+0.51; 95% CI 0.21 to 0.81), whereas when overweight participants (BMI 24–28) were included, no significant change emerged. The authors propose a biphasic model: MOTS-c secretion may rise early in metabolic imbalance (obesity) but fall as obesity progresses to overt T2DM [8, meta_analysis].

#### Obesity and insulin resistance

Du et al. 2018 [9, cohort] (n=97: 40 obese children/adolescents vs. 57 controls, Hubei Province, China) found circulating MOTS-c significantly lower in obese males (465 ± 25 vs. 584 ± 21 ng/mL; p<0.001) but not in females. In the male cohort, MOTS-c negatively correlated with BMI-SDS, waist circumference, fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, and HbA1c. Sex-specific findings require replication.

Yoon et al. 2026 [10, cohort] (n=54: 22 lean vs. 32 obese adults; plus 10 obese patients followed 6 months post-bariatric surgery; Mayo Clinic / USC collaboration, Cohen P co-author) found circulating MOTS-c paradoxically **higher** in obese vs. lean individuals (273 ± 56 vs. 223 ± 50 pg/mL; p<0.01). BMI and HOMA-IR independently predicted elevated MOTS-c, with a biphasic relationship. After bariatric surgery with significant BMI reduction, MOTS-c remained unchanged (p=0.913). The post-bariatric null result is the most interpretively challenging finding in the human biomarker literature: two readings are consistent with it, and the data do not clearly favor one. A compensatory secretion model predicts elevated MOTS-c in obesity as a metabolic stress signal — but if it were truly compensatory, meaningful weight loss would be expected to reduce the secretory stimulus and bring levels down, which the bariatric data do not show. The bystander interpretation — that elevated MOTS-c in obesity tracks adipose or metabolic mass without playing an active compensatory role — is, if anything, more consistent with levels that are unresponsive to a major metabolic intervention like bariatric surgery. Neither reading can be dismissed; both should be held open. This directly contradicts a simple "biomarker of health" narrative and introduces genuine ambiguity about whether circulating MOTS-c reflects active mitochondrial signaling or passive co-variation with metabolic state.

Ozkaya et al. 2025 [11, cohort] (n=85: 48 obese, 37 normal-BMI adults, Turkey) found no significant difference in serum MOTS-c between obese and normal-weight groups (14.33 ± 3.76 vs. 13.67 ± 3.44 pg/mL; p=0.395). MOTS-c positively correlated with HOMA-IR (p<0.05) and decreased with age (~0.078 ng/mL per year). No significant association with high-sensitivity CRP or asymmetric dimethylarginine was detected.

#### Endothelial function

Qin et al. 2018 [12, cohort] (n=40 adults undergoing coronary angiography, divided by coronary blood flow response to intracoronary acetylcholine) found plasma MOTS-c lower in patients with endothelial dysfunction (p=0.007) and positively correlated with microvascular (p=0.01) and epicardial (p=0.02) coronary endothelial function. This is an observational association in n=40; mechanism and causality are unestablished.

#### Liver fat (NAFLD context)

Sequeira et al. 2021 [13, cohort] (n=125 Chinese adults with subset undergoing MRI for organ fat) found plasma MOTS-c positively associated with android fat distribution (p<0.004) and hepatic fat content — the opposite direction from a protective biomarker. The authors interpret this as stress-induced mitochondrial signaling from metabolically challenged hepatocytes. This is cross-sectional; directionality cannot be established.

### 3.3 Human intervention data (exogenous administration)

**None published.** A Phase 2a double-blind placebo-controlled trial (NCT07505745; Hudson Biotech / Peking University Shenzhen Hospital, China; subcutaneous MOTS-c, 12 weeks, n=120 adults with prediabetes and overweight/obesity [BMI 27–40 kg/m²], primary endpoint: OGTT-derived insulin sensitivity index; recruiting as of February 2026; estimated primary completion February 2027) is underway; no results are publicly available [14, regulatory]. Effect sizes for exogenous MOTS-c in humans remain unknown.

### 3.3.1 The Zhou 2024 meta-analysis: what a biphasic model means in practice

The biphasic model proposed by Zhou et al. 2024 [8, meta_analysis] deserves explicit unpacking because it carries a counterintuitive implication. If MOTS-c is higher in early/established obesity but lower in overt T2DM, then two opposite conclusions are both consistent with the aggregate cross-sectional data: (a) higher endogenous MOTS-c is compensatory and protective, which would predict that supplementing it exogenously in obesity could be beneficial; or (b) higher endogenous MOTS-c in obesity reflects a failed compensatory response, meaning further elevation via exogenous administration might be futile or even harmful. The meta-analysis cannot distinguish these narratives. The Yoon 2026 finding [10, cohort] that MOTS-c remained unchanged after bariatric surgery and significant weight loss is consistent with the "compensation" framing but also consistent with MOTS-c being a bystander marker of adipose mass rather than a metabolically active signal at the circulating concentrations detected. These competing interpretations are not resolvable from the available human observational data.

A further methodological concern runs across the entire human biomarker literature: MOTS-c immunoassay methods are not standardized. The absolute concentration values reported across studies vary by several orders of magnitude (from ~14 pg/mL in Ozkaya et al. 2025 [11, cohort] to ~465–584 ng/mL in Du 2018 [9, cohort] — a four-orders-of-magnitude spread). This is not biologically plausible and reflects different antibody specificities, calibrator matrices, and assay platforms. The directional findings (lower in T2DM, variable in obesity) are more robust than any specific concentration value, but even the directional findings must be read with the caveat that different assays may measure different forms or fragments of the circulating peptide. No head-to-head assay comparison has been published.

## 4. Exercise, Aging, and Longevity Evidence

### 4.1 Exercise-induced endogenous MOTS-c in humans

MOTS-c is measurably responsive to acute physical exercise in healthy humans. Reynolds et al. 2021 (n=10 healthy young men, mean age 24.5 ± 3.7 years) had participants perform acute stationary cycling and measured MOTS-c in skeletal muscle biopsies and plasma. Skeletal muscle MOTS-c increased approximately 11.9-fold immediately post-exercise; circulating plasma MOTS-c rose approximately 1.6-fold (from ~125 pg/mL to ~190 pg/mL). Both elevations were sustained at 4 hours post-exercise [3, animal]. Note: the tag reflects the primary study design (mouse treatment experiments); the human exercise sub-study was an n=10 observational component within the same paper.

A 16-week supervised aerobic and resistance exercise intervention (3 sessions/week, 65–80% max HR) in 49 overweight/obese breast cancer survivors found a significant increase in circulating MOTS-c among non-Hispanic White participants (p<0.01) with post-exercise MOTS-c correlating with reductions in fat mass, body weight, HOMA-IR, and CRP, but no significant change in Hispanic participants (p>0.05) [15, open_label]. This ethnic divergence is unexplained and unreplicated.

Whether chronic endurance training shifts resting MOTS-c is unsettled: one cross-sectional study (75 professional athletes vs. 30 sedentary controls) reported lower resting-state levels in high-endurance athletes [16, cohort] — but this is from a non-whitelisted publisher (IMR Press, tier-reduced) and other reports describe the opposite; the chronic-adaptation direction cannot be stated with confidence. The acute-exercise elevation is the better-established finding; the acute-vs-chronic distinction matters for clinical interpretation.

### 4.2 Physical capacity and aging: mouse treatment studies

The most cited exogenous MOTS-c treatment data comes from Reynolds et al. 2021, conducted in C57BL/6N male mice at three ages: young (2 months), middle-aged (12 months), and old (22 months). MOTS-c was administered at 15 mg/kg/day IP for 2 weeks. Treated mice across all age groups showed significantly improved treadmill running performance. In aged mice (22 months), MOTS-c treatment approximately doubled total running work output, and treated old mice outperformed untreated middle-aged mice on running capacity. Grip strength and gait metrics also improved in the old-treated group [3, animal]. A late-life regimen initiated at 23.5 months (3x/week, intermittent) increased physical capacity and improved healthspan markers in treated aged mice versus vehicle controls. No lifespan extension (survival curve to natural death) was reported [3, animal].

**Population note:** all exogenous MOTS-c treatment data showing physical performance benefits are from rodents (C57BL/6N male mice). No human exogenous MOTS-c administration study for exercise or physical performance has been published.

### 4.3 Aging, mitochondrial decline, and MOTS-c

Endogenous MOTS-c declines with age in both rodents and humans. Reynolds et al. 2021 document that skeletal muscle MOTS-c is reduced in aged (22-month) vs. young (2-month) mice, paralleling the age-dependent reduction in physical capacity [3, animal]. Burtscher et al. 2023 (Aging Cell) reviewed mitokine biology across aging, noting that multiple mitochondrial stress signals — including MOTS-c — show age-dependent dysregulation and that the exercise–mitokine axis may represent a mechanistic link between physical activity and healthspan; no direct intervention data in humans [17, mechanism_review].

### 4.4 The longevity-genetics claim: refuted

As detailed in §1.5, the Fuku 2015 hypothesis [4, mechanism_review] that m.1382A>C contributes to exceptional longevity is not supported by the Zempo 2021 large-cohort refutation (n=27,527; n=736 centenarians) [5, cohort]. MOTS-c's genetic contribution to exceptional human longevity is unestablished.

### 4.5 The "exercise mimetic" framing: evidence vs. marketing

The label "exercise mimetic" applied to MOTS-c is a consumer/media extrapolation from two distinct bodies of evidence that must not be conflated:

1. **Mouse treatment data:** Exogenous MOTS-c (injected, 15 mg/kg/day) improves running capacity, grip strength, and AMPK-mediated metabolism in aged mice. This is the only controlled intervention evidence that MOTS-c improves physical performance [3, animal].

2. **Human biomarker data:** Endogenous MOTS-c rises in human skeletal muscle and plasma after acute exercise. This makes MOTS-c a marker that tracks exercise — not an agent that substitutes for it [3, animal].

The logical gap between these two facts is large: that a peptide rises during exercise does not establish that administering it externally replicates the exercise stimulus. No human RCT or open-label trial has administered exogenous MOTS-c and measured physical performance or healthspan outcomes. Claims that MOTS-c "acts like exercise" or "gives the benefits of exercise without working out" in humans are not supported by peer-reviewed data and constitute marketing overreach.

**Translational gap (explicit):** As of mid-2026, no published study has administered exogenous MOTS-c to humans and measured exercise capacity, muscle function, healthspan, or longevity outcomes. The exercise/longevity benefit profile is established only in rodents receiving pharmacological IP doses (15 mg/kg/day).

## 5. Safety, Dosing, Route, PK, Regulatory Status, and Monitoring

### 5.1 Human dosing — no established dose

There is **no established or approved human dose** for exogenous native MOTS-c. MOTS-c has not completed a dedicated safety/dose-finding trial in humans as a standalone native peptide. Grey-market vendors circulate doses of 5–15 mg administered subcutaneously once daily, with purported cycling protocols (e.g., 5-on/2-off); these figures originate exclusively from vendor labeling and community aggregation [31, vendor_label][32, anecdote_aggregate] and are not grounded in human pharmacology data.

The only human clinical pharmacology data available is for CB4211, an engineered analog of MOTS-c (see §6.1).

### 5.2 Route of administration and pharmacokinetics

**Route:** All preclinical MOTS-c studies used intraperitoneal (IP) or subcutaneous (SC) injection in rodent models [1, animal]. The native peptide is not orally bioavailable; proteolytic degradation in the GI tract is expected for a 16-amino-acid peptide.

**Pharmacokinetics:** No formal human PK study of exogenous native MOTS-c has been published. The native peptide is understood to have a short circulating half-life and rapid clearance [33, anecdote_aggregate] — a qualitative inference consistent with the rapid post-exercise normalization of endogenous MOTS-c and explicitly cited by CohBar as the engineering rationale for the stabilized CB4211 analog [34, open_label]. No specific human half-life value is supportable from a primary source.

**Preclinical dosing range:** Mouse studies used IP doses of 0.5 mg/kg/day (foundational 2015 work) up to 5–15 mg/kg/day in later exercise-metabolism studies. These cannot be converted to human equivalent doses without formal allometric scaling and safety bridging studies, neither of which has been published for MOTS-c.

### 5.3 Contraindications and theoretical risks

**Hypoglycemia:** MOTS-c activates AMPK and promotes GLUT4 translocation to muscle cell membranes, increasing insulin-independent glucose uptake. In CB4211 Phase 1b, CohBar reported significant decreases in glucose as an exploratory endpoint (company press release [25, open_label]). Co-administration with insulin, sulfonylureas, metformin, or GLP-1 agonists creates an additive glucose-lowering risk (metformin and GLP-1 agonists share the AMPK/metabolic-modulator pathway); hypoglycemia is a plausible pharmacodynamic interaction without a characterized safe threshold.

**Unknown long-term effects:** No chronic exposure data in humans exists. MOTS-c influences mitochondrial biogenesis, systemic metabolism, and potentially immune and inflammatory pathways [1, animal]. Long-term consequences of sustained supraphysiologic AMPK activation are uncharacterized in humans.

**Metabolic-modulator class concerns:** AMPK activators can affect hepatic glucose output, lipid oxidation, and potentially suppress mTOR signaling. The clinical significance of these effects with exogenous MOTS-c dosing is unknown.

**Folate pathway:** MOTS-c's primary mechanism is depletion of 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (5Me-THF); sustained administration carries a theoretical risk of folate depletion. Folate/homocysteine monitoring is warranted with extended use.

**Population-specific cautions:**
- **Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM)** and individuals using insulin pumps: heightened hypoglycemia risk distinct from — and additive with — basal insulin; monitor with particular care.
- Insulin-treated type 2 diabetes or individuals on sulfonylureas, metformin, or GLP-1 agonists: additive glucose-lowering risk.
- Pregnancy/lactation: no data; theoretical risk given metabolic and mitogenic signaling.

**Injection site reactions** are the best-documented adverse effect from human data (CB4211 Phase 1). Whether native MOTS-c carries the same formulation-dependent nodule risk is unknown.

### 5.4 Regulatory status

#### FDA (United States)

MOTS-c is **not FDA-approved** for any indication and is not legally marketed for human therapeutic use in the United States.

**Compounding (503A/503B):** MOTS-c is not on the FDA 503A Category 1 (eligible for compounding) list. Federal Register notice FR 2026-07361 (published April 16, 2026, 91 Fed. Reg. 20465) announces a forthcoming PCAC (Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee) public meeting and explicitly names "MOTS-C (free base)/MOTS-C acetate" as a bulk drug substance nominated for inclusion on the Section 503A Bulk Drug Substances List, scheduled for PCAC evaluation on July 23, 2026 [27, regulatory]. Vendor-authored sources characterize this as a "Category 2 removal effective April 22, 2026," but cite no Federal Register document for that specific removal action and are not used as the regulatory ground here. Even a favorable PCAC recommendation requires subsequent FDA rulemaking before licensed 503A pharmacies may legally compound MOTS-c with a prescription. As of June 2026, **legal compounding of MOTS-c is not authorized**.

#### WADA (Anti-doping)

MOTS-c is **prohibited** under the 2026 WADA Prohibited List, **Section S4 (Metabolic Modulators)** as an AMPK activator (the AICAR/AMPK-activator class; verify the exact subsection against the current WADA Prohibited List) [28, regulatory]. Prohibition applies **in- and out-of-competition** (S4 non-specified substance). S4 non-Specified Substances carry no reduction in sanction period for contamination defenses. Athletes subject to WADA-compliant testing must consider MOTS-c prohibited regardless of prescription status or therapeutic intent.

Three practical notes on the WADA prohibition warrant explicit statement:

First, the prohibition is based on mechanism (AMPK activation), not on demonstrated performance-enhancing effect in humans. WADA prohibits substances with the potential to enhance performance — even if that potential has not been proven in clinical trials. MOTS-c's classification under Section S4 (Metabolic Modulators, AMPK-activator class) is rationally grounded (AMPK activation in skeletal muscle is a pathway relevant to endurance and metabolic performance) but does not constitute an admission by WADA that MOTS-c has been proven to work in humans.

Second, the in-competition/out-of-competition scope of the prohibition (it applies both in and out of competition, unlike some other S4 substances) means athletes cannot use MOTS-c in a "loading" or "off-season" cycle and expect clearance before competition. Half-life data for the native peptide in humans does not exist, so clearance timelines are unknown.

Third, contamination of grey-market peptides is a documented risk in the broader research-chemical peptide space [35, anecdote_aggregate]. A positive test resulting from a contaminated product sold as a different compound would still fall under the non-Specified substance framework with no contamination-defense reduction available — a significant legal risk for any athlete using grey-market peptides of any kind.

### 5.5 Monitoring parameters

A clinician supervising any investigational or off-label MOTS-c use would rationally monitor the following given the known mechanistic profile:

**Glucose metabolism:**
- Fasting plasma glucose (pre-treatment baseline and at intervals)
- HbA1c (baseline; at 8–12 weeks of sustained use)
- Post-prandial glucose or OGTT-derived insulin sensitivity index (as used in NCT07505745 [14, regulatory])
- Signs/symptoms of hypoglycemia, especially if co-administered with glucose-lowering agents

**Liver enzymes:**
- ALT, AST: monitor for **increases** above baseline — an elevation ≥3× ULN is the standard hepatotoxicity stopping rule. Note: CB4211 Phase 1b in NASH subjects showed ALT/AST *reductions* (exploratory, company-reported); a *fall* from a NASH-elevated baseline is not a safety signal and should not trigger a false stop. The concern in non-NASH users is an unexplained *rise*.

**Folate / homocysteine:**
- Baseline plasma folate and homocysteine; recheck at 8–12 weeks with sustained use (MOTS-c mechanism depletes 5Me-THF; folate supplementation may modulate AICAR accumulation — uncharacterized in vivo).

**Injection site:**
- Palpation for subcutaneous nodules at each visit (informed by CB4211 2018 suspension experience)

**Body weight and metabolic panel:**
- Weight, lipids (given AMPK effects on fat oxidation)

**Stopping criteria (theoretical, not from completed trial data):**
- Confirmed hypoglycemic episode (blood glucose <70 mg/dL with symptoms)
- ALT/AST >3× upper limit of normal from baseline
- Persistent painful or expanding injection site nodules
- Systemic allergic reaction

## 6. CB4211, CohBar, and the Commercialization That Failed

### 6.1 Native MOTS-c vs CB4211: the molecules are not the same

CB4211 is **not** the native 16-amino-acid MOTS-c peptide. CohBar describes it as "a novel and improved analog of MOTS-c, a naturally occurring mitochondrial derived peptide (MDP)" (U.S. Patent 11,111,271) [24, regulatory]. CB4211 is a stabilized, chemically modified analog designed to overcome the native peptide's limitations of rapid clearance and poor formulation stability [34, open_label]. The chemical modifications are proprietary and covered by the patent. Grey-market vendors sell synthetic replicates of the native 16-amino-acid sequence — the unmodified peptide. Phase 1 human data for CB4211 does not translate to the native peptide; stability, bioavailability, and receptor engagement may differ. **No human efficacy trial of native MOTS-c has been completed or published** (the one active trial, NCT07505745, is recruiting in 2026 with no results; estimated primary completion February 2027).

### 6.2 CB4211 Phase 1a/1b trial: design, findings, termination

CB4211 is the **only MOTS-c-class compound to enter human clinical trials** (NCT03998514, CohBar Inc.) [26, open_label].

**Trial design:**
- Phase 1a: Double-blind, placebo-controlled, single and multiple ascending dose in 65 healthy adults over one week.
- Phase 1b: Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; 25 mg SC once daily × 4 weeks in 20 obese adults with NAFLD (minimum 10% liver fat); 11 CB4211, 9 placebo.

**Safety and tolerability (2021 topline):** No serious adverse events were reported. Primary endpoints of safety and tolerability were met. The only adverse events occurring in >10% of CB4211 recipients during Phase 1b were transient, generally mild-to-moderate injection site reactions [25, open_label]. No peer-reviewed publication of Phase 1a/1b results exists; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03998514 shows the trial as completed with no results posted.

**Earlier suspension (2018):** The Phase 1 trial was temporarily suspended in November 2018 due to persistent subcutaneous nodules at injection sites, characterized by CohBar's CSO as non-tender, non-erythematous, non-purulent, freely mobile accumulations of undissolved drug [29, open_label]. No evidence of local tissue harm was reported; the suspension was used to refine injection technique.

**Exploratory efficacy signals (company press release only):** CohBar reported exploratory secondary signals: ALT −21% vs −4% placebo; AST −28% vs −11% placebo; fasting glucose −6% vs 0% placebo; body weight showed a numerical trend without statistical significance [25, open_label]. These figures are company-reported with no peer review and no posted registry results.

**Formulation failure:** Following Phase 1b, CohBar stated that "the formulation of CB4211 used in the Phase 1b stage of the trial is not suitable for further development" and that "efforts to develop an improved formulation have not been successful to date" [30, open_label].

**Bottom line for wiki readers:** CB4211's Phase 1 established short-term tolerability at one dose level in a small, controlled population — per company press releases only (no peer-reviewed publication). It does NOT constitute a safety profile for the native MOTS-c peptide. Phase 2 efficacy and safety data do not exist.

### 6.3 CohBar's fate: dissolution 2023

Following the Phase 1a/1b results, CohBar announced a proposed reverse merger with Morphogenesis Inc. (May 2023) to pivot toward an immuno-oncology pipeline and rename the combined entity TuHURA Biosciences [24, regulatory]. On October 30–31, 2023, Nasdaq issued guidance that the proposed transaction structure would not receive listing approval; CohBar's board determined no viable path forward existed and resolved to commence liquidation and dissolution [24, regulatory]. The CB4211 program has no active corporate sponsor. No Phase 2 trial has been initiated. The only clinical program for any MOTS-c-class molecule has ended in program dissolution.

### 6.3.1 What CB4211's failure tells us about the native peptide's translational prospects

CB4211's Phase 1b formulation failure is instructive about the native peptide's development path, though the lessons cut both ways. On one hand, CohBar's inability to create a stable formulation for an engineered analog suggests the native peptide — which has additional stability and solubility challenges relative to CB4211 — faces even more formidable formulation hurdles. If CohBar, with full access to the original intellectual property and a funded development team, could not make a manufacturable MOTS-c-analog formulation after several years of effort, the probability that a grey-market vendor has solved the same problem with the native unmodified peptide is low. Lyophilized peptide vials containing an authentic, potent, sterile, endotoxin-free preparation of native MOTS-c are theoretically producible by contract manufacturers, but without FDA manufacturing oversight, any specific product's identity and potency is unverifiable.

On the other hand, the formulation failure of CB4211 does not tell us the native peptide would have failed efficacy endpoints had a stable formulation been achieved. The Phase 1b exploratory signals (ALT reduction, glucose reduction) are intriguing but statistically unreported and company-sourced — they represent neither confirmation nor refutation of the hypothesis. CohBar's dissolution was a business event driven by Nasdaq listing issues and merger failure, not a scientific event triggered by negative efficacy findings. This distinction matters for interpreting the commercial failure: the program did not fail because MOTS-c was shown to be ineffective; it failed because the company ran out of viable strategic options before Phase 2 could test efficacy.

### 6.4 Consumer access and sourcing realism

Consumer MOTS-c is available only through research-chemical / grey-market channels [35, anecdote_aggregate]. There is no FDA-approved product and no approved 503A bulk-substance compounding pathway — the FDA PCAC was scheduled to evaluate MOTS-c for potential 503A inclusion at its July 2026 meeting, with no approval granted as of this writing [27, regulatory].

Grey-market vendors sell MOTS-c labeled "for research use only / not for human consumption," exempting them from pharmaceutical-grade manufacturing standards, sterility testing, identity verification, and potency certification. Injectable research-chemical peptides carry documented risks: bacterial endotoxins (fever, systemic inflammation), injection-site infections, unknown impurity profiles, and no established human dosing [35, anecdote_aggregate]. No specific contamination prevalence figure is supportable from a primary source.

Additionally, the gene–peptide confusion is prevalent in consumer discourse: MOTS-c is encoded by the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene, not a nuclear gene — a distinction that vendors frequently obscure.

## 7. What a Legitimate Use Case Would Require

Given the current evidence state, a meaningful claim that exogenous MOTS-c benefits human health would require at minimum:

**Mandatory, not optional:**
1. A completed RCT of native MOTS-c in humans showing a pre-specified primary endpoint met.
2. A pharmacokinetic characterization of native MOTS-c by the intended clinical route (subcutaneous or intramuscular) — including half-life, Tmax, Cmax at the proposed dose, and linearity across the dose range.
3. A dose-finding study establishing the minimum pharmacologically active dose in humans, because the mouse doses (0.5–15 mg/kg/day IP) cannot be directly converted to human SC equivalents without allometric scaling and route-adjustment factors that are currently absent from the literature.
4. An independent replication of at least one key efficacy finding (insulin sensitization; physical capacity; body weight) in any species other than C57BL/6 or CD-1 mice from any lab other than Cohen/Lee USC.

**None of these exist as of mid-2026.** NCT07505745 (Hudson Biotech / Peking University Shenzhen Hospital) is the first step toward item 1; results are not yet available (estimated primary completion February 2027). If it completes and reports a primary-endpoint positive result, that would be the first human therapeutic signal for native MOTS-c. Even then, items 2–4 would remain open.

This does not mean exogenous MOTS-c is biologically inert in humans — it means the dose, route, timing, and expected magnitude of any human effect are entirely unknown. The person who uses grey-market MOTS-c today is not taking an evidence-based risk; they are conducting an uncontrolled, unmonitored, unblinded n=1 experiment with no positive-control group, no validated outcome measure, no established dose, and a peptide whose characterization in their species has never been published.

## 8. Independent Animal Research (2023–2025)

Three independent animal studies have extended the Cohen/Lee mechanistic scaffold without independently establishing new foundational claims:

Tang et al. 2023 [19, animal] (Chengdu Sport University, China; Scientific Reports) examined the role of MOTS-c-mediated antioxidant defense in aerobic exercise alleviating diabetic myocardial injury in a mouse model, finding that MOTS-c improved antioxidant capacity and reduced myocardial damage markers.

Yang et al. 2024 [20, animal] (Xuzhou Medical University, China; Acta Biochim Biophys Sin) investigated MOTS-c for treating cancer-induced bone pain via AMPK-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis in a rodent pain model.

Pham et al. 2025 [21, animal] (University of Auckland, New Zealand; Frontiers in Physiology) showed that MOTS-c restores mitochondrial respiration in a type 2 diabetic heart model. This represents the first independent animal replication from a Western institution outside the Cohen/Lee network, though still following the established AMPK-mitochondrial mechanistic framework.

Review articles from 2023 onward (Gao et al. Metabolites 2023 [22, mechanism_review]; Wan et al. Journal of Translational Medicine 2023 [23, mechanism_review]) synthesize the Cohen/Lee corpus rather than extending it with novel primary data.

The pattern visible in the 2023–2025 independent animal literature is instructive: each paper applies MOTS-c to a new mouse disease model (diabetic cardiomyopathy, cancer pain, cardiac mitochondrial dysfunction) and finds AMPK-mediated benefit. This is consistent with a broad AMPK-activating effect being pharmacologically real in rodents. However, the selection of new disease models by independent groups without an established human indication tracks the Cohen/Lee papers' own exploratory trajectory. What is absent is a disciplined dose-replication study: no independent group has tested the specific Lee et al. 2015 dose (5 mg/kg/day IP for 7 days in diet-induced obese C57BL/6 mice) and independently reported the euglycemic clamp result. Until that particular experiment is externally replicated, the core insulin-sensitization claim rests on a single-lab, unduplicated dataset, however plausible the mechanism. This is not an allegation of misconduct; it is the ordinary standard of scientific evidence that is missing.

## 9. Bottom Line

MOTS-c is a biologically plausible and mechanistically interesting mitochondrial-derived peptide. The scientific case that it could improve metabolic health and physical capacity is coherent at the level of mechanism: AMPK activation in skeletal muscle is a known lever for glucose disposal and mitochondrial biogenesis, and the mouse data showing improved insulin sensitivity and running performance in aged animals is internally consistent within the Cohen/Lee research program. Against that coherence, a clear-eyed accounting requires the following:

1. **No human therapeutic evidence exists for the native peptide.** Every efficacy number in this entry comes from male mice receiving pharmacological IP doses. The 100% single-lab concentration in foundational papers (Cohen/Lee USC) and disclosed financial conflicts with CohBar mean independent corroboration of the core claims in any species is absent through 2021.

2. **The only human clinical program used a different molecule and failed.** CB4211 (the analog) reached Phase 1b, met safety endpoints, showed exploratory signals in an n=11 cohort via company press release only, then failed on formulation and was discontinued when CohBar dissolved in 2023. There is no Phase 2 efficacy evidence for anything in the MOTS-c class in humans.

3. **Human biomarker data is real but not therapeutic.** Multiple independent cohort studies confirm that endogenous MOTS-c circulates, correlates with metabolic states, and responds to exercise. These associations tell us MOTS-c is biologically active in humans; they say nothing about whether exogenous administration would recapitulate mouse-model effects.

4. **The regulatory and practical environment is unfavorable.** WADA-prohibited under Section S4 (Metabolic Modulators), not FDA-approved, not currently compoundable, available only as grey-market research-chemical with no purity or identity guarantees.

5. **The longevity-genetics angle has been refuted at scale.** The Fuku 2015 longevity hypothesis (n~96 centenarians) does not hold in the Zempo 2021 analysis (n=27,527; n=736 centenarians).

This is an entry worth watching as NCT07505745 results emerge (Phase 2a, Hudson Biotech / Peking University Shenzhen Hospital; estimated primary completion February 2027). Until a human efficacy trial with native MOTS-c publishes results, the evidence for using exogenous MOTS-c in any human context remains entirely preclinical, from a single lab with commercial conflicts, in a compound class that failed its one serious commercialization attempt.

**What a thoughtful clinician or researcher would take from this entry:**
- The mechanism is real and interesting; AICAR-mediated AMPK activation in skeletal muscle is pharmacologically rational.
- The mouse data is consistent and repeated, but all from one lab, one species (primarily C57BL/6 males), one route (IP), and at pharmacological doses with no established human equivalent.
- The observational human biomarker literature confirms MOTS-c is biologically active in humans and responds to exercise and metabolic state, but does not establish what happens when you administer it exogenously.
- The only human experiment in the class (CB4211 analog, Phase 1b, n=11, company press release only) generated directionally interesting but statistically unreported exploratory signals, then failed on formulation, and the company dissolved.
- The regulatory, safety, and quality-assurance environment for grey-market MOTS-c is materially worse than for any approved or compounded pharmaceutical.
- None of these facts preclude MOTS-c from being an effective human metabolic therapeutic; they just mean we do not know yet whether it is, and current users are absorbing risks whose magnitude has not been characterized.
