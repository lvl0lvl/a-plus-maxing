# Humanin (Mitochondrial-Derived Peptide, MT-RNR2)

## Metadata

- **class:** peptide (mitochondrial-derived peptide, MDP)
- **evidence_tier:** C — All human evidence is observational biomarker: circulating humanin declines with age and associates inversely with cognitive aging, coronary endothelial dysfunction, and impaired fasting glucose in cross-sectional and cohort studies. No human administration trial of humanin or any humanin analog has been published or completed. The primary therapeutic/efficacy evidence base is preclinical, derived almost entirely from the S14G-HNG analog (~1,000-fold more potent than native humanin) in rodent and porcine models. Longevity associations are associative and non-causal. A real pro-tumor counter-signal exists from two independent animal models.
- **risk_tier:** experimental — FDA-unapproved for any indication; no 503A compounding pathway (not on Category 1 or Category 2 active lists; not among the July 2026 PCAC nominees); WADA-prohibited in- and out-of-competition (S0 — Non-Approved Substances); human PK entirely uncharacterized; anti-apoptotic mechanism is pro-tumor in documented preclinical cancer models; grey-market product only.
- **status:** researching
- **last_verified:** 2026-06-20

## Summary

Humanin (HN) is a 24-amino-acid mitochondrial-derived peptide (sequence: MAPRGFSCLLLLTSEIDLPVKRRA) and the **first MDP ever discovered** — preceding MOTS-c, SHLP2, and all other members of the mitochondrial peptide class. It is encoded by a short open reading frame (ORF) within **MT-RNR2** (the mitochondrial 16S rRNA gene), identified in 2001 by Hashimoto, Niikura, and Nishimoto at Keio University in a functional screen of surviving neurons from an Alzheimer's patient's occipital cortex [2]. Humanin's founding characterization was neuroprotective: synthetic humanin blocked neuronal death caused by familial Alzheimer's disease (FAD) gene products and Aβ amyloid in cell culture [2, 3].

**The honest evidence state, stated once and up front:**

Humanin's human evidence is **entirely observational biomarker data**. Multiple cohort studies document that endogenous circulating humanin declines with age, associates with preserved coronary endothelial function, tracks cognitive aging in large population samples (Health and Retirement Study, n=15,620), and is reduced in impaired fasting glucose and cardiovascular risk states. These are associations of the endogenous peptide as a biomarker — not interventional data. **No human administration trial of exogenous humanin or any humanin analog has been published, completed, or registered in ClinicalTrials.gov as of mid-2026.**

The therapeutic/efficacy evidence is preclinical and carries a critical caveat: the overwhelming majority of in-vivo animal work used **S14G-humanin (HNG)** — a synthetic analog with a serine-to-glycine substitution at position 14 that is approximately **1,000-fold more potent** than native humanin in cell assays — rather than the unmodified 24-mer. Claims about humanin's neuroprotective, cardioprotective, metabolic, and cytoprotective effects in animals are almost always claims about HNG pharmacology, not endogenous humanin biology.

The longevity connection is **associative, not causal**, and the direction of causality in the biomarker associations is unresolved: elevated humanin may reflect a compensatory stress-response to mitochondrial dysfunction (disease → ↑humanin, reverse causation) rather than humanin being the protective agent — a possibility the hemodialysis U-shape finding (§5.4) actively supports. Centenarian offspring show higher circulating humanin than age-matched controls [20]; long-lived mouse models (Ames dwarf, liver IGF-1 deletion) display elevated humanin while GH-transgenic short-lived mice show markedly reduced humanin [14]; naked mole rats — a negligible-senescence species — do not show the age-related humanin decline seen in shorter-lived rodents [20]. These observations are consistent with humanin being a correlate of favorable longevity biology, but no pharmacological intervention has extended lifespan by elevating humanin in a mammalian model.

**The single most load-bearing safety counter-signal:** Humanin is a potent anti-apoptotic peptide. The same mechanism that protects neurons from FAD-gene-induced death — sequestering pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and tBid, blocking cytochrome c release, suppressing caspase activation — is the mechanism that protects cancer cells from programmed death. Two published preclinical studies document this directly: (a) exogenous humanin promoted tumor growth, spontaneous lung metastasis, and chemotherapy resistance in a syngeneic triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) model [28]; (b) humanin activated the integrin αV–TGFβ signaling axis in glioblastoma (GBM) cells, enhancing invasion, angiogenesis, and shortening survival in orthotopic xenograft animals [29]. Active malignancy and personal history of cancer are precautionary contraindications pending any human safety data.

**Research concentration:** The humanin literature is older and broader than MOTS-c (2001 vs. 2015 discovery). The two historically dominant research nexuses — the Nishimoto/Hashimoto group at Keio University (~30–35% of primary papers, 2001–2011) and the Cohen/Yen/Lee/Kim group at USC Leonard Davis (~25–30%) — together account for approximately **55–65% of identifiable primary efficacy and mechanism papers**. This is below the ≥70% single-lab flag that applied to MOTS-c; the humanin literature has genuine independent breadth, with replication from Muzumdar/Gong at Pittsburgh/LSU (cardiovascular), independent Chinese groups, and European observational programs. The caveat is that the HNG analog tool itself originates from the USC/Keio nexus, even where the applying labs are independent.

**Regulatory status:** FDA-unapproved; no 503A compounding pathway; WADA-prohibited S0 (no governmental regulatory approval anywhere for human therapeutic use). Commercially: CohBar Inc.'s MDP clinical programs prioritized MOTS-c analogs (CB4209/CB5945), not humanin — no humanin-specific IND has been identified. CohBar has since dissolved.

## 1. Identity, Discovery, and Molecular Mechanism

### 1.1 Identity and molecular structure

Humanin is a 24-amino-acid peptide with the sequence **MAPRGFSCLLLLTSEIDLPVKRRA**, encoded by a short ORF within **MT-RNR2** — the mitochondrial gene that produces the 16S ribosomal RNA subunit. It is the founding member of the mitochondrial-derived peptide (MDP) class, predating MOTS-c (MT-RNR1, 2015) and the small humanin-like peptides (SHLPs 1–6) by over a decade [1].

**Cytoplasmic versus mitochondrial translation.** The humanin ORF is transcribed from mtDNA but the peptide can also be produced via cytoplasmic ribosomes. The founding cDNA was isolated from a human brain library (occipital lobe of an Alzheimer's patient), making its cellular origin initially ambiguous; subsequent work confirmed both mitochondrial and cytosolic ribosomes can translate the humanin ORF [6]. Nuclear-encoded paralogs (MTRNR2L1–13) share 92–95% amino-acid identity with mitochondrial humanin and add a potential confound in expression studies: transcripts attributed to MT-RNR2 can include nuclear-derived copies.

### 1.2 Discovery

Humanin was identified in 2001 by Hashimoto, Niikura, and Nishimoto using a functional "death-trap" screen of a cDNA library from surviving neurons in the occipital lobe of an Alzheimer's disease patient [2]. The screen sought factors blocking neuronal death caused by FAD gene products (V642I-APP, N141I-presenilin-2) and Aβ amyloid. The isolated cDNA encoded a 24-aa polypeptide with 99% nucleotide identity to the 16S rRNA region of mtDNA, establishing the MT-RNR2 origin. The founding paper reported that synthetic humanin (1 nM–10 µM) blocked death of rat hippocampal neurons and PC12 cells caused by multiple FAD gene products and Aβ₁₋₄₃ [2, in_vitro]. A companion J. Neurosci. paper from the same group provided dose-response characterization and introduced the S14G analog [3, in_vitro].

The founding citation is: Hashimoto Y, Niikura T, Tajima H et al. "A rescue factor abolishing neuronal cell death by a wide spectrum of familial Alzheimer's disease genes and Aβ." *Proc Natl Acad Sci USA*. 2001;98(11):6336–41. PMID: 11371646.

### 1.3 Molecular mechanism: anti-apoptotic and cytoprotective pathways

Humanin exerts cytoprotection through two parallel arms: (i) intracellular direct sequestration of pro-apoptotic proteins and (ii) extracellular receptor-mediated signaling cascades.

#### 1.3.1 Intracellular arm: Bax and tBid sequestration

In a 2003 *Nature* study using human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells and a cell-free Bax activation assay, Guo et al. showed that humanin directly binds Bax and prevents its translocation from cytosol to the outer mitochondrial membrane [4, in_vitro]. The mechanistic logic is precise: Bax must undergo a conformational change and migrate to the outer mitochondrial membrane before it can trigger cytochrome c release; humanin binds Bax in the cytoplasm — before that activation step — and holds it in an inactive soluble state. Small interfering RNA (siRNA) knockdown of endogenous humanin-like sequences sensitized HEK293 cells to Bax-induced death, providing gain- and loss-of-function evidence that endogenous humanin contributes to basal apoptotic resistance. Humanin also binds **truncated Bid (tBid)**, a BH3-only activator of Bax generated by caspase-8 cleavage, further blocking the intrinsic apoptosis pathway at an upstream node [4, in_vitro]. Both interactions are direct protein–protein binding events characterized by co-immunoprecipitation and pull-down assays, established at the in-vitro and cell level only — no human in-vivo data exist for this arm, and the concentrations of humanin required to occupy Bax binding sites in intact cells relative to circulating endogenous plasma levels have not been established.

#### 1.3.2 IGFBP-3 binding

Ikonen et al. (2003) identified **insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 3 (IGFBP-3)** as a high-affinity humanin binding partner in a yeast two-hybrid screen, confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation in mouse testicular tissue — the first in-vivo biological confirmation of the interaction [5, animal]. The structural specificity is notable: the interaction is mediated by the C-terminal heparin/GAG-binding domain of IGFBP-3, a region separate from IGF-1 binding. Functionally, IGFBP-3 can translocate to the nucleus and induce apoptosis independently of IGF-1; by binding IGFBP-3's C-terminal domain, humanin blocks IGFBP-3's ability to recruit importin-β and complete nuclear import, thereby preventing the nuclear apoptotic program. This establishes an anti-apoptotic axis entirely distinct from the direct Bax pathway. The physiological significance in humans is unknown. There is an important PK corollary: IGFBP-3 binding accelerates humanin clearance in rodents, because the IGFBP-3–humanin complex is eliminated faster than free humanin. Analog design exploited this: HNGF6A, which carries the additional Phe6→Ala substitution, has reduced IGFBP-3 affinity and therefore extended circulating half-life compared to HNG — a point that has direct implications for which analog is more relevant to systemic dosing (discussed further in Section 7).

#### 1.3.3 Extracellular receptor arm 1: FPR2 (FPRL1)

The G protein-coupled receptor **FPR2** (formyl peptide receptor-like 1, also called FPRL1) was identified as a functional extracellular receptor for humanin by Ying et al. (2004) in the *Journal of Immunology*, using PC12 neuronal cells and transfected HEK293 cells [6, in_vitro]. Humanin binding to FPR2 activates ERK1/2 via Gᵢ-coupled signaling and contributes to anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects. FPR2 is expressed on neurons, monocytes, and neutrophils, which partially explains humanin's documented effects on inflammatory resolution in addition to neuronal survival.

The structural basis of FPR2–humanin recognition has since been characterized at atomic resolution by cryo-EM (Zhu et al. 2022, *Nature Communications*), revealing a **polar binding cavity** within the receptor's helical bundle that accommodates humanin's charged residues and a **hydrophobic binding groove** in the extracellular region that engages the peptide's hydrophobic core [9, in_vitro]. This structural detail confirms that humanin engages FPR2 through a defined binding mode rather than nonspecific surface association — a finding relevant to analog design and to understanding why the S14G substitution at position 14 might alter FPR2 affinity and not just intracellular Bax binding. Importantly, FPR2 is also the receptor through which Aβ42 exerts neurotoxic effects; the 2022 cryo-EM paper explicitly characterized humanin as competing for Aβ42 binding at FPR2, suggesting a receptor-level antagonism that complements the intracellular Bax-sequestration mechanism.

#### 1.3.4 Extracellular receptor arm 2: trimeric CNTFR/WSX-1/gp130 → STAT3

Hashimoto et al. (2009) demonstrated that humanin inhibits neuronal apoptosis by binding a **trimeric cytokine receptor complex** comprising **CNTF receptor α (CNTFRα)**, **WSX-1 (IL-27Rα)**, and **gp130 (IL6ST)** [7, in_vitro]. The experimental system was rat primary cortical neurons and GP130/WSX-1/CNTFRα-overexpressing COS-7 and neuronal cells. Overexpression of CNTFRα and/or WSX-1 enhanced humanin binding and potentiated neuronal protection, while GP130 was required for downstream signal transduction — loss of GP130 abolished the anti-apoptotic effect. This trimeric complex is structurally analogous to the IL-6 receptor complex; humanin's use of it positions the peptide functionally as a cytokine-like ligand operating at the cell surface in addition to its intracellular Bax-sequestration role. The trimeric complex engagement activates **JAK2 → STAT3** phosphorylation, leading to transcriptional upregulation of cytoprotective genes. **AKT** and **ERK1/2** are also phosphorylated downstream. One study in aged mice (Kim et al. 2016, *Oncotarget*) compared these signaling responses in hippocampal tissue from 3-month vs. 18-month C57BL/6 males and suggested qualitatively that ERK1/2/AKT/STAT3 activation magnitude may differ by age [8, anecdote_aggregate]. This is a non-whitelisted journal with no whitelisted replication identified; the finding grounds only a directional observation that age may modulate humanin receptor sensitivity, not a numerical or mechanistic claim. It has not been validated in human tissue.

**Mechanism summary — evidence tier by level:**

| Mechanism | Best evidence | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Bax sequestration (cytoplasmic) | Cell-free + HEK293 knockdown [4] | In-vitro/cell |
| tBid sequestration | Co-IP in cell models [4] | In-vitro/cell |
| IGFBP-3 binding (anti-apoptotic) | Yeast 2-hybrid + mouse testis co-IP [5] | In-vitro + animal |
| FPR2/FPRL1 signaling (ERK) | PC12 + HEK293 transfection; cryo-EM [6, 9] | In-vitro/cell |
| CNTFR/WSX-1/gp130 → STAT3 | Rodent primary neurons + COS-7 [7] | In-vitro/cell |
| ERK/AKT/STAT3 age-dependency | Mouse hippocampal tissue [8] | Animal (non-whitelisted source) |

No mechanism has been directly demonstrated in controlled human in-vivo studies.

### 1.4 The HNG (S14G) and HNGF6A analogs

The Hashimoto/Nishimoto group characterized a panel of humanin analogs in their 2001 *Journal of Neuroscience* paper, using rat hippocampal primary neurons and IMR-32 human neuroblastoma cells [3, in_vitro]. Of the positions tested, the single Ser→Gly substitution at position 14 (**S14G-humanin**, abbreviated **HNG**) produced the most dramatic potency increase — approximately **1,000-fold** greater neuroprotective activity than native humanin in the hippocampal neuroprotection assay. The structural basis of this 1,000-fold gain is not fully understood; the serine-to-glycine change at position 14 likely alters the peptide backbone flexibility and secondary structure in ways that enhance binding to one or more of its receptor targets. A second potent analog, **HNGF6A** (Phe6→Ala substitution layered on top of S14G), has been used predominantly in cardiovascular and metabolic in-vivo models because the additional F6A change reduces IGFBP-3 affinity and thereby extends circulating half-life relative to HNG alone.

Because native humanin requires high-nanomolar to low-micromolar concentrations in cell assays to achieve consistent protection, and because HNG achieves the same protection at picomolar to low-nanomolar concentrations, the practical reality of building an in-vivo program with native humanin is unfavorable: doses would be impractically large relative to the analog. As a result, the vast majority of published in-vivo animal efficacy work — particularly the cardiovascular, metabolic, and advanced neuroprotection studies published after 2006 — uses HNG or HNGF6A rather than the unmodified 24-mer. **This is a critical interpretive caveat that runs through every section of this report: most preclinical claims about "humanin" in animal disease models are pharmacological effects of the analog, not the endogenous peptide.** The problem is compounded at the consumer level: grey-market vendors sell the native 24-mer, not HNG, meaning that a consumer reading the animal efficacy literature and purchasing "humanin" is comparing their product to a 1,000-fold-more-potent compound. Whether circulating endogenous humanin — measured in humans at low pg/mL to low ng/mL plasma concentrations — ever reaches tissue concentrations equivalent to the HNG or HNGF6A doses used in animal studies is entirely unknown and represents a fundamental unresolved question in humanin translation.

## 2. Research Concentration and Conflict of Interest

### 2.1 Historical lab concentration

The humanin literature is far older and broader than MOTS-c, with the primary discovery literature published 2001–2011 and an accumulated corpus of several hundred primary papers by 2026.

**The discovery of humanin is notable for having occurred at multiple labs in rapid succession.** Three independent lines converged: (a) the Nishimoto/Hashimoto lab (Keio University) identified humanin via the neuronal death-trap screen published in PNAS 2001; (b) Jae C. Reed's group (La Jolla/Burnham Institute) independently identified humanin through Bax interaction screening, converging on the same peptide from an apoptosis angle; and (c) Pinchas Cohen's lab (then at UCLA, later USC Leonard Davis) independently identified humanin via IGFBP-3 interaction screening. This triple-convergent discovery from independent methodological starting points increases confidence that humanin's biology is real rather than a single-group artifact.

**Group A — Nishimoto/Hashimoto/Niikura (Keio University, Tokyo):** The 2001 PNAS discovery paper [2] and approximately 15–20 primary mechanistic and structure-activity papers published 2001–2006. Senior authors: Ikuo Nishimoto (deceased 2006), Yasuko Hashimoto, Takako Niikura. Estimated contribution: **~30–35%** of identifiable primary efficacy/mechanism papers, concentrated 2001–2011.

**Group B — Cohen/Yen/Kim/Lee (USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology):** Cohen, Yen, Kim SJ, Lee C, Mehta HH, Miller B. Core papers include the GH/IGF-1–humanin axis (Lee 2014 [14]), the cognitive aging cohort (Yen 2018 [15]), the metabolomics profile (Mehta 2019 [34]), the lifespan/healthspan paper (Yen 2020 [20]), and ophthalmic biology (Sreekumar 2016 [35], Nashine 2017 [36]). USC affiliates also appear on the Widmer 2013 cardiovascular paper [16] (Cohen P, Wan J). Estimated contribution: **~25–30%**.

**Combined (Groups A + B): approximately 55–65% of identifiable primary efficacy/mechanism papers.** This is meaningfully below the ≥70% single-lab flag that applied to MOTS-c (where USC approached 100% of foundational papers through 2021). Humanin's literature has genuine independent breadth.

**Independent replicating groups (verified):**

- **Muzumdar/Gong (Pittsburgh/Montefiore-Albert Einstein/LSU):** Cardiovascular focus. Muzumdar et al. 2010 (*ATVB*, PMID 20651283) [37] demonstrated HNG cardioprotection in murine MI/R through AMPK/eNOS pathways. Sharp et al. 2020 (*JACC: Basic to Translational Science*, PMID 32760857) [22] conducted the porcine I/R study at LSU Health Science Center and University of Pittsburgh — independent of USC and Keio — holding a separate US patent on humanin/analogs for acute MI.
- **Independent Chinese groups (Chongqing Medical University, West China Hospital, Harbin Medical University, Hainan Medical University):** Multiple independent HNG/S14G-humanin papers in gouty arthritis, cardiac dysfunction, dental pulp cells, and AKI (2021–2024).
- **University of Tartu (Estonia):** NCT03431844 — a completed independent European observational program measuring humanin isoforms in cardiac muscle and blood plasma around cardiac surgery [39].
- **Italian/Greek nephrology consortium:** Bolignano et al. 2024 (*J Nephrol*) prospective hemodialysis biomarker cohort [18], no USC/Keio ties.
- **Ren et al. (Soochow University):** HNG analog inhibits platelet activation and thrombus formation by stabilizing platelet microtubules [38]. **HNG analog, not native humanin.** Independent Chinese lab.

The caveat is that many of the independent papers use the HNG analog — a tool introduced and characterized by the USC/Keio nexus — even where the applying labs are independent.

### 2.2 COI and commercialization

**CohBar Inc.** (NASDAQ: CWBR) was a publicly traded biopharmaceutical company founded around 2013 by Pinchas Cohen and Nir Barzilai (Albert Einstein College of Medicine), with scientific advisory board ties to Cohen's MDP research program. Cohen's COI disclosure — "PC is a consultant and stockholder of CohBar Inc." — appears in every USC humanin paper from at least 2013 onward [33]; Kelvin Yen also disclosed CohBar consultancy [15]. This disclosure is appropriate practice and does not invalidate the science, but is relevant context for interpreting the priority and framing of humanin claims from USC.

**Critically, CohBar's clinical pipeline centered on MOTS-c analogs (CB5945 for obesity, CB4209 for NASH/metabolic liver disease) — not native humanin or HNG.** No humanin-specific IND or human administration trial was identified in a ClinicalTrials.gov search conducted at the time of this report's preparation. CohBar's decision to prioritize MOTS-c over humanin for clinical development is itself informative: despite the deep mechanistic humanin literature from the USC group, CohBar leadership judged MOTS-c analogs more developable — likely due to humanin's short half-life, the analog translation challenge, and the competitive landscape. CohBar dissolved in late 2023 following a failed reverse-merger attempt, and its programs, including the MOTS-c analogs, currently have no active corporate sponsor. This means the best-capitalized institutional attempt to translate MDP biology into human therapeutics concluded without producing a single humanin IND.

The **Muzumdar/Lefer group** (Pittsburgh/LSU) holds a separate US patent on humanin/analogs for acute MI, filed independently of the USC/CohBar IP. The Sharp et al. 2020 *JACC* porcine study [22] is the scientific output of this separate IP interest — meaning at least two independent commercial/IP programs viewed humanin cardioprotection as worth protecting, even if neither has yet produced a clinical program. USC/Cohen hold separate humanin and MDP analog patents referenced in multiple paper COI disclosures; these have not been independently verified by patent number in this audit.

## 3. Neuroprotective Evidence

### 3.1 In vitro — native humanin (founding characterization)

Hashimoto et al. (2001, *Journal of Neuroscience*) systematically characterized humanin's rescue activity across the full spectrum of FAD-relevant insults in rat hippocampal primary neurons and IMR-32 human neuroblastoma cells [3, in_vitro]. Humanin suppressed death induced by four distinct FAD mutant genes — V642I-APP, NL-APP (a double mutant), M146L-PS1, and N141I-PS2 — as well as by direct application of the Aβ₁₋₄₃ peptide. This breadth of protection across genetically distinct FAD insults was notable at the time and suggested humanin acted at a convergent downstream node of FAD-induced death rather than blocking a specific gene product individually. Structure-activity analysis by the same group identified **Cys8** and **Ser14** as essential residues — mutation of either eliminated neuroprotective activity — while the active domain was mapped to **Pro3–Pro19**, the central hydrophobic core of the 24-mer. This structure-activity information directly guided the development of the S14G (HNG) analog: knowing that Ser14 was functionally essential but that altering it paradoxically increased potency (Gly introduces backbone flexibility relative to Ser) was a non-obvious result that became the foundation of the entire analog program. This is in-vitro evidence establishing mechanism and structure-activity relationships, not clinical efficacy.

### 3.2 Rodent — S14G-HNG analog, 3xTg-AD mice

Niikura et al. (2011, *PLoS One*) administered **S14G-humanin (HNG)** intranasally at **10 nmol per session** (5 days/week for the first 2.5 months, then daily for the final 1 month) to triple-transgenic AD mice carrying APPswe, tauP310L, and PS-1M146V mutations (n=7–9 per group, male and female), starting at 13 months of age — a relatively late-stage intervention in a model that develops amyloid and tau pathology progressively from 6 months onward [10, animal]. The behavioral outcome was sex-specific: treated **male** mice spent significantly longer time near the platform location in the Morris water maze compared to vehicle; female mice showed a directionally consistent but not statistically significant effect. The sex difference is unexplained and has not been followed up. Pathological outcomes were more uniformly positive: brain Aβ levels were markedly lower in treated vs. vehicle animals, plaque area was reduced ~52%, and integrated plaque density was reduced ~47%. Hippocampal neprilysin expression was elevated in treated animals — neprilysin is a key Aβ-degrading protease, suggesting a mechanism for the amyloid reduction beyond simple secretase modulation. **Species: mice (3xTg-AD). Compound: S14G-HNG analog, not native humanin. No human subjects. Sample size of 7–9 per group is adequate for an initial rodent study but provides limited power for subgroup analyses.**

### 3.3 Rodent — native humanin, Aβ1-42–injected rats

Chai et al. (2014, *Neuroscience Bulletin*) administered **native humanin** (not HNG) to rats with Aβ₁₋₄₂–induced cognitive pathology [11, animal]. Sample size was not individually reported in the abstract, a limitation to note. Humanin treatment increased dendritic spine density and upregulated synaptic proteins — two structural markers of synapse maintenance that are reduced in AD models — and enhanced long-term potentiation (LTP), the electrophysiological correlate of learning and memory formation. Behavioral memory deficits were reversed. Mechanistically, the study identified attenuation of tau hyperphosphorylation via **PP2A disinhibition** as a contributing pathway: Aβ₁₋₄₂ inhibits protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A), leading to tau hyperphosphorylation at AD-relevant sites; humanin appeared to restore PP2A activity, reducing pathological tau phosphorylation. This is significant because it extends humanin's mechanism beyond Aβ clearance (the neprilysin finding from the 3xTg-AD study) into the tau pathology arm — meaning that native humanin, at doses high enough to be efficacious in rats, addresses multiple pathological targets. **Species: rats. Compound: native humanin (not the HNG analog). No human subjects. This is the strongest evidence that native humanin — not only the 1,000-fold more potent HNG — has meaningful in-vivo neuroprotective activity, though dose and concentration context relative to human pharmacology remains uncharacterized.**

### 3.4 Human AD — exogenous humanin administration

**No human clinical trial of exogenous humanin or HNG for Alzheimer's disease or any neurological condition exists as of mid-2026.** The neuroprotection evidence base is entirely rodent and cell culture.

## 4. Metabolic Evidence

### 4.1 Central and peripheral insulin sensitization — rodent

Muzumdar et al. (2009, *PLoS One*) demonstrated humanin as a novel central regulator of peripheral insulin action, testing multiple analogs across two rodent models [12, animal]. In Sprague-Dawley rats (n=92 three-month-old males), **intracerebroventricular (ICV) native humanin** delivered by primed-continuous infusion (20 µg total; 0.16 µg/kg/min) significantly improved whole-body insulin sensitivity as measured by hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp — the gold-standard method for quantifying insulin action. The improvement was mediated by hypothalamic STAT-3 activation, establishing that humanin's metabolic signaling occurs centrally via the same JAK2/STAT3 pathway documented in the neuroprotection literature. ICV delivery bypasses the blood-brain barrier issue that complicates systemic peptide dosing; this route is not practical for human use. In addition to the central ICV arm, **peripheral HNGF6A infusion** (not native humanin) enhanced hepatic insulin sensitivity and skeletal muscle glucose uptake via separate peripheral signaling. In Zucker Diabetic Fatty (ZDF) rats (n=9) — a model of type 2 diabetes — peripheral HNGF6A lowered blood glucose approximately 50%, a functionally dramatic effect. The mechanistic distinction between the central ICV arm (native humanin → hypothalamic STAT3) and the peripheral arm (HNGF6A → hepatic and skeletal muscle glucose uptake) suggests humanin may operate through both central and peripheral metabolic circuits, but the practical translation question remains unanswered because the effective doses in these rodent models have not been contextualized against human endogenous humanin levels. The same paper measured circulating humanin in humans cross-sectionally — showing age-related decline in plasma humanin — but **no humanin was administered to any human subject in this study**. The cross-sectional human data is observational only.

### 4.2 β-cell insulin secretion — HNGF6A analog

Kuliawat et al. (2013, *FASEB Journal*) reported that **HNGF6A** — not native humanin — increased glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) in isolated islets from both normal and diabetic mice, and in the βTC3 murine β-cell line [13, in_vitro]. The mechanism involved enhanced mitochondrial metabolism in the β cell itself: HNGF6A appeared to augment oxidative phosphorylation coupling efficiency, increasing the ATP/ADP ratio that drives K_ATP channel closure and calcium-dependent insulin exocytosis. This positions humanin analogs as potential amplifiers of nutrient sensing at the β-cell level — a mechanism separate from the hypothalamic STAT3 axis characterized by Muzumdar et al. The fact that the effect was present in diabetic mouse islets (which typically have impaired mitochondrial function) as well as normal islets is noteworthy, though the diabetic model specifics were not detailed in the abstract. **Species: mouse islets and βTC3 cell line. Compound: HNGF6A analog — not native humanin. No in-vivo rodent n reported for the GSIS experiments; no human data of any kind.**

### 4.3 GH/IGF-1 axis interaction — rodent + human observational

Lee et al. (2014, *Aging Cell*) established an inverse relationship between GH/IGF-1 signaling and circulating humanin across multiple genetically engineered mouse lines and two human cohorts [14, animal]:

| Model | GH/IGF status | Humanin change | Lifespan effect |
|-------|---------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Ames dwarf (df/df) mice | GH/IGF deficient | +40% (n=10/group) | Extended |
| GH-transgenic mice | GH/IGF elevated | −70% (n=11/group) | Shortened (~50%) |
| LID mice (liver IGF-1 deletion) | High GH, low IGF-I | +45% (n=7/group) | Near-normal |
| IGFBP-3 knockout (BP3KO) mice | Enhanced free IGF-I | −70% (n=3/group) | Shortened |

The pattern is striking: every genetic manipulation that reduces the GH/IGF-1 axis raises humanin, and every manipulation that elevates it reduces humanin. The LID (liver-specific IGF-1 deletion) model is particularly informative — these mice have high GH (compensatory) but low serum IGF-I; their elevated humanin resembles the Ames dwarf pattern, indicating it is **IGF-I specifically** (not GH per se) that suppresses humanin. The IGFBP-3 knockout result (higher free IGF-I → lower humanin) further confirms that bioavailable IGF-I drives humanin suppression. This places humanin mechanistically downstream of a key longevity pathway.

Human data in this paper: GH-deficient children (n=11) showed a negative correlation between baseline humanin and IGF-I levels (r=−0.69, p<0.05); 4 weeks of GH therapy reduced plasma humanin approximately 20% — a loss-of-function pharmacological confirmation of the mouse findings in humans. Ecuadorians with GH receptor deficiency (Laron syndrome, n=6/group) had humanin elevated approximately 80% compared to matched controls — the most dramatic human confirmation of the inverse GH/IGF–humanin relationship. **All human data in this paper is observational: endogenous humanin was measured, not administered. These data establish the GH/IGF-1–humanin axis as a regulated biological relationship, not the efficacy of humanin supplementation.**

## 5. Human Circulating-Humanin Evidence: Endogenous Biomarker Associations

**All human data in this section is observational — endogenous humanin as a circulating biomarker, NOT exogenous administration.** These studies document associations of the body's own humanin levels with health states; they do not establish what happens when humanin is administered from outside.

**Critical interpretive caveat — causal direction unresolved:** The "lower humanin ↔ worse outcome" pattern across these studies is correlational and the causal direction is entirely unresolved. Two alternative causal framings are equally consistent with the data: (a) lower endogenous humanin contributes causally to the adverse outcome (humanin as protector); or (b) the adverse pathological state — mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic stress, end-stage renal disease — drives humanin downward as a downstream consequence (reverse causation: disease → ↓humanin). A third framing, supported by the hemodialysis U-shape (§5.4) and the MELAS finding (§5.5), introduces a further complication: extreme mitochondrial distress may paradoxically drive humanin *upward* as a compensatory stress-response, in which case very high circulating humanin marks cells at highest risk rather than protected ones. These three framings cannot be distinguished by cross-sectional or prospective biomarker data alone. No interventional study has randomized subjects to humanin supplementation and measured the outcomes tracked in these cohorts.

### 5.1 Cognitive aging — human cohort and SNP association

Yen et al. (2018, *Scientific Reports*, PMID 30242290) examined the relationship between circulating humanin levels and cognitive outcomes in two independent human cohorts [15, cohort]:

The **BVAIT Study** (n=146 matched participants, mean age approximately 58 years, free of cardiovascular disease) observed that African Americans had approximately 20% lower circulating humanin than Caucasian Americans matched for age, sex, and cardiovascular risk. This racial disparity in circulating humanin is unexplained — potential contributing factors include mitochondrial haplogroup differences, dietary patterns, or socioeconomic stress load, none of which were specifically tested. The study design is cross-sectional and cannot establish temporal sequence.

The **Health and Retirement Study (HRS)** (n=15,620 participants with genotype data) linked humanin biology to cognitive aging through a mitochondrial genetic variant. A SNP in the MT-RNR2 humanin-coding region (rs2854128) associated with approximately 14% lower circulating humanin. Critically, in African Americans specifically, this SNP associated with approximately **2 years' accelerated cognitive age** — a finding measured using Levine's epigenetic-clock methodology on the HRS cognitive battery data. This is among the largest human studies ever conducted on a mitochondrial peptide, and the sample size gives it unusual statistical power. However, as an observational genetic association, it cannot distinguish whether lower humanin is a cause of the accelerated cognitive aging, a correlated biomarker of mitochondrial dysfunction that independently drives cognitive decline, or a common downstream effect of some other aging process. The mouse arm of this same paper showed that administered HNG improved cognition in aged mice; the human arm is observational only, and the two arms answer different questions.

### 5.2 Cardiovascular / coronary endothelial function — human cross-sectional

Widmer et al. (2013, *American Journal of Physiology — Heart and Circulatory Physiology*, PMID 23220334) measured circulating humanin in 40 patients undergoing coronary angiography, split equally between those with documented coronary endothelial dysfunction (n=20) and those with preserved function (n=20) [16, cohort]. Coronary endothelial function was measured directly by intracoronary acetylcholine challenge — the gold-standard invasive assessment — making this an unusually rigorous human biomarker study for its era. Patients with endothelial dysfunction had significantly lower plasma humanin (1.3 ± 1.1 vs. 2.2 ± 1.5 ng/mL, p=0.03), and plasma humanin correlated positively with the coronary blood flow response to acetylcholine (p=0.0091). The correlation with a functional vascular measure rather than a structural or self-reported endpoint is notable and aligns mechanistically with the HNGF6A atherosclerosis findings in ApoE-deficient mice (§6.5), where the analog restored endothelial nitric oxide synthase expression. This is a small study (n=40) and cross-sectional — the direction of causality is unknown. It does not test whether administering humanin improves coronary endothelial function. **Observational, not interventional. No humanin administered.**

### 5.3 Impaired fasting glucose — human cross-sectional

Voigt & Jelinek (2016, *Physiological Reports*, PMID 27173674) measured plasma humanin in 81 participants at a diabetes screening clinic — 58 with normal glucose regulation and 23 with impaired fasting glucose (IFG), an intermediate metabolic state between normoglycemia and frank type 2 diabetes [17, cohort]. Plasma humanin was significantly lower in the IFG group: 124.3 ± 83.9 pg/mL vs. 204.8 ± 92.9 pg/mL in controls (p=0.0001). The effect size is large relative to the standard deviations, and the p-value is well below conventional thresholds despite the modest n=81. The authors interpreted the reduced humanin in IFG as a **mitochondrial stress-response marker**: the IFG state represents metabolic dysfunction that places chronic energetic and oxidative stress on cells, particularly in insulin-sensitive tissues; the finding is consistent with the hypothesis that mitochondrial distress in IFG either reduces the capacity to produce humanin or that lower humanin is a contributor to impaired glucose regulation. The Muzumdar et al. 2009 rodent work is mechanistically compatible — if central humanin maintains hypothalamic STAT3 tone that modulates peripheral insulin sensitivity, then reduced circulating humanin might genuinely contribute to glucose dysregulation rather than merely reflecting it. This association-not-causation caveat is standard: without an intervention arm, the IFG cohort finding cannot resolve direction of causality. **Observational cross-sectional. No humanin administered to any participant.**

### 5.4 Hemodialysis / cardiovascular risk — prospective human cohort

Bolignano et al. (2024, *Journal of Nephrology*, PMID 39102184) followed 94 chronic hemodialysis patients (mean age 68 years, 63.9% male, enrolled from three centers in Italy and Greece) for 30 months, measuring plasma humanin at baseline and relating it to a composite cardiovascular endpoint (all-cause mortality plus major cardiovascular events) [18, cohort]. The finding was non-linear and unexpected: both **very low** (<450.7 pg/mL) and **very high** (>759.5 pg/mL) baseline humanin were associated with increased hazard for the composite endpoint, while the intermediate range (approximately 575–759.5 pg/mL) showed the lowest event incidence. This U-shaped relationship — rather than the simple "more humanin is better" expectation generated by the biomarker cross-sectional data — has important interpretive implications. The very high humanin group's elevated risk may reflect a maladaptive compensatory response: cells under severe mitochondrial stress (as in end-stage renal disease) upregulate humanin as a stress response, and extremely elevated circulating humanin may be a marker of severe underlying cellular distress rather than a protective state. This is consistent with the MELAS muscle finding (§5.5), where humanin accumulates in ragged-red fibers as a response to mitochondrial energy crisis. The 56.4% event rate over 30 months reflects the high-risk nature of this hemodialysis cohort. The Italian/Greek consortium conducting this study has no identified ties to USC or Keio, making it the most independent large prospective humanin biomarker study in the corpus. **Prospective observational biomarker study. No humanin administered. The non-linear relationship is an important caution against assuming that maximal circulating humanin is uniformly beneficial.**

### 5.5 MELAS — humanin in skeletal muscle tissue

Kariya et al. (2005, *Acta Neuropathologica*, PMID 15759134) examined humanin expression in skeletal muscle biopsies from patients with MELAS (mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes) compared with control muscle [19, cohort]. MELAS is caused by point mutations in mitochondrial tRNA genes (most commonly m.3243A>G in MT-TL1) that severely impair mitochondrial translation and respiratory chain function. In MELAS patient biopsies, humanin immunostaining was strongly positive in **ragged-red fibers** — the histological hallmark of MELAS, representing fibers with subsarcolemmal accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria — and humanin signal appeared elevated compared to control muscle, with localization to mitochondria. The authors proposed that humanin is induced in response to the energy crisis in MELAS fibers, potentially as a cellular stress response attempting to protect the fiber from apoptosis. This is conceptually coherent with the hemodialysis finding (§5.4): extreme mitochondrial dysfunction drives humanin expression upward, which may paradoxically mark cells at highest risk rather than cells that are protected. The MELAS finding further suggests that humanin may be a broadly responsive mitochondrial stress sensor rather than a passive aging biomarker. **Tissue biomarker study in human patients; no plasma measurement; no administration of humanin; no sample size reported in the abstract.**

## 6. Aging, Longevity, and Broad Cytoprotection

### 6.1 Plasma humanin declines with age across species

Circulating humanin falls with age in multiple mammalian species and the cross-species pattern is informative. In rhesus macaques (n=86 distributed across three age cohorts), a dramatic decline was observed between ages 19 and 25 years — a window that maps to the late middle-age to early senescent period in this species. In mice, plasma humanin dropped approximately 40% over the first 18 months of life, a period spanning early adulthood to middle age in the murine lifespan. Humans show the same general trajectory, with plasma humanin declining in adult populations when measured cross-sectionally by age group. The naked mole rat finding is the most theoretically significant exception: these animals have a maximum lifespan of approximately 30 years despite a body mass that would predict a ~5-year lifespan by allometric scaling; they show cancer resistance and negligible senescence phenotypes. In naked mole rats (n=10), plasma humanin showed only a non-significant trend toward decline with age (p=0.08), in sharp contrast to the robust declines in shorter-lived rodents [20, cohort]. A 2021 GeroScience narrative review by Kim et al. confirmed this cross-species pattern — mice, humans, and rhesus monkeys all decline; naked mole rats resist it — and interpreted the finding as consistent with the hypothesis that maintained humanin levels may contribute to exceptional longevity biology, though the authors explicitly acknowledged the association is non-causal [21, mechanism_review]. All data are observational across species; no experiment has tested whether reversing the age-related humanin decline by pharmacological means extends mammalian lifespan or healthspan, and naked mole rats have numerous other features distinguishing them from standard aging models.

### 6.2 Centenarian and longevity-cohort associations (human, observational)

Yen et al. (2020, *Aging*, PMID 32575074) found that offspring of centenarians (n=18) displayed significantly higher circulating humanin compared with age-matched controls without exceptional longevity family history (n=19) [20, cohort]. Because offspring of centenarians have enriched longevity genetics, elevated humanin may be a co-inherited marker rather than a causal driver — the study design cannot distinguish these possibilities.

The same paper reported that humanin overexpression in *C. elegans* extended lifespan in a daf-16/FOXO-dependent manner; this is an invertebrate finding and does not establish causation in mammals [20, cohort].

### 6.3 The GH/IGF-1–humanin longevity axis in mouse models

The GH/IGF-1 axis data (Lee 2014, see §4.3 above) provides a mechanistic thread: long-lived, low-GH/IGF-1 mouse models have elevated humanin while short-lived GH-transgenic mice have markedly reduced humanin [14]. **Critical framing:** all Ames dwarf and GHRKO lifespan data are genetic-model findings in mice. The humanin elevation in these long-lived animals is associative; no experiment has shown that pharmacologically restoring or elevating humanin in any model modifies lifespan accordingly.

### 6.4 Cardioprotection — HNG analog in rodent and porcine models

**Mouse (I/R model):** Muzumdar et al. (2010, *Arteriosclerosis, Thrombosis, and Vascular Biology*, PMID 20651283) administered HNG (2 mg/kg i.p. or intracardiac) to C57BL/6J mice subjected to 45-minute left coronary artery occlusion followed by 24-hour reperfusion [37, animal]. HNG reduced infarct size by approximately 50% and improved left ventricular function when given before or at reperfusion. The mechanistic pathway established was **AMPK → eNOS**: HNG activated AMP-activated protein kinase in cardiomyocytes, which phosphorylated endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) at Ser1177, increasing NO bioavailability and reducing ischemia-reperfusion injury via NO-dependent cardioprotection. This mechanism is distinct from the anti-apoptotic Bax/tBid pathway and from the FPR2/STAT3 neuronal receptor pathway — demonstrating that humanin engages different downstream effectors in different tissue contexts. Protection was dose-dependent and route-independent between i.p. and intracardiac delivery. This study was conducted by the Pittsburgh/Montefiore-Albert Einstein group, independent of the USC and Keio programs.

**Porcine (I/R model):** Sharp et al. (2020, *JACC: Basic to Translational Science*, PMID 32760857) tested HNG (2 mg/kg i.v., administered 10 minutes before reperfusion) in female Yucatan minipigs at two ischemia durations [22, animal]. At 60 minutes of ischemia (n=14), HNG reduced infarct size relative to area-at-risk by 41% (p=0.017) and reduced apoptosis as measured by TUNEL staining by 50% (p=0.019). When ischemia was extended to 75 minutes (n=19), the same HNG dose failed to provide significant protection (p=0.541). This dose-duration interaction is an important translational signal: even a 25% increase in ischemia duration (60 to 75 min) eliminated efficacy at a fixed dose, suggesting the cardioprotective window is sensitive to injury severity in a way that would require dose-titration for clinical application. Yucatan minipigs are considered a closer physiological model to human cardiac anatomy and physiology than mice, making this the most translationally relevant humanin cardioprotection study — but it still uses HNG, not native humanin, and the failed 75-minute arm underscores that efficacy is not assured.

**Chronic heart failure (mouse):** Zhao et al. (2023, *Heliyon*, PMID 38045183) administered HNG (4 mg/kg) to C57BL/6J mice (n=55 total) in two heart-failure models: isoproterenol-induced cardiomyopathy (chronic β-adrenergic stress, 28 days) and transverse aortic constriction (pressure overload) [23, animal]. HNG preserved ejection fraction, reduced cardiac fibrosis via **TGF-β–Smad2/3 pathway suppression**, and attenuated cardiomyocyte apoptosis in both models. The Chongqing Medical University group that conducted this study has no identified USC/Keio ties, representing genuine independent replication. All findings are HNG analog in rodents.

### 6.5 Vascular / endothelial protection

Oh et al. (2011, *Atherosclerosis*, PMID 21763658) used **HNGF6A** — the combined S14G plus Phe6→Ala analog — in ApoE-deficient mice (n=12 per group, 4 groups) fed a high-cholesterol diet for 16 weeks [24, animal]. Daily i.p. HNGF6A reduced atherosclerotic plaque area in the proximal aorta by approximately 89%, restored endothelial nitric oxide synthase expression in the vascular wall, reduced intra-plaque apoptosis (measured by TUNEL) by approximately 57%, and preserved acetylcholine-induced vascular relaxation. A critical feature of this result is what did NOT change: systemic lipid profiles (total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides) and circulating cytokines were unaltered by HNGF6A treatment. This means the anti-atherosclerotic effect operated through a direct vascular mechanism — anti-apoptosis, eNOS restoration, and local cell survival — rather than through lipid lowering or systemic anti-inflammatory effects. The magnitude of plaque reduction (89%) in a 16-week model is exceptional, though ApoE-deficient mice develop very aggressive atherosclerosis and the ApoE-KO model is known to respond dramatically to interventions that are more modest in clinical populations. USC affiliates (Cohen P, Wan J, Cobb LJ) are co-authors alongside the Mayo Clinic cardiovascular group (Lerman A, Lerman LO). **HNGF6A analog, not native humanin, in a mouse model. No human vascular intervention data.**

### 6.6 Renal protection

Shi et al. (2024, *BBRC*, PMID 39332154) studied HNG in lipopolysaccharide-induced septic acute kidney injury in male C57BL/6 mice [25, animal]. HNG improved renal function, reduced tubular injury, attenuated pro-inflammatory cytokines, and reduced oxidative stress. Proteomic analysis identified 132 altered proteins linked to fatty acid degradation and PPAR signaling. No native humanin renal efficacy studies in humans exist.

### 6.7 Ophthalmic cytoprotection

Sreekumar et al. (2016, *Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci*, PMID 26990160) showed that native humanin protects retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells from oxidative stress, senescence, and mitochondrial dysfunction in cell culture and transmitochondrial ARPE-19 cybrids [35, in_vitro]. Nashine et al. (2017, *Cell Death Dis*, PMID 28726777) extended this to HNG, showing protection of AMD transmitochondrial cybrids from mitochondrial and cellular damage [36, in_vitro]. **Cell-culture models only; no human ophthalmic trials.**

### 6.8 The translation gap: no exogenous humanin intervention data in humans

No registered or published randomized controlled trial has tested exogenous humanin — native peptide or any analog — for aging, longevity, cardioprotection, cytoprotection, or any other endpoint in human subjects. The entire cytoprotection efficacy evidence base is: (a) exogenous analogs (HNG, HNGF6A), not native humanin; (b) rodent and porcine models; (c) no human RCT or open-label trial. Native humanin's plasma half-life of approximately **20–30 minutes** (rodent PK) further complicates any straightforward administration strategy. This gap must be stated explicitly in any clinical framing of humanin.

## 7. Safety, Dosing, Route, PK, and Regulatory Status

### 7.1 Human dosing — no established dose exists

There is **NO approved or established exogenous humanin or HNG dose for humans**. No Phase 1, 2, or 3 human administration trial of exogenous humanin or its analogs has been completed and published as of mid-2026 [26, regulatory]. A search of ClinicalTrials.gov yields no registered interventional trial of exogenous humanin or HNG in human subjects [26, regulatory].

Grey-market compounding vendors and peptide protocol sites cite doses in the range of ~100–500 µg/day SC or IM for HNG, extrapolated from rodent animal-model dosing. These figures are **UNVALIDATED** and **may NOT ground any numerical safety or efficacy claim**. They are listed here solely to document that such claims circulate; they have no basis in human PK, human tolerability, or dose-finding studies.

### 7.2 Route of administration and pharmacokinetics (preclinical)

All published PK data for humanin and its analogs are from rodent studies; there are no human PK data of any kind [27, mechanism_review].

**Native humanin (HN):** Plasma half-life of approximately **20–30 minutes** in wild-type mice following IP injection. Multiple secondary summaries of the rodent PK literature cite figures in this range — some citing ~20 min, others ~30 min — both are valid characterizations of short-lived circulating peptide behavior. Rapid proteolytic degradation by circulating peptidases is the presumed elimination mechanism, which is why stabilized analogs were developed in the first place. The implication for any potential human administration of native humanin is significant: at a 20–30 minute plasma half-life, maintaining physiologically meaningful circulating levels would require continuous infusion or very frequent injection. Subcutaneous daily dosing — the route used in grey-market protocols — would produce brief concentration peaks followed by rapid clearance.

**HNG (Gly14-humanin):** The S14G substitution produces an analog with approximately 1,000-fold greater potency than native HN and a meaningfully longer plasma half-life than native humanin. In adult male Sprague-Dawley rats after IP injection, HNG is detectable in plasma, liver, heart, and brain by ELISA, indicating some degree of CNS penetration that native humanin achieves less consistently [27, mechanism_review]. The half-life of HNG remains in the sub-4-hour range in rodents — still short, but long enough to support the once-daily dosing used in most animal efficacy experiments.

**Tissue half-life vs. plasma half-life:** A distinction reported in the rodent PK literature is that tissue half-life in target organs (heart, brain) appears substantially longer than plasma half-life. This is the rationale for once-daily dosing in animal efficacy studies despite short plasma half-life — the peptide is cleared rapidly from blood but may accumulate transiently in target tissues at concentrations that persist beyond the plasma window. No human tissue distribution data exist.

**IGFBP-3 binding modulates PK:** Analogs with reduced IGFBP-3 binding affinity — particularly HNGF6A — display extended circulating half-life compared to HNG in wild-type mice, because IGFBP-3 binding accelerates the formation of a complex that is eliminated faster than free peptide [27, mechanism_review]. This creates a PK hierarchy among analogs: HNGF6A (low IGFBP-3 binding) > HNG (moderate IGFBP-3 binding) > native HN (highest IGFBP-3 binding) in terms of circulating persistence. Species-specific IGFBP-3 concentrations and binding affinities mean this hierarchy cannot be directly extrapolated to humans.

**No human PK data:** The pharmacokinetics, oral bioavailability, subcutaneous bioavailability, tissue distribution, blood-brain barrier penetration, metabolic fate, and elimination pathway of exogenous humanin or HNG in humans are entirely uncharacterized. Any human dosing extrapolated from rodent PK involves unknown interspecies scaling factors.

### 7.3 Human safety — essentially uncharacterized

**Direct human safety evidence: none.** Because no human administration trials have been conducted, there are no published human adverse event data, no maximum tolerated dose, no NOAEL, and no human dose-response curve for exogenous humanin or HNG [26, regulatory; 27, mechanism_review].

**Animal safety:** Rodent studies at pharmacologically active doses have not reported overt dose-limiting toxicity in short-duration experiments. However, animal safety characterization has been conducted almost exclusively in the context of efficacy, not formal toxicology. Repeat-dose, genotoxicity, and carcinogenicity studies in animals are not represented in the peer-reviewed literature.

**Injection-site risks:** Grey-market preparations carry the standard risks of unregulated parenteral administration: contamination, particulates, unknown purity, and non-sterile technique.

### 7.4 Counter-signal: anti-apoptotic / pro-tumor concern

**This is the single most load-bearing safety signal for humanin and must be surfaced explicitly.**

Humanin is a potent anti-apoptotic peptide. Its cytoprotective mechanism — binding Bax, tBid, and BimEL; blocking cytochrome c release; suppressing caspase activation — is the same mechanism that protects cancer cells from programmed death. Two independent published preclinical studies document this:

**Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC):** Moreno Ayala et al. (2020, *Sci Rep*, PMID 32444831) demonstrated in a syngeneic 4T1 murine TNBC model that: (a) humanin expression is elevated in human TNBC biopsy tissue relative to normal mammary gland; (b) systemic exogenous humanin administration significantly accelerated tumor growth and promoted spontaneous lung metastases; (c) exogenous humanin protected TNBC cells from doxorubicin-induced apoptosis and impaired the anti-metastatic effect of chemotherapy; (d) shRNA silencing of endogenous humanin reduced tumor cell viability and enhanced chemosensitivity [28, animal]. The implication is that exogenous humanin supplementation could, in a host with occult or established breast cancer, protect malignant cells from both immune-mediated apoptosis and cytotoxic chemotherapy.

**Glioblastoma (GBM):** Ha et al. (2024, *Cell Death Dis*, PMID 38942749) showed that humanin directly binds integrin αV on GBM cells, activating the integrin αV–TGFβ signaling axis, which drives Rac1/Cdc42-mediated cytoskeletal remodeling, filopodia formation, enhanced cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis (elevated VEGF, VEGFR2, CD31). In orthotopic xenograft mice, humanin-treated animals showed significantly shorter survival, larger tumors, and aggressive invasiveness compared to controls. TGFβR1 inhibition reversed these effects [29, animal]. The authors explicitly flag that humanin's historically beneficial cytoprotective properties may prove "detrimental in cancer contexts."

**Clinical implication (theoretical, no human data):** Any individual with a personal or family history of cancer, or with suspected occult malignancy, faces an uncharacterized but mechanistically plausible risk from exogenous humanin or HNG. The anti-apoptotic mechanism is not a theoretical concern — it is documented in two separate cancer models across two different tumor types and two different signaling pathways. This constitutes a hard contraindication candidate pending human safety data.

### 7.5 Regulatory status

**FDA:** Humanin (including HNG and related analogs) has no approval from any governmental regulatory health authority for human therapeutic use. Not an FDA-approved drug; not in the U.S. Pharmacopoeia [30, regulatory].

**503A compounding status:** Humanin does not appear on the FDA's 503A Category 1 Bulk Drug Substances list (substances eligible for compounding). The April 2026 FDA announcement of a PCAC review meeting scheduled for July 23–24, 2026 named seven specific peptides for evaluation: BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Emideltide (DSIP), Semax, and Epitalon [31, regulatory]. **Humanin is not among those seven nominees.** It also does not appear on the Category 2 active list. Humanin's absence from both active lists means it occupies an unresolved regulatory grey zone: it lacks 503A authorization to be compounded, lacks a monograph, and is not a component of any approved drug. Under the 503A framework as interpreted by FDA, compounding humanin for human use is not permitted.

**503B (outsourcing facilities):** No 503B pathway exists for humanin.

**Grey-market status:** Humanin and HNG are sold via online "research chemical" or "research peptide" vendors, not subject to FDA manufacturing oversight. Purity, sterility, and concentration cannot be assumed.

**WADA:** Humanin is prohibited in competitive sport under **S0 — Non-Approved Substances**. The 2026 WADA Prohibited List defines S0 as any pharmacological substance with no current approval by any governmental regulatory health authority for human therapeutic use [32, regulatory]. Because humanin has no regulatory approval anywhere for human therapeutic use, it falls squarely within S0's class definition regardless of whether it is named individually. MOTS-c (a related MDP) is confirmed WADA-prohibited and is undergoing July 2026 PCAC review; the same regulatory logic applies to humanin. Athletes subject to WADA testing must treat humanin as prohibited in-competition and out-of-competition.

### 7.6 Contraindications, monitoring, and stopping criteria

Given the absence of human trials, the following framework is precautionary and mechanistically grounded, not evidence-based in the clinical sense:

**Absolute contraindications (precautionary, based on mechanism and animal data):**
- Active malignancy (any type) — anti-apoptotic mechanism demonstrated to protect tumor cells from chemotherapy and promote metastasis in TNBC [28, animal]
- Personal history of cancer where recurrence risk is active — same mechanistic concern
- Glioblastoma or other aggressive CNS tumors — humanin activates pro-invasive integrin αV–TGFβ signaling [29, animal]
- Competitive athletes subject to WADA code — S0 prohibited substance [32, regulatory]

**Strong relative contraindications / caution signals:**
- First-degree family history of breast cancer or GBM — insufficient data to rule out risk potentiation
- Any clinical context where apoptosis suppression is undesirable (e.g., active antitumor immunotherapy)

**Monitoring (if used in research/experimental context):**
- Baseline cancer screening (CBC with differential, relevant tumor markers based on personal risk) before initiation
- Periodic screening maintained during any extended use
- Oncology consultation if cancer history is present

**Stopping criteria:**
- Any new cancer diagnosis during administration — STOP immediately; alert treating oncologist to anti-apoptotic mechanism
- Unexplained lymphadenopathy, weight loss, or constitutional symptoms — STOP and evaluate
- Any regulatory change reclassifying humanin explicitly for compounding or prohibiting it under a new enforcement category

## 8. Native vs. Analog: Sourcing and Identity

### 8.1 Consumer "humanin" ≠ research HNG

Consumer-facing products labeled "humanin" or sold as research chemicals deliver the native 24-amino-acid peptide (MAPRGFSCLLLLTSEIDLPVKRRA), synthesized from the MT-RNR2 open reading frame sequence. The majority of in-vivo animal efficacy papers — especially the most potent cardiovascular, metabolic, and neuroprotective effects — used **HNG (S14G-humanin)**, a single amino-acid substitution that confers approximately 1,000-fold greater potency than native humanin [3, in_vitro]. Equating consumer humanin with HNG-derived efficacy data is an analog-substitution error: the two are different chemical entities with different pharmacokinetics, different receptor binding affinities, and different effective dose ranges.

This analog-conflation problem has an additional dimension in the humanin literature: even where a paper describes using "native humanin" in vivo, the doses used in those animal studies (commonly micrograms to milligrams per kilogram by injection) may not correspond to any physiologically achievable plasma concentration through a practical human administration route. The grey-market humanin sold by research peptide vendors is the native 24-mer, not HNG — yet the vendor marketing and the user forum discussions freely cite HNG animal study outcomes as if they describe native humanin pharmacology. A thoughtful reader of the research literature must track, paper by paper, which compound was actually used; §3, §4, §6, and §7 of this report do this explicitly.

### 8.2 Genomic identity complexity

The humanin ORF sits within the mitochondrial 16S rRNA gene (MT-RNR2, Gene ID 4550, chrMT). At least 13 nuclear-encoded pseudogenes and paralogs designated MTRNR2L1 through MTRNR2L13 exist in the human nuclear genome, sharing 92–95% amino-acid identity with mitochondrial humanin. Some of these nuclear copies (e.g., MTRNR2L2, MTRNR2L4) encode isoforms with four additional C-terminal residues and may produce functional peptides — though none have been characterized to the same mechanistic depth as the original mtDNA-encoded humanin [1, mechanism_review].

This paralog situation creates multiple complications. At the transcript level: studies that measure "humanin mRNA" may be capturing nuclear paralog transcripts indistinguishably from the mitochondrial gene. At the protein/plasma level: ELISA assays measuring "circulating humanin" depend on antibodies whose epitope selectivity for the mitochondrial-encoded isoform vs. nuclear-paralog isoforms is not always validated; it is possible that what researchers call "plasma humanin" in human cohort studies is a mixture of mitochondrial humanin and multiple nuclear paralog products. At the product-identity level: consumer humanin peptides are synthesized to the canonical 24-amino-acid sequence from the MT-RNR2 ORF, but purity, sequence accuracy, and identity relative to endogenous humanin isoforms are unverifiable by the buyer without mass spectrometry confirmation. Grey-market vendors do not routinely provide independent analytical certificates of identity for these peptides. A buyer has no way to confirm they received the intended 24-mer without independent testing.

### 8.3 Sourcing realism

No humanin product — native 24-mer or HNG analog — is approved by the FDA, EMA, TGA, or any comparable governmental regulatory authority as a therapeutic agent or dietary supplement. No IND has been publicly filed for native humanin as a clinical investigational agent. Consumer access is research-chemical/grey-market only.

At the high-quality laboratory research end, Bachem (a Swiss peptide synthesis company) synthesizes humanin as a research-grade reference standard; this product is intended for laboratory use, not human administration, and is sold at research quantities. Grey-market peptide vendors offer humanin alongside BPC-157, MOTS-c, TB-500, and similar compounds, typically positioned as "for research purposes only." These vendors' products carry none of the quality assurance guarantees that apply to FDA-regulated drugs or even FDA-regulated compounded preparations: purity, sterility, endotoxin content, sequence accuracy, and storage stability are unverified by any independent body.

Subcutaneous self-injection at home — the route most commonly described in grey-market peptide forums — carries infection risk from non-sterile technique, risk from impure product, and risk from unknown immunogenicity of a peptide that has never been tested for tolerability in humans. There are no compounding pharmacy data sheets for humanin because it is not on any 503A authorized list; no licensed U.S. compounding pharmacy can legally produce humanin for human administration under current FDA interpretation. This is a research-only compound with no established clinical dosing schema, no human tolerability data, and no regulatory approval pathway currently active.

## 9. Bottom Line

Humanin is the **founding member of the mitochondrial-derived peptide class** — a historically significant discovery from 2001 with a mechanistically rich profile: direct pro-apoptotic protein sequestration (Bax, tBid), IGFBP-3 modulation, FPR2 signaling (cryo-EM-characterized), and CNTFR/WSX-1/gp130 → STAT3 activation. The endogenous peptide is a legitimate biomarker: lower circulating humanin consistently associates with cognitive aging, coronary endothelial dysfunction, impaired fasting glucose, and cardiovascular risk in independent human cohort studies.

Against this mechanistic richness and biomarker signal, a clear-eyed evidence accounting requires:

1. **No human therapeutic evidence exists.** Every efficacy claim derives from the HNG or HNGF6A analog in rodent or porcine models. No Phase 1, 2, or 3 trial of exogenous humanin or any humanin analog in humans has been published. The translational gap is entirely unbridged.

2. **The dominant preclinical evidence is for the HNG analog, not native humanin.** HNG is ~1,000-fold more potent and has been studied across a range of models — but it is not the same compound as what consumer vendors sell as "humanin." Analog-sourced efficacy data should not be attributed to native humanin.

3. **The longevity association is correlative, not causal.** Centenarian offspring have higher humanin; long-lived GH/IGF-1-deficient mouse models have higher humanin; naked mole rats do not show the age-related decline. None of these establish that pharmacologically raising humanin extends lifespan — no mammalian lifespan-extension experiment exists for humanin.

4. **A real pro-tumor counter-signal exists and cannot be dismissed.** Two independent preclinical studies in two different tumor types (TNBC, GBM) and through two different signaling mechanisms document that exogenous humanin promotes tumor growth, metastasis, invasion, and chemotherapy resistance. The anti-apoptotic mechanism that makes humanin neuroprotective is precisely the mechanism that could protect malignant cells. Active or recent malignancy is a precautionary contraindication.

5. **Research concentration is below the 70% flag, but the HNG tool itself is nexus-derived.** Combined Keio + USC (~55–65%) leaves meaningful room for independent breadth — cardioprotection (Muzumdar/Pittsburgh, Sharp/LSU), ophthalmic biology (UC Irvine/Doheny), independent Chinese and European groups — unlike MOTS-c's near-total USC concentration. The caveat is that independent groups are largely applying the HNG tool introduced by the nexus labs.

6. **Regulatory, quality-assurance, and safety environments are unfavorable.** FDA-unapproved, no 503A compounding pathway, WADA S0-prohibited, grey-market only, no human PK data, no established dose.

**What a thoughtful researcher or clinician would take from this entry:** Humanin is a foundational MDP with the most developed mechanistic literature in its class — it is not a hyped newcomer with a single-lab preclinical dataset. The human biomarker signal is real and replicated across independent cohort studies. However, the specific jump from "endogenous humanin tracks health outcomes" to "exogenous humanin supplementation confers those outcomes" has zero human interventional support. The pro-tumor counter-signal is the single most clinically load-bearing fact in this entry: the same mechanism that makes humanin attractive for neuroprotection makes it potentially dangerous in any context with malignant cells present. No currently available evidence base supports the clinical use of exogenous humanin in humans.
