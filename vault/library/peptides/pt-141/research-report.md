# PT-141 (Bremelanotide / Vyleesi)

## Metadata

- **class:** peptide (synthetic cyclic melanocortin-receptor agonist; des-amide structural analog of Melanotan-II; FDA-approved as Vyleesi)
- **evidence_tier:** B — Two replicated Phase 3 RCTs (RECONNECT, n=1,247 premenopausal women with HSDD); both co-primary endpoints met at p<.001; FDA approved June 2019. Effect is real but honestly modest: placebo-adjusted FSFI desire domain +0.35 (Cohen's d ~0.27–0.39). Single-sponsor (100% Palatin/AMAG); no independent Phase 3 replication; narrow approved population only; no Phase 3 RCT in men; the sole large male RCT carries an active Expression of Concern. (The approval is genuine and the trials are replicated, but single-sponsor concentration, narrow population, modest effect, and no independent corroboration keep this at B rather than A — matching the compound page's adjudicated tier.)
- **risk_tier:** medium — Approved for premenopausal women only; contraindicated in uncontrolled HTN and known CVD. Dominant AE: nausea ~40% (discontinuation driver, ~8–20% in trials). Focal hyperpigmentation (face/gingiva/breasts): 1% at label dose, 38% with daily dosing; resolution after discontinuation not confirmed in all patients. Transient BP rise ~6/3 mmHg; resolves within 12 h. (Adverse events are characterized, CV contraindication has a defined screening threshold, and label dosing limits hyperpigmentation burden — but irreversibility uncertainty, CV gap in grey-market use, and off-label-population safety data void prevent low.)
- **status:** researched
- **last_verified:** 2026-06-21

## Summary

PT-141 (research designation), also known as bremelanotide (INN) and marketed as **Vyleesi** (Palatin Technologies / AMAG Pharmaceuticals / Cosette Pharmaceuticals), is a synthetic cyclic heptapeptide lactam with the sequence **Ac-Nle-cyclo[Asp-His-D-Phe-Arg-Trp-Lys]-OH**, molecular weight 1025.2 Da, CAS 189691-06-3 [1, regulatory]. It is the **C-terminal free-acid (des-amide) structural analog of Melanotan-II (MT-II)** — the parent cyclic heptapeptide that terminates in a primary amide (–NH₂), whereas bremelanotide terminates in a free carboxylic acid (–OH) [1, regulatory; 2, regulatory]. This single C-terminal modification accounts for meaningful differences in receptor profile and metabolic handling between the two compounds, though both are non-selective melanocortin agonists.

The central honest tension in the PT-141 literature is the gap between what bremelanotide actually is — a **genuinely FDA-approved drug for a narrow indication, with a modest effect size, a significant nausea burden, and a single-sponsor evidence base** — and how "PT-141" is positioned in grey-market channels: as a broad-spectrum libido and erectile booster suitable for all sexes and all purposes. That gap is large and load-bearing for any honest assessment of the compound.

**What bremelanotide genuinely is.** PT-141/Vyleesi received FDA approval on **June 21, 2019** (NDA 210557) for the treatment of **acquired, generalized hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD) in premenopausal women** — women whose low sexual desire causes marked distress or interpersonal difficulty and is not attributable to a coexisting medical or psychiatric condition, relationship problems, or medication effects [1, regulatory]. This is the only approved indication. The label explicitly states: "Not for postmenopausal women, men, or to enhance sexual performance" [1, regulatory]. The approval rests on two identically designed Phase 3 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials (RECONNECT Study 301 and Study 302) enrolling a combined 1,247 premenopausal women with HSDD [7, rct]. Both trials met both co-primary endpoints. The compound is the only FDA-approved pharmacotherapy targeting central sexual desire pathways in premenopausal women as of last review.

**The honest effect size.** The approval is real, but the effect is modest. Placebo-adjusted improvement in the Female Sexual Function Index desire domain (FSFI-D, 0–6 scale) was **+0.35** across the integrated RECONNECT population, with Cohen's d approximately 0.27–0.39 [9, rct]. Nausea occurred in **~40%** of treated patients versus ~1.3% of placebo recipients [1, regulatory]; it was the primary discontinuation driver, with roughly 8% discontinuing during the blinded phase and ~20% during the 52-week open-label extension [8, open_label]. The responder rate was approximately 58% vs. 36% placebo — a real but substantially placebo-inflated separation [8, open_label].

**PT-141 is not cleanly MC4R-selective — this is a load-bearing point.** Grey-market vendor literature frequently describes PT-141 as "100-fold MC4R-selective" over MC1R. The FDA-approved prescribing information documents the opposite potency order: **MC1R > MC4R > MC3R > MC5R > MC2R** [1, regulatory]. Residual MC1R activity is not a minor footnote — it is the mechanistic basis for two clinically significant adverse effects: the nausea and flushing cluster, and the focal hyperpigmentation signal (face, gingiva, breasts). The claim of robust MC4R selectivity is simply not supported by the regulatory evidence.

**The three-compound disambiguation.** MT-II, PT-141/bremelanotide, and afamelanotide are three distinct compounds sharing a research lineage but differing in structure, receptor profile, and approval status, and they must not be conflated:

- **MT-II** (Melanotan-II; Ac-Nle-cyclo[Asp-His-D-Phe-Arg-Trp-Lys]-**NH₂**) is the unapproved parent compound with a C-terminal primary amide. MT-II has a broader and more potent receptor engagement, including prominent MC1R-mediated tanning and stronger nausea/yawning burden. It is not approved anywhere [2, regulatory].
- **PT-141 / bremelanotide / Vyleesi** (Ac-Nle-cyclo[Asp-His-D-Phe-Arg-Trp-Lys]-**OH**) is the des-amide structural analog of MT-II, with a C-terminal free acid, independently developed by Palatin Technologies. FDA-approved June 2019 for HSDD in premenopausal women [1, regulatory].
- **Afamelanotide** (Melanotan-I; Scenesse; Clinuvel) is a structurally unrelated **linear 13-amino-acid** α-MSH analog — not a cyclic heptapeptide — that is preferentially MC1R-agonist and approved by FDA (October 2019) and EMA (December 2014) for erythropoietic protoporphyria (EPP), a photosensitivity disorder [4, rct]. Afamelanotide does not carry the MC4R sexual-arousal pharmacology of bremelanotide.

**Off-label male use — the evidence gap is substantial.** No Phase 3 RCT of bremelanotide in men has ever been completed. The pre-approval male evidence consists of three small Phase I/II trials (n not confirmed in accessible sources; publications do not state enrollment in available abstracts) from 2004–2005 using intranasal and subcutaneous formulations that were ultimately abandoned [13, rct; 14, rct; 15, rct]. The only reasonably large male RCT — Safarinejad & Hosseini 2008, n=342, *Journal of Urology* — carries an **active Expression of Concern issued January 10, 2023** (PMID 36626345) citing data-integrity concerns; a companion Safarinejad paper in women was fully retracted for the same class of problems [12, rct]. The honest characterization of male evidence is: modest signal in early-phase trials, no Phase 3 data, no regulatory approval, and the only large trial under active integrity scrutiny.

**The well-characterized adverse-event profile.** The AE landscape is defined by four concerns, all prominently labeled:

1. **Nausea ~40%**: the dominant AE, mild-to-moderate in most cases but the primary reason for discontinuation. Antiemetic pretreatment required in ~13% of patients. Incidence highest with first dose; partial attenuation with repeat dosing [1, regulatory; 8, open_label].
2. **Focal hyperpigmentation**: face, gingiva, and breasts. Incidence 1% at the label regimen (≤8 doses/month); rises dramatically to 38% with daily dosing over 8 consecutive days [1, regulatory]. Mechanism is MC1R agonism of melanocytes. **Not confirmed reversible after discontinuation** — the label explicitly states resolution cannot be assumed [1, regulatory].
3. **Transient blood pressure elevation**: mean +6 mmHg systolic / +3 mmHg diastolic, peaking 2–4 hours post-dose, resolving within 8–12 hours [1, regulatory]. Basis for the hard contraindication in uncontrolled hypertension and known cardiovascular disease. Men who use PT-141 off-label for erectile dysfunction are precisely the demographic in which these conditions are more prevalent.
4. **Injection site reactions**: 13.2% with bremelanotide vs. 8.4% with placebo [1, regulatory].

**WADA status — not confirmed.** Direct fetch of the 2026 WADA Prohibited List PDF did not confirm bremelanotide by name. The compound is likely to be captured under S2 catch-all language covering substances with "similar chemical structure or similar biological effect(s)" to named melanocortin peptides, but this has not been verified from the primary list document. Athletes subject to anti-doping testing must consult GlobalDRO.com or their National Anti-Doping Organisation (NADO) before using any melanocortin compound, including bremelanotide. The label indication (HSDD in premenopausal women) does not confer automatic anti-doping exemption [22, regulatory].

**Sponsor concentration — 100% for Phase 3 data.** The entire Phase 3 registration-quality efficacy evidence derives from the Palatin/AMAG-sponsored RECONNECT program. No independent (non-sponsor) Phase 3 replication of bremelanotide HSDD efficacy has been conducted. Multiple RECONNECT co-authors are disclosed as AMAG employees and stockholders (Williams, Krop) or as Palatin VP with direct involvement in bremelanotide development (Jordan) [7, rct; 8, open_label]. This is structurally normal for a single-company FDA approval and does not invalidate the effect signal, but it means the published effect sizes carry zero external Phase 3 corroboration.

**The commercial arc underscores the gap between expectation and reality.** Vyleesi launched in September 2019 with peak-sales projections of $810M–$1B; within 13 months, AMAG paid Palatin $16.3M to exit the North American license [26, vendor_label]. Palatin direct-commercialized the drug until December 2023, generating gross revenues of <$6M per year. Palatin then sold Vyleesi to Cosette Pharmaceuticals for $12M upfront plus milestones contingent on reaching annual net sales of $15M–$200M — milestone thresholds that imply sales were well below $15M at time of sale [27, vendor_label]. The compound is genuinely approved, modestly effective in a narrow indication, commercially marginal, and widely used off-label in a grey market that bears essentially no relationship to the evidence base.

## 1. Identity, Mechanism, and the Three-Compound Disambiguation

### 1.1 Chemical Identity and Structure

PT-141 (bremelanotide/Vyleesi) is a synthetic cyclic heptapeptide lactam with the full sequence Ac-Nle-cyclo[Asp-His-D-Phe-Arg-Trp-Lys]-OH, where the ring is formed by a lactam bridge between the aspartic acid side-chain carboxyl and the lysine side-chain amine [1, regulatory]. Molecular formula: C₅₀H₆₈N₁₄O₁₀ (acetate salt: C₅₀H₆₈N₁₄O₁₀ · xCH₃COOH, 1 ≤ x ≤ 2); molecular weight 1025.2 Da (free base); CAS 189691-06-3; PubChem CID 9941379 [1, regulatory; 29, regulatory].

The compound is the direct C-terminal des-amide analog of Melanotan-II (MT-II; PubChem CID 92432; molecular formula C₅₀H₆₉N₁₅O₉; MW ~1024.2 Da; C-terminal primary amide –NH₂) [2, regulatory]. The single structural difference — the replacement of –NH₂ with –OH at the C-terminus — accounts for differences in receptor selectivity profile and metabolic handling while preserving the core cyclic heptapeptide pharmacophore. The FDA label describes bremelanotide as having "a free acid at the carboxyl terminus" [1, regulatory], and the peer-reviewed mechanism literature characterizes it as "the carboxylate derivative of MT-II" [5, mechanism_review].

The key structural features relative to endogenous α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH) — a linear 13-amino-acid peptide — are: (a) substitution of norleucine (Nle) for methionine at position 4, improving oxidative stability; (b) incorporation of D-phenylalanine in place of L-phenylalanine at position 7, contributing the key binding-pharmacophore contact; and (c) the lactam cyclization, which constrains the compound into a bioactive β-turn conformation that is only transiently adopted by linear endogenous peptide, conferring substantially greater potency and proteolytic resistance. These are the same structural innovations as MT-II — bremelanotide differs from MT-II at C-terminus only.

### 1.2 The Three-Compound Disambiguation in Detail

Three compounds sharing the "Melanotan" or "PT-141" consumer namespace require precise distinction:

| Compound | Sequence | MW | C-terminus | Key receptors | Approval |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Melanotan-I (afamelanotide; Scenesse) | Linear 13-residue [Nle⁴,D-Phe⁷]-α-MSH | 1646.8 Da | Free acid | MC1R preferential | EU 2014; FDA 2019 (EPP) |
| Melanotan-II (MT-II) | Cyclic lactam heptapeptide | 1024.2 Da | –NH₂ (amide) | MC1R, MC3R, MC4R, MC5R (non-selective) | Not approved anywhere |
| PT-141 (bremelanotide; Vyleesi) | Cyclic heptapeptide (same ring as MT-II) | 1025.2 Da | –OH (des-amide) | MC1R (highest affinity), MC4R, MC3R, MC5R | FDA June 21, 2019 (HSDD, premenopausal women) |

**Afamelanotide** is a structurally distinct linear 13-amino-acid analog — it shares no cyclic scaffold with either MT-II or bremelanotide. Its MC1R preferential activity was specifically engineered to minimize CNS MC4R-driven sexual and neurological effects. The approved clinical program (EPP) is entirely separate from the bremelanotide HSDD program [4, rct]. Afamelanotide data does not generalize to bremelanotide, and vice versa.

**MT-II** is the unapproved research parent from which bremelanotide was developed. MT-II's broader receptor engagement (stronger non-selective MC1R activity) produces more prominent tanning, more pronounced nausea/yawning/stretching, and spontaneous (non-stimulation-dependent) erections in men — the hallmark MT-II phenomenology. Bremelanotide was specifically developed to reduce non-target effects while preserving the MC4R-driven sexual desire and arousal signal, though it did not achieve clean MC4R selectivity [1, regulatory; 5, mechanism_review]. MT-II evidence does not extend to bremelanotide: different receptor profile, different formulation, different clinical program.

**Bremelanotide** is the approved member of this class. Its FDA approval belongs to its own regulatory program (Palatin Technologies/AMAG Pharmaceuticals RECONNECT Phase 3 trials), developed from the MT-II cyclic scaffold via Palatin's license from the University of Arizona's commercialization partner Competitive Technologies [25, vendor_label]. The approval cannot be attributed to MT-II, nor can MT-II clinical data be cited as bremelanotide efficacy evidence.

Grey-market products labeled "PT-141" may contain bremelanotide, MT-II, structurally adjacent impurities, or a different compound entirely. This identity uncertainty is a primary safety concern of unregulated purchase.

### 1.3 Receptor Pharmacology: The MC1R > MC4R Potency Order

Bremelanotide is a melanocortin receptor (MCR) agonist with an in-vitro potency order at human receptors of **MC1R > MC4R > MC3R > MC5R > MC2R** per the FDA-approved prescribing information [1, regulatory]. This is the established, label-documented potency hierarchy. The grey-market claim that PT-141 is "100-fold MC4R selective over MC1R" is directly contradicted by the FDA label and is not supported by the peer-reviewed receptor-binding literature. The actual selectivity differences between MT-II and bremelanotide are modest and population-model-dependent — a shift in relative affinity, not the clean selective redesign that vendor literature implies [5, mechanism_review].

Understanding why the MC1R > MC4R potency order matters requires tracing what each receptor actually does. The five melanocortin receptor subtypes are G-protein-coupled receptors with distinct expression patterns and physiological roles. **MC1R** is expressed predominantly on cutaneous melanocytes and is the molecular gatekeeper of skin pigmentation: α-MSH binding activates adenylate cyclase, driving cAMP/PKA signaling that upregulates the transcription factor MITF and its downstream targets — tyrosinase, TYRP1, and DCT — resulting in the production of photoprotective eumelanin (brown-black) over phaeomelanin (red-yellow, photosensitizing). MC1R is also a major melanoma susceptibility locus in humans, and its pharmacological agonism by a systemically circulating peptide (rather than locally via UV-induced keratinocyte paracrine signaling) bypasses the normal homeostatic feedback that limits melanocyte activation under physiological conditions [3, mechanism_review]. **MC4R** is expressed in hypothalamic and limbic nuclei including the paraventricular nucleus, arcuate nucleus, medial preoptic area, nucleus accumbens, amygdala, and ventral tegmental area — regions centrally involved in reward, arousal, and motivated sexual behavior. MC4R agonism in these circuits modulates dopaminergic tone to the nucleus accumbens, providing a neurochemical substrate for increased sexual desire and motivation. **MC3R**, expressed in hypothalamus and gut, participates in energy homeostasis and feeding regulation; its involvement in the nausea cluster remains incompletely separated from MC4R contributions. **MC2R** is exclusively activated by ACTH rather than MSH peptides and mediates adrenocortical steroidogenesis; bremelanotide does not activate MC2R at therapeutic concentrations. **MC5R** is expressed in exocrine glands and peripheral tissues; its human pharmacology remains incompletely characterized.

The practical consequence of MC1R ranking first in binding affinity is that every dose of bremelanotide — including the approved 1.75 mg SC dose — activates MC1R-expressing melanocytes across the body simultaneously and pharmacologically, without the UV-gated, keratinocyte-paracrine regulation that governs normal melanocyte activation. This is the mechanistic basis for the hyperpigmentation adverse event. It also means that the Palatin development program's original goal — designing a compound with enough MC4R engagement for sexual desire benefit but minimal MC1R-driven tanning — was not fully achieved at the receptor-binding level. What was achieved, pragmatically, was a formulation and dosing schedule (1.75 mg on-demand, ≤8 doses/month) that limits the cumulative MC1R activation burden enough to produce hyperpigmentation in only ~1% of patients at the label regimen, while accepting a much higher hyperpigmentation rate (38%) with more frequent dosing [1, regulatory].

The clinically relevant pharmacodynamic effects at therapeutic doses are attributed primarily to:

- **MC4R** (sexual arousal and desire): MC4R is highly expressed in hypothalamic nuclei (paraventricular nucleus, arcuate nucleus), limbic structures (medial preoptic area, nucleus accumbens, amygdala), and the ventral tegmental area [3, mechanism_review]. Activation of presynaptic MC4R in these regions modulates dopaminergic release to the nucleus accumbens, providing the neurochemical substrate for increased sexual desire and arousal. This CNS mechanism is fundamentally distinct from the peripheral vascular (NO/cGMP/PDE5) mechanism of PDE5 inhibitors; early male trials showed bremelanotide and PDE5 inhibitors can be additive [5, mechanism_review].
- **MC1R** (melanocyte activation): Residual MC1R agonism at Vyleesi doses underlies the nausea, flushing, and focal hyperpigmentation adverse effects. The MC1R activity is not incidental — it is the mechanistic driver of two of the most clinically significant adverse effects in the label.

The FDA label explicitly states that "the mechanism by which VYLEESI improves HSDD in women is unknown" [1, regulatory] — the MC4R hypothesis is supported by animal and in-vitro evidence but has not been directly demonstrated by human mechanistic studies. Animal data from rodent mating models established MC4R as "the principal effector of MC-induced erection," with MC3R involvement unresolved [5, mechanism_review]. In vitro, bremelanotide's Ki values at cloned human receptors show meaningful engagement at all four receptor subtypes (MC1, MC3, MC4, MC5) with sub-nanomolar to low-nanomolar affinity — not selective engagement at MC4R alone.

### 1.4 Pharmacokinetics: The On-Demand Architecture

The pharmacokinetic profile of bremelanotide is well-characterized from the FDA label [1, regulatory] and supporting review literature [3, mechanism_review]:

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Route | Subcutaneous injection (abdomen or thigh) |
| Bioavailability (SC) | ~100% |
| T_max | ~1.0 h (range 0.5–1.0 h) |
| C_max | 72.8 ng/mL |
| Elimination half-life (t½) | ~2.7 h (range 1.9–4.0 h) |
| AUC | 276 h·ng/mL |
| Protein binding | 21% |
| Clearance | 6.5 ± 1.0 L/h |
| Metabolism | Hydrolysis of peptide amide bonds; cellular peptidases |
| Excretion | ~64.8% urine; ~22.8% feces |

The short half-life (~2.7 h), fast T_max (~1 h), and near-complete SC bioavailability support the approved **on-demand dosing model**: a single 1.75 mg SC injection self-administered at least 45 minutes before anticipated sexual activity, not more than once per 24 hours [1, regulatory]. There is no approved chronic or daily dosing regimen. The on-demand design reflects both the transient CNS activation required for the intended pharmacodynamic effect and the AE burden (nausea, blood pressure elevation) that resolves within 8–12 hours post-dose. Blood pressure peaks at 2–4 hours post-dose and returns to baseline within 12 hours [1, regulatory; 8, open_label].

## 2. The RECONNECT Phase 3 Program: FDA Approval Basis and Honest Effect-Size Assessment

### 2.1 Trial Design and Population

The RECONNECT program comprised two identically designed Phase 3 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter trials (Study 301 and Study 302) enrolling a combined safety population of 1,247 premenopausal women with acquired, generalized HSDD; the efficacy-evaluable population was 1,202. Participants were predominantly US-based (96.6%), predominantly white (85.6%), with a mean age of 39 years. Both trials ran 24 weeks, with bremelanotide 1.75 mg self-administered subcutaneously on an as-needed basis (not more than once in 24 hours) before anticipated sexual activity [7, rct].

The diagnosis of HSDD was physician-confirmed and required that the low desire cause marked distress or interpersonal difficulty, be not better explained by another medical or psychiatric condition, relationship factors, or medication. This is a specific, well-defined clinical population — not a general population of women desiring increased libido.

### 2.2 Co-Primary Endpoints: Real Effect, Honestly Modest Magnitude

The co-primary endpoints were (1) change from baseline in the Female Sexual Function Index desire domain (FSFI-D; 0–6 scale) and (2) change from baseline in item 13 of the Female Sexual Distress Scale — Desire/Arousal/Orgasm (FSDS-DAO; 0–4 scale), capturing distress specifically related to low desire. Both studies met both co-primary endpoints [7, rct].

The placebo-adjusted treatment effects, while statistically robust, were modest in absolute magnitude:

| Outcome | Study 301 | Study 302 | Integrated |
|---|---|---|---|
| FSFI-D change vs. placebo | +0.30 (P<.001) | +0.42 (P<.001) | +0.35 (P<.001) |
| FSDS-DAO item 13 change vs. placebo | −0.37 (P<.001) | −0.29 (P=.005) | −0.33 (P<.001) |

Cohen's d effect sizes for the integrated RECONNECT data were approximately **0.39 for the desire domain and 0.27 for the distress item** — small-to-medium by convention [9, rct]. These figures are not cherry-picked by critics; they are reported and contextualized by the trial authors themselves. A placebo-adjusted improvement of 0.35 points on a 6-point desire scale is a real treatment effect — the trials were adequately powered and the separation was consistent across both trials — but it is at the lower end of clinical meaningfulness by most standards, and the high absolute placebo response rate (~36% responder rate in placebo arms) reflects the sensitivity of subjective sexual-desire instruments to expectation, attention, and trial-engagement effects [8, open_label].

In the 52-week open-label extension, participants who had been on bremelanotide throughout showed larger cumulative FSFI-D improvements (+1.25 to +1.30 on the desire subscale; −1.4 to −1.7 on the distress item) exceeding the pre-specified minimal clinically important difference threshold [8, open_label]. These open-label data must be interpreted with caution — they lack placebo control, have high attrition, and likely reflect survivor bias toward satisfied users.

### 2.3 Tolerability and Discontinuation

Nausea is the dominant adverse effect and the primary driver of trial discontinuation. In the pooled RECONNECT AE population (n=627 bremelanotide, n=620 placebo per the FDA label AE table [1, regulatory]; note these arm-level counts sum to 1,247, the safety population — the DailyMed label uses these arm sizes as the denominator for all AE rate calculations), nausea occurred in **40.0% vs. 1.3%** [1, regulatory]. Flushing occurred in 20.3% vs. 0.3%; headache in 11.3% vs. 1.9%. These rates were confirmed and slightly lower in the 52-week open-label extension (40.4% nausea incidence overall) [8, open_label].

Approximately 13% of bremelanotide-treated patients required antiemetic medication [1, regulatory]. Discontinuation due to adverse effects — primarily nausea — occurred in approximately 8% during the double-blind phase and in 18.7–23.4% across the two arms of the 52-week extension [8, open_label]. Nausea was the only severe treatment-emergent adverse event experienced by more than one participant in the extension study. Nausea incidence is highest with the first dose, partly attenuates with repeated dosing, but does not consistently resolve — 40.4% incidence persisted through the extension period.

The blood pressure effect, addressed in a dedicated ambulatory monitoring substudy in n=397 premenopausal women with female sexual dysfunction, showed a mean systolic increase of approximately 3.1/3.2 mmHg at the approved 1.75 mg SC dose, typically lasting less than 15 minutes [11, rct]. The FDA label documents the maximum mean increase as approximately 6 mmHg systolic and 3 mmHg diastolic, peaking at 2–4 hours, accompanied by a compensatory heart rate decrease of up to 5 bpm [1, regulatory].

### 2.4 Responder Analysis and the Placebo Context

A meaningful-response threshold analysis yielded responder rates of approximately **58% bremelanotide vs. 36% placebo** in the integrated efficacy-evaluable population [8, open_label]. The responder-threshold methodology was established in a Phase 2b dose-ranging study that preceded RECONNECT, which identified the clinically meaningful response criteria applied to the Phase 3 population [10, rct]. This is real treatment signal above placebo, but the placebo responder rate — 36% — is substantial and reflects the well-documented susceptibility of subjective desire measures to trial-design effects, patient-expectation activation, and increased attention to one's sexual experiences during trial participation. Trial authors explicitly noted the high placebo rates and cautioned that design effects may have inflated the apparent treatment benefit [8, open_label].

**Honest tier assessment.** PT-141/Vyleesi holds the highest evidence tier of any sexual peptide discussed in this wiki — two replicated Phase 3 RCTs in a top obstetrics/gynecology journal, supporting a genuine regulatory approval. The effect is real and statistically robust across both trials. The absolute treatment-versus-placebo separation is modest; nausea-driven discontinuation is substantial; and the approved population is narrow. Any use in men, or in women without an HSDD diagnosis, is off-label and unsupported by registration-quality evidence.

## 3. Off-Label Male Use, the Grey-Market Reality, and Melanocortin-Class Context

### 3.1 Off-Label Male Use: What the Evidence Actually Shows

The FDA label explicitly excludes male use: "Not for postmenopausal women, men, or to enhance sexual performance" [1, regulatory]. Male use is therefore entirely off-label, supported only by pre-approval development-era trials that were never carried to a Phase 3 NDA-enabling stage for this population.

The male evidence consists of three small early-phase trials published between 2004 and 2005:

**Diamond et al. 2004 (intranasal PT-141, PMID 14963471)** [13, rct] — Double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase I trial in healthy males and mild-to-moderate ED patients. Intranasal PT-141 at doses >7 mg produced statistically significant RigiScan-measured erectile responses versus placebo, with onset at approximately 30 minutes. Flushing and nausea were the most common adverse events; no clinically significant vital-sign changes were reported at doses tested. Published in *Int J Impot Res* (tier 2).

**Rosen, Diamond et al. 2004 (subcutaneous PT-141, PMID 14999221)** [14, rct] — Phase I/II randomized crossover trial in healthy male subjects and ED patients with inadequate response to 100 mg sildenafil. Subcutaneous PT-141 produced significant erectile responses at doses >1.0 mg in healthy subjects and at 4–6 mg in the sildenafil-failure cohort. Published in *Int J Impot Res* (tier 2).

**Diamond et al. 2005 (intranasal PT-141 + sildenafil, PMID 15833522)** [15, rct] — Randomized crossover trial, n=19 men with ED responsive to sildenafil or vardenafil. Three arms: 25 mg sildenafil + 7.5 mg intranasal PT-141 vs. sildenafil + placebo vs. double-placebo. The combination produced significantly greater RigiScan erectile response than sildenafil alone, with no new or increased-frequency adverse events. Published in *Urology* (tier 2).

None of these trials reports enrollment size in their available abstracts (both 2004 Int J Impot Res papers are paywalled; Unpaywall confirms no open-access versions). They are Phase I/II proofs of concept — none was designed or powered for registration.

**The Safarinejad trial and the integrity concern.** The only reasonably large male RCT is Safarinejad & Hosseini 2008 (*J Urol*; PMID 18206919), a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study in n=342 married men aged 28–59 with sildenafil-unresponsive ED, reporting a 33.5% positive response rate versus 8.5% placebo (p=.03) [12, rct]. The *Journal of Urology* issued a formal **Expression of Concern on January 10, 2023** (PMID 36626345) for this paper, citing data-integrity concerns identified by independent statisticians. A companion Safarinejad paper on bremelanotide in women (*J Sex Med* 2008, PMID 18179455) was **fully retracted** for the same class of concerns — authorial non-response to reviewer requests about methodology and data integrity. The J Urol male trial therefore carries a cloud of uncertainty and should not be treated as clean positive evidence [12, rct].

**Net assessment for males.** The pre-approval male trials are small (Phase I/II), used intranasal or subcutaneous formulations that were ultimately abandoned, were published before the pivotal female program began, and were not taken forward to Phase III for men. No RCT of bremelanotide in men reaches the evidentiary standard of the approved female indication. The sole reasonably large male RCT is under active integrity scrutiny. The honest characterization: modest short-term signal in early-phase male ED trials, no Phase III data in men, no regulatory approval, and the Safarinejad data unreliable pending investigation resolution.

### 3.2 The Intranasal Program: Why It Was Abandoned

Before the subcutaneous HSDD program succeeded, bremelanotide was investigated as an intranasal spray (10 mg) for erectile dysfunction in men who were non-responsive to PDE5 inhibitors. The intranasal formulation was abandoned not primarily because of failed efficacy but because of blood-pressure safety concerns and pharmacokinetic variability. The FDA halted the intranasal development program in 2007. Wide bioavailability variability with intranasal delivery exposed some patients to excessive plasma concentrations with heightened cardiovascular risk. The subcutaneous route provided tighter dose-exposure relationships, and the small-but-transient blood pressure elevations at the approved 1.75 mg SC dose were deemed acceptable for the approved indication with appropriate contraindications [11, rct; 1, regulatory].

### 3.3 The Grey-Market Reality

Despite the narrow approved indication (premenopausal HSDD, women only), PT-141 circulates widely in grey-market channels as a libido and erectile booster for both sexes — sold as research-chemical injectable vials, nasal sprays, and lyophilized powders through online peptide vendors. The gap between label and market is substantial:

- **Population mismatch**: approved for premenopausal women with physician-confirmed HSDD; marketed grey-market to men, postmenopausal women, and healthy individuals without any diagnosed dysfunction.
- **Formulation and purity**: Vyleesi is a pharmaceutical-grade 1.75 mg/0.3 mL subcutaneous autoinjector with controlled excipients and sterility validation. Grey-market peptides carry no such validation. Evans-Brown et al. 2009 documented this exact problem for the parent compound MT-II: unregulated products obtained online had no quality assurance, users reported adverse events including nausea, hypertension, and unintended skin darkening, and compound identity was unverifiable [16, mechanism_review]. The same structural issue applies to grey-market PT-141 — no pharmacopoeial standard, no batch release testing, no sterility guarantee.
- **Compound-identity hazard**: MT-II and PT-141 are frequently conflated by vendors and buyers. A vial labeled "PT-141" from an unregulated source may contain MT-II (which carries stronger tanning activity, broader receptor non-selectivity, and potentially a different blood pressure profile), a structurally adjacent impurity, or a different compound entirely.
- **Dosing without monitoring**: the label restricts Vyleesi to ≤1 dose per 24 hours and ≤8 doses per month, with contraindications in uncontrolled hypertension and known CVD. Grey-market use occurs at the user's discretion with no clinical monitoring, no cardiovascular pre-screening, and frequently in combination with PDE5 inhibitors.
- **Efficacy misrepresentation**: the approved evidence supports only modest effect on specific HSDD outcome measures in premenopausal women. Grey-market marketing positions PT-141 as a potent broad-spectrum libido booster with near-universal effect — a representation unsupported by any regulatory-quality evidence in the claimed populations.

A 2020 FDA warning letter to Tailor Made Compounding LLC cited bremelanotide among substances compounded without meeting 503A conditions [30, regulatory]. Under FDA's "essentially a copy" doctrine, a 503A pharmacy may compound bremelanotide only if a prescriber documents a significant clinical difference for an individual patient (e.g., a different route) — the commercially available autoinjector formulation already exists [23, regulatory].

### 3.4 The Melanocortin-Sexual Class: MT-II, PT-141, and Kisspeptin Distinguished

PT-141/bremelanotide is one of three distinct pharmacological approaches to centrally mediated sexual function that appear in the peptide literature. Distinguishing their mechanisms prevents evidence from one compound or pathway being incorrectly attributed to another.

**Melanotan-II — the non-selective parent.** MT-II demonstrated the melanocortin erectogenic proof-of-concept in men: in three double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover RCTs at the University of Arizona (n=10, 20, and 10 in psychogenic and organic ED cohorts), subcutaneous MT-II at 0.025 mg/kg induced clinically measurable penile erections in 80–85% of participants, with mean tip rigidity >80% lasting 38–45 minutes versus <5 minutes for placebo [18, rct; 19, rct]. The adverse event profile with MT-II was more prominent than bremelanotide's: severe nausea in ~12.9% of administrations, frequent yawning and stretching (a hallmark MC receptor effect), and spontaneous erections without sexual stimulation. MT-II's broader receptor engagement accounts for its more pronounced nausea/yawning burden and its tanning effect. PT-141 was developed specifically to reduce non-target effects while preserving the central erectogenic/arousal signal, though it still carries nausea and flushing as common adverse events and retains meaningful MC1R activity at label doses [17, mechanism_review]. MT-II evidence should not be cited as PT-141 evidence — different receptor profile, different adverse event burden, different formulation, no Phase 3 for either in men.

**Kisspeptin — a different mechanism, different pathway.** Kisspeptin is a neuropeptide that acts as the upstream orchestrator of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, driving GnRH release from the hypothalamus and modulating limbic circuits implicated in sexual behavior, mood, and emotional-behavioral responses to sexual cues [20, mechanism_review]. The mechanistic distinction from melanocortin agonists is fundamental: PT-141/MT-II act at MC3R/MC4R receptors on hypothalamic neurons to produce rapid acute arousal and erectile responses independent of the HPG hormonal axis; kisspeptin acts upstream of GnRH to regulate the hormonal cascade that sets tonic sexual drive and reproductive capacity. The two pathways converge on sexual behavior via the hypothalamus through entirely different receptor families, second-messenger cascades, and time courses — kisspeptin effects are hormonal/axis-level; PT-141 effects are acute central arousal. Kisspeptin does not directly agonize melanocortin receptors. Evidence on kisspeptin's role in human sexual behavior should not be conflated with the melanocortin literature, and vice versa.

**Attribution rule (critical):** when citing erectogenic or libido effects, evidence must be attributed to the specific compound tested. MT-II evidence does not generalize to PT-141; kisspeptin evidence does not generalize to either melanocortin agonist.

## 4. Safety, Dosing, and Clinical Monitoring

### 4.1 Approved Dosing and Route

The FDA-approved Vyleesi dosing regimen [1, regulatory]:

- **Dose**: 1.75 mg subcutaneously via single-use autoinjector
- **Route**: Subcutaneous self-injection, abdomen or thigh
- **Timing**: At least 45 minutes before anticipated sexual activity (on-demand; not daily)
- **Frequency cap**: No more than one dose within any 24-hour period
- **Monthly cap**: Maximum 8 doses per month
- **Response assessment**: Discontinue after 8 weeks if no symptom improvement

The 45-minute pre-activity window aligns with the T_max of ~1 hour; the monthly cap (≤8 doses) is grounded in the blood pressure signal and the hyperpigmentation dose-dependence. There is no approved dose-adjustment strategy and no dose-titration schedule — the 1.75 mg on-demand dose is the single approved regimen.

Compounded bremelanotide via 503A pharmacies is available at substantially lower cost (~$50–$150/month estimated retail); compounded nasal and sublingual routes are used off-label. No peer-reviewed dose-finding data exist for routes other than SC. The nasal route was studied in early Phase 1/2 male ED trials but was abandoned due to pharmacokinetic variability and cardiovascular risk [11, rct; 1, regulatory].

### 4.2 The Adverse-Event Profile in Full

Data from two sources: the FDA label drawing on pooled RECONNECT Phase 3 trials (n=627 bremelanotide, n=620 placebo) [1, regulatory] and the RECONNECT 52-week open-label extension (Simon et al. 2019, PMID 31599847) [8, open_label].

**Adverse reactions at ≥2% incidence in RECONNECT** [1, regulatory]:

| Adverse reaction | Bremelanotide (n=627) | Placebo (n=620) |
|---|---|---|
| Nausea | 40.0% | 1.3% |
| Flushing | 20.3% | 0.3% |
| Injection site reactions | 13.2% | 8.4% |
| Headache | 11.3% | 1.9% |
| Vomiting | 4.8% | 0.2% |
| Cough | 3.3% | 1.3% |
| Fatigue | 3.2% | 0.5% |
| Hot flush | 2.7% | 0.2% |
| Paresthesia | 2.6% | 0.0% |
| Dizziness | 2.2% | 0.5% |
| Nasal congestion | 2.1% | 0.5% |

**Nausea — dominant AE and primary discontinuation driver.** Nausea occurred in 40.0% vs. 1.3% placebo [1, regulatory]. Temporal pattern: median onset ~1 hour; median duration ~2 hours; incidence highest with the first dose, partly attenuating with repeat use. Severity was mostly mild-to-moderate, but nausea was the only severe treatment-emergent AE experienced by more than one participant in the 52-week extension [8, open_label]. Anti-emetic pretreatment was required in approximately 13% of treated patients [1, regulatory]. Discontinuation due to nausea: ~8% during the double-blind phase; 18.7–23.4% during the extension, spanning the two study arms [8, open_label]. The 40.4% overall nausea incidence persisted through 52 weeks of extension data — this is not a self-limiting first-dose phenomenon for a substantial proportion of users.

**Focal hyperpigmentation — MC1R-mediated, potentially irreversible.** Bremelanotide's residual MC1R agonism drives melanogenesis in susceptible individuals [3, mechanism_review]:

- Incidence at the label regimen (≤8 doses/month): **1%** [1, regulatory]
- Sites: face, gingiva (gums), and breasts — primary documented locations [1, regulatory]
- Risk amplification: darker baseline skin pigmentation increases risk; daily dosing dramatically amplifies risk — **38% incidence with daily dosing over 8 consecutive days** [1, regulatory]
- Reversibility: **not confirmed reversible in all patients after discontinuation** — the label explicitly states resolution cannot be assumed [1, regulatory]
- Mechanism: MC1R agonism stimulates melanocyte cAMP/MITF/tyrosinase activity independently of solar UV exposure; the cyclic peptide structure confers persistent receptor engagement relative to endogenous α-MSH [3, mechanism_review]

The 1% incidence at label dosing versus 38% with daily dosing illustrates why off-label grey-market protocols (often involving frequent or daily dosing for nasal spray or "loading" phases) carry materially higher hyperpigmentation risk than the label regimen. The fact that resolution is not confirmed in all patients makes this the adverse event with the most durable potential harm — it is not simply a nuisance that resolves on stopping.

**Cardiovascular effects — transient BP rise, contraindication basis.**

- Systolic BP: mean maximum increase ~6 mmHg (FDA label) / ~3 mmHg (52-week extension) [1, regulatory; 8, open_label]
- Diastolic BP: mean maximum increase ~3 mmHg (FDA label) / ~2 mmHg (52-week extension) [1, regulatory; 8, open_label]
- Peak timing: 2–4 hours post-dose
- Heart rate: compensatory decrease up to ~5 bpm
- Duration: returns to baseline within 8–12 hours [1, regulatory; 8, open_label]
- Net myocardial workload: did not meaningfully increase due to the BP–HR offset [8, open_label]

The transient pressor response is the basis for the hard contraindication in uncontrolled hypertension and known cardiovascular disease [1, regulatory]. A dedicated ambulatory monitoring substudy (White WB et al. 2017, n=397 premenopausal women with FSD, normotensive or with controlled hypertension) confirmed the BP effect pattern and its transient nature [11, rct]. The public health implication for grey-market off-label use is direct: individuals self-injecting unregulated PT-141 are typically not pre-screened for hypertension or CVD, are not monitored during the 2–4 hour BP peak window, are not limited to ≤8 doses per month, and may be combining the peptide with PDE5 inhibitors. The CV safety concern is modest in magnitude within the approved, screened population but uncharacterized in the unscreened grey-market population in which hypertension and CVD are more prevalent.

A comprehensive safety meta-analysis (Clayton et al. 2022, reviewing 43 studies, ~3,500 subjects across the full clinical development program) confirmed the BP increase pattern, characterizing it as "not deemed clinically important" in the screened, normotensive trial population, but explicitly noting bremelanotide "should be used with caution in patients at risk of cardiovascular disease" [21, meta_analysis].

**Hepatotoxicity — rare.** The LiverTox entry documents one confirmed case of acute hepatitis (ALT >1,000 U/L) with resolution after discontinuation; likelihood score D (possible rare cause) [6, regulatory]. No acute liver failure or chronic hepatic injury has been documented. Liver function monitoring is not required by the label but should be considered if symptomatic.

### 4.3 Warnings, Contraindications, and Monitoring Summary

**Hard contraindications** [1, regulatory]:
1. Uncontrolled hypertension
2. Known cardiovascular disease

**Warnings** [1, regulatory]:
- Transient BP increase / HR decrease: measure BP before initiating; adhere to dosing frequency limits (1 dose/24 h; ≤8 doses/month)
- Focal hyperpigmentation: examine face, gingiva, and breasts at baseline and each follow-up visit; counsel patients with darker skin about elevated risk; advise that reversal after discontinuation is not guaranteed
- Nausea management: antiemetic pretreatment may be warranted in nausea-prone patients; the ≥45-minute pre-activity window allows nausea onset (T_max ~1 h) to partly resolve before sexual activity

**Stopping criteria** [1, regulatory]:
- No symptom improvement after 8 weeks → discontinue
- Sustained or severe BP elevation post-dose → hold and evaluate
- New or progressive hyperpigmentation → consider discontinuation; no dose-reduction strategy is established
- Symptomatic CV events (chest pain, syncope, dyspnea) → stop and evaluate urgently

**No REMS program** is required for Vyleesi [1, regulatory].

### 4.4 WADA Anti-Doping Status: Unconfirmed, Verification Required

Direct retrieval of the 2026 WADA Prohibited List PDF was not successful in the primary research fetch; the document returned empty content via WebFetch. The USADA 2026 advisory page was successfully fetched and confirmed S2 received further clarifications for 2026 but did not identify bremelanotide by name [22, regulatory]. Based on available secondary evidence: melanotan II (a non-approved melanocortin peptide) appears to be explicitly prohibited under S2. Whether bremelanotide is covered by S2 catch-all class language — "and other substances with similar chemical structure or similar biological effect(s)" — is unverified from primary source [22, regulatory].

The label indication (HSDD in premenopausal women) does not automatically confer anti-doping exemption, and the compound's pharmacological class (melanocortin peptide mimetic) places it squarely in the territory where S2 catch-all language was designed to apply. The uncertainty is not a permissive signal — it is an absence of confirmation that itself warrants verification.

## 5. Sponsor Concentration, Author COIs, Commercialization, and Sourcing Realism

### 5.1 Lineage and Platform Origin

Bremelanotide was derived from melanocortin research initiated at the University of Arizona in the 1980s, originally aimed at tanning peptides (melanotan-I and melanotan-II). The University of Arizona licensed its melanocortin technology to Competitive Technologies, Inc. (CTT), a university technology-transfer company, which in turn granted Palatin Technologies, Inc. an exclusive license to develop the MT-II lineage [25, vendor_label]. Palatin synthesized bremelanotide — the des-amide, carboxyl-terminated analog of MT-II — as a compound intended to carry reduced non-target receptor activity while preserving CNS MC4R signaling for sexual function. PT-141/bremelanotide is not an independent discovery; it is the single downstream commercialization of one licensed university platform, flowing through a single company (Palatin) to a single commercialization partner (AMAG Pharmaceuticals) and subsequently to Cosette Pharmaceuticals [27, vendor_label].

### 5.2 RECONNECT Sponsorship and Primary-Evidence Concentration

The pivotal efficacy evidence for bremelanotide rests on two replicate Phase 3 RCTs — the RECONNECT studies — enrolling a combined **1,267 randomized / 1,247 safety-population / 1,202 efficacy-evaluable (mITT) premenopausal women with HSDD** [7, rct; efetch-verified PMID 31599840]. These three population figures are distinct: "1,267" is the randomized total; "1,247" is the safety population used for AE reporting; "1,202" is the modified intent-to-treat efficacy-evaluable population. Both trials were **sponsored jointly by Palatin Technologies, Inc. and AMAG Pharmaceuticals, Inc.** [7, rct]. The funding source is stated explicitly in the primary publication; editorial support was provided by Phase Five Communications, paid by AMAG [7, rct].

**Sponsor-concentration finding: 100% of Phase 3 registration-quality evidence derives from the Palatin/AMAG-sponsored RECONNECT program.** No independent (non-Palatin, non-AMAG) Phase 3 replication of bremelanotide HSDD efficacy has been conducted. Subgroup analyses published subsequently [24, rct] carry the same author team with the same disclosed financial ties and derive from the same RECONNECT dataset. The 52-week open-label extension [8, open_label] similarly lists AMAG employee co-authors (Williams and Krop are disclosed AMAG employees and stockholders; Jordan is disclosed as Palatin Vice President) embedded in the trial design and reporting chain.

The concentration figure (0% non-sponsor Phase 3 RCT data; 100% Palatin/AMAG registration-program data) is confirmed by the absence of any non-sponsor Phase 3 RCT in the RECONNECT search literature (PMIDs 31599840, 35230162, PMC6819023) plus a no-new-data narrative review (PMC8788464). **This does not invalidate the efficacy signal** — single-sponsor registration trials are structurally normal for FDA-approved drugs — but it means the effect sizes (desire score +0.35, p<.001; distress score −0.33, p<.001) carry no external Phase 3 corroboration.

### 5.3 Author COI Disclosures

The RECONNECT lead author team has extensive, documented financial relationships with the sponsors [7, rct; 8, open_label]:

- **Sheryl A. Kingsberg** (RECONNECT lead author): advisory boards and consulting for AMAG and Palatin, plus consulting for 15+ other companies in women's health.
- **James A. Simon** (52-week extension lead): advisory and consulting roles with AMAG and Palatin, plus 18+ other companies.
- **David Portman**: advisory roles with AMAG and Palatin; employee and stockholder of Sermonix Pharmaceuticals (an AMAG-affiliated entity at the time).
- **Laura A. Williams and Julie Krop**: disclosed as **AMAG employees and stockholders** — not arms-length academic authors; they are sponsor employees on the byline [8, open_label].
- **Robert Jordan**: disclosed as **Palatin Technologies Vice President** with a "significant role in bremelanotide development" [8, open_label].
- **Anita H. Clayton**: consulting for Palatin and multiple other companies.

These disclosures confirm that multiple authors in both the primary RECONNECT publication and the long-term extension study have direct employment, equity, or paid consulting relationships with the sponsors. This is material context — not disqualifying by itself, but load-bearing for the interpretation that the effect sizes carry no external corroboration.

### 5.4 The Vyleesi Commercial Arc: Approved but Commercially Marginal

The commercialization history of Vyleesi is a case study in the gap between regulatory success and market acceptance, and it provides an independent empirical signal about the gap between the narrow approved indication and the commercial opportunity that was imagined:

**2017**: Palatin and AMAG execute an exclusive North American commercialization license for bremelanotide.

**June 21, 2019**: FDA approves Vyleesi (NDA 210557). Analyst projections: peak sales $810M–$1B. Drug launched September 2019 with ~125 AMAG sales representatives.

**Q4 2019**: Only approximately 3,000 prescriptions were written across ~1,300 providers in the first four weeks post-launch [28, anecdote_aggregate]. Vyleesi did not generate a separate revenue line item in AMAG's quarterly filings. Analysts quickly revised projections downward, citing the 40% nausea rate, the self-injection delivery requirement, and the ~$800/4-pack list price.

**July 2020**: Palatin and AMAG mutually terminated the North American license within 13 months of commercial launch. AMAG paid Palatin $12M at closing plus $4.3M due March 2021 (total $16.3M exit payment) [26, vendor_label]. AMAG implemented a 30% workforce reduction. Palatin regained North American rights and began direct commercialization.

**FY2021–FY2022 under Palatin**: Vyleesi gross product sales were $4.7M (FY2021, ending June 30, 2021) with negative net product revenue (−$283,286). FY2022 gross product sales reached $5.8M, net product revenue $1.2M — a large percentage improvement on a very small absolute base [26, vendor_label].

**December 2023**: Palatin completed the sale of Vyleesi to **Cosette Pharmaceuticals** for $12M upfront plus up to $159M in sales-based milestone payments contingent on annual net sales reaching thresholds from $15M to $200M [27, vendor_label]. The milestone structure — requiring sales to first reach $15M — implies annual sales were well below $15M at time of sale. Palatin cited a strategic decision to focus on its melanocortin pipeline in dry eye disease (PL9643) and ulcerative colitis (PL8177). As of 2024–2026, Cosette Pharmaceuticals is the listed manufacturer on the Vyleesi package insert.

**Chain of ownership**: University of Arizona → Competitive Technologies (license) → Palatin Technologies (development; licensed to AMAG 2017) → AMAG (commercialization, terminated July 2020) → Palatin (direct commercialization 2020–2023) → Cosette Pharmaceuticals (2023–present).

**Commercial interpretation.** The drug is FDA-approved and genuinely effective for its label indication. Its commercial failure does not negate the clinical evidence. What it does signal is that the combination of narrow indication (premenopausal women with physician-confirmed HSDD), modest effect size, high nausea rate, self-injection delivery, and substantial cost created a market much smaller than pre-approval projections. The grey-market PT-141 economy, which operates on a completely different population and efficacy narrative, is not the approved product and does not benefit from the regulatory evidence that supports it.

### 5.5 Grey-Market Sourcing Realism

The approved Vyleesi product (4-pack autoinjector) carries an approximate retail list price of ~$800 before insurance; copay assistance programs reduced out-of-pocket costs for insured patients but the drug was never widely covered. The same peptide sequence is available from grey-market "research peptide" vendors at a fraction of the cost (commonly $20–$50 per 10 mg vial), sold under a "not for human consumption" legal fiction.

**Purity and identity are unverifiable in the grey market.** Grey-market PT-141 is sold as lyophilized powder requiring reconstitution. Vendor-supplied Certificates of Analysis (COAs) reference HPLC purity and mass spectrometry identity, but these certificates are generated by the vendor or a vendor-selected lab, are not subject to FDA cGMP oversight, and cannot be independently verified by the end user. No batch-level third-party audit exists in the grey-market supply chain.

**MT-II / PT-141 compound confusion is a documented risk.** MT-II and PT-141 are structurally adjacent but pharmacologically distinct — different C-terminal group, different receptor potency rank order, different adverse-event burden. Grey-market vendors routinely conflate these compounds; a vial labeled "PT-141" may contain MT-II (which carries stronger tanning activity, more pronounced nausea/yawning/spontaneous erections, and a potentially different blood pressure profile). Health authorities in the UK, Australia, and US have issued warnings about melanotan II specifically due to reports of melanoma risk association and adverse events from unregulated sales [16, mechanism_review].

A 2020 FDA warning letter to Tailor Made Compounding LLC cited bremelanotide among substances compounded without meeting 503A conditions [30, regulatory]. Analysis of FDA warning letters to compounding pharmacies between 2017 and 2022 found adulteration — strength, quality, or purity below label claim — as the leading violation category [31, mechanism_review].

**The honest bottom line on PT-141/bremelanotide:** This is the most evidence-grounded compound in the melanocortin sexual class — a genuine FDA approval resting on two replicated Phase 3 RCTs, for a specific, well-defined indication, in a specific population. The effect is real, statistically robust across both trials, and clinically meaningful for a subset of treated patients. But the effect is modest in absolute terms, the nausea burden is substantial, the approved population is narrow, the entire Phase 3 evidence base derives from a single sponsor's program, and the gap between the approved indication and the grey-market positioning of "PT-141" — as a broad-spectrum libido booster for all comers — is the central honesty failure in the public discourse around this compound. The commercial arc of Vyleesi (approved in 2019, commercially failed by 2020, sold twice by 2023 at prices reflecting sub-$15M annual sales) is an empirical signal about what the drug actually does in the real world for the population it was marketed to — a much narrower benefit than the market imagined, even with a legitimate FDA approval behind it.

## 7. Clinical Prescribing Reality, Patient Selection, and the Honest Conversation About HSDD

### 7.1 The Diagnostic Threshold: Why HSDD Is Not "Low Libido"

One of the most important contextual points for any clinician or researcher interpreting the RECONNECT data is what HSDD actually means as a diagnostic category and how demanding the enrollment criteria were. The DSM-5 criteria for Female Sexual Interest/Arousal Disorder (FSIAD) — the current diagnostic frame — require that the loss of sexual desire or arousal cause clinically significant distress or interpersonal difficulty for a minimum of six months, and that it not be better explained by a nonsexual mental disorder, significant relationship distress, the effects of a medication or drug substance, or another medical condition. In the RECONNECT trials, participants were required to have acquired (not lifelong) and generalized (not situational) HSDD, physician-confirmed, meeting formal diagnostic criteria. They were not simply women who wanted more libido than they currently had.

This diagnostic bar is load-bearing for how the drug's effect size is interpreted. A placebo-adjusted FSFI-D improvement of +0.35 on a 6-point desire subscale sounds modest — and it is modest in absolute terms. But the baseline FSFI-D scores in RECONNECT participants were low (reflecting genuine dysfunction), the endpoint captures change from a clinically impaired baseline rather than enhancement from a normal baseline, and the population had a specific, diagnosed medical condition rather than a normal variation in sexual interest. Applying RECONNECT effect sizes to predict what bremelanotide would do in the grey-market population — healthy men and women without HSDD — is not a valid extrapolation. The grey-market "libido-for-everyone" framing implicitly treats RECONNECT efficacy as evidence for enhancement effects in a non-clinical population; the trial design simply does not support that inference.

### 7.2 Why Clinical Practice Has Been Slow to Adopt Vyleesi

Despite FDA approval, Vyleesi's prescription uptake has been limited. Multiple factors explain the gap:

**Patient experience concerns.** A 40% nausea rate — sustained through 52 weeks of open-label extension use, not resolved after the first dose — is a substantial barrier for a drug taken on-demand before anticipated sexual activity. Nausea onset at ~1 hour post-injection means symptoms typically arrive during or just after the targeted window of sexual activity. Combined with flushing (~20%), headache (~11%), and the self-injection requirement, the drug's tolerability profile is genuinely challenging. In a therapeutic area where patient acceptability and adherence are central to efficacy, these barriers translate into real-world attrition even among patients who might benefit from the drug's pharmacological mechanism.

**Prescriber hesitancy and limited awareness.** HSDD is underdiagnosed and underdiscussed in clinical practice; many prescribers have low comfort with sexual medicine as a domain. The blood pressure warning and CV contraindication add monitoring requirements that may deter generalists. The need to rule out relationship, psychiatric, and medication causes before prescribing requires a careful assessment that is time-intensive in primary care visits. The label's narrow scope (premenopausal women only) limits the patient population, and in a specialty-limited healthcare system, the women most likely to benefit may have limited access to the prescribers most comfortable with the diagnosis and the drug.

**Cost and coverage.** At ~$800/4-pack list price — roughly $200 per dose — with limited insurance coverage, cost is a direct barrier. Copay assistance programs under AMAG and later Palatin reduced out-of-pocket costs for commercially insured patients, but the broader coverage landscape never became permissive. The combination of a modest effect, a challenging tolerability profile, and substantial out-of-pocket cost creates a value-perception gap that has limited uptake even in the approved population.

**The compounding alternative.** 503A compounding pharmacies have offered bremelanotide at substantially lower cost, leveraging the "significant clinical difference" carveout — typically a different concentration or the nasal spray route — to provide individualized prescriptions outside the branded autoinjector. These compounded products are not subject to the same manufacturing quality standards as the approved Vyleesi product, are not subject to the same bioavailability evidence, and may differ meaningfully in actual delivered dose [23, regulatory]. The FDA's 2020 warning letter to Tailor Made Compounding LLC illustrates that not all compounding of bremelanotide meets 503A conditions [30, regulatory].

### 7.3 The HSDD-in-Men Diagnostic Gap

The grey-market narrative for men typically frames PT-141 as a treatment for "low libido" or "erectile dysfunction" without engaging with what would actually need to be true for the drug to be clinically useful in men. Erectile dysfunction and low sexual desire are mechanistically distinct conditions with different causes and different evidence-based treatments. PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) address the vascular mechanics of erection — insufficient penile blood flow during sexual stimulation — and have robust Phase 3 evidence in men with ED. They do not address desire, arousal, or the motivational aspect of sexual drive.

Bremelanotide's CNS mechanism (MC4R activation in hypothalamic and limbic circuits) is at least theoretically relevant to desire and arousal, not the vascular mechanics of erection. The early male trials showing RigiScan-measurable penile rigidity (Diamond 2004 [13, rct]; Rosen 2004 [14, rct]; Diamond 2005 [15, rct]) measured a downstream outcome — erection — of the central arousal signal. But there is no established concept of "HSDD in men" that is diagnostically analogous to the RECONNECT population, no validated endpoint tool equivalent to FSFI-D for the male desire domain, and no Phase 3 trial to anchor any claim about what bremelanotide would do for a clinically defined population of men with low desire. What the early male trials showed is that subcutaneous bremelanotide and intranasal bremelanotide can trigger penile tumescence in men, including some who had not responded to PDE5 inhibitors — consistent with the compound's CNS mechanism operating independently of the vascular pathway. What they cannot show, by virtue of their design and size, is what the drug's efficacy, tolerability, and risk profile would be across a population-representative sample of men seeking treatment for sexual desire or erectile problems. That trial was never done.

The specific subset of men for whom bremelanotide might provide real clinical benefit — those with primary desire-deficit rather than vascular ED, who do not respond to PDE5 inhibitors, who are normotensive and without CVD, and who can tolerate a 40% nausea rate — is real but probably small, and exists without any Phase 3 trial to quantify it or guide dosing [21, meta_analysis]. The grey-market framing conflates this theoretical niche with a mass market.

One further taxonomic point reinforces why the approved female HSDD indication does not transfer to a male diagnosis: **DSM-5 (2013) retired "Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder" as a unified cross-sex diagnosis**. Under DSM-IV-TR, HSDD was a single category applicable to both sexes. DSM-5 split the condition: the female low-desire condition became **Female Sexual Interest/Arousal Disorder (FSIAD)**, which fuses desire and arousal into a single polythetic construct; the male low-desire condition became a distinct category, **Male Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder (MHSDD)**, with its own diagnostic criteria. Bremelanotide's RECONNECT trials enrolled participants meeting the pre-DSM-5 HSDD criteria in women — a population that maps onto FSIAD under DSM-5 framing. There is no analogous validated diagnostic label or validated male-specific endpoint battery for MHSDD in the bremelanotide trial history. The diagnostic taxonomy itself is sex-differentiated: female and male low-desire conditions are now formally distinct constructs, and an approval for one does not logically transfer to the other.

### 7.4 Flibanserin (Addyi) — The Other FDA-Approved Premenopausal HSDD Drug

Bremelanotide is not the only FDA-approved pharmacotherapy for HSDD in premenopausal women. **Flibanserin (Addyi; Sprout Pharmaceuticals)** was approved by the FDA on **August 18, 2015** (NDA 022526) — nearly four years before Vyleesi — for acquired, generalized HSDD in premenopausal women [32, regulatory]. The two drugs occupy the same regulatory indication niche but differ materially in mechanism, dosing architecture, and safety profile.

**Mechanistic contrast.** Flibanserin acts through a central **serotonergic** mechanism: it is a **5-HT1A agonist and 5-HT2A antagonist** (with additional weak 5-HT2B, 5-HT2C, and dopamine D4 antagonism) that modulates the balance between serotonergic and dopaminergic/noradrenergic tone in the prefrontal cortex — net effect is proposed to be a reduction in serotonin-mediated inhibition of sexual desire alongside a modest increase in dopamine and norepinephrine release [32, regulatory]. This is a chronic daily neurochemical rebalancing mechanism, not an acute on-demand central arousal trigger. Bremelanotide, by contrast, acutely agonizes melanocortin receptors (MC4R predominant for arousal; MC1R residual for tanning/nausea) in hypothalamic and limbic circuits on an as-needed basis with effects resolved within ~12 hours. The two mechanisms are pharmacologically orthogonal — different receptors, different time courses, different dosing architectures.

**Dosing architecture contrast.** Flibanserin is a **daily oral bedtime dose** (100 mg at bedtime; not for daytime use due to hypotension/CNS depression risk) — a chronic regimen requiring weeks of consistent use [32, regulatory]. Bremelanotide is **on-demand SC injection**, taken ≥45 minutes before anticipated sexual activity, not more than once per 24 hours and ≤8 times per month [1, regulatory]. For patients, the difference is practically significant: daily commitment vs. situational use.

**Safety profile contrast.** Flibanserin carries a **boxed warning** for hypotension and syncope when combined with alcohol — "use of ADDYI and alcohol together close in time increases the risk of severe hypotension and syncope" — requiring that patients wait ≥2 hours after consuming 1–2 drinks before taking the bedtime dose [32, regulatory]. CNS depression (dizziness 11.4%, somnolence 11.2%, nausea 10.4%, fatigue 9.2%) is the dominant adverse-event cluster. Bremelanotide's AE signature is dominated by nausea (~40%) and flushing (~20%) related to its MC1R activity, plus the focal hyperpigmentation signal; it does not carry an alcohol-interaction boxed warning, but does carry contraindications for uncontrolled hypertension and cardiovascular disease that flibanserin does not.

**Efficacy — both modest.** Flibanserin's Phase 3 trials showed a median increase of approximately 0.5–1.0 satisfying sexual events per 28 days versus placebo, with 29–39% of flibanserin-treated patients reporting "much improved" versus 21–31% of placebo recipients [32, regulatory]. These are statistically significant but modest absolute effects — a pattern that closely parallels bremelanotide's RECONNECT results (desire score +0.35 on FSFI-D; ~58% vs. ~36% responder rate). **Both FDA-approved HSDD drugs, operating through entirely different receptor systems, converge on a similar honest characterization: real regulatory approval, real statistical effect, modest absolute magnitude.** This convergence is not coincidental — it likely reflects both the biological complexity of sexual desire as a modifiable phenotype and the real contribution of placebo response in this therapeutic area.

The existence of two approved HSDD drugs reinforces the class's regulatory legitimacy while also reinforcing the effect-size honesty: neither drug is a dramatic libido restorative. Both work in a specific, well-defined population (premenopausal women with physician-confirmed HSDD causing marked distress); neither has been validated for the grey-market population of healthy individuals seeking desire enhancement.
