# PT-141 (Bremelanotide / Vyleesi) — Prescribing-Practice Layer

> **Scope discipline.** This document records *how bremelanotide is actually prescribed, accessed, dosed, and monitored in practice* — the FDA-approved Vyleesi pathway (narrow indication, premenopausal women with HSDD), the compounding reality (different-route formulations not "essentially a copy" under FDA doctrine, legitimate 503A pathway exists for off-label prescribing), and the grey-market landscape of "PT-141" nasal sprays and injectables sold far outside the approved indication. Source-tagging rule: `regulatory` (Tier 1) and `rct` (Tier 2) ground safety and monitoring parameters. `practitioner_protocol` (Tier 2.7), `anecdote_aggregate` (Tier 4), and `vendor_label` (Tier 4) are admissible **only** for dose/route/formulation/reconstitution conventions. **None** of these lower tiers grounds an efficacy, adverse-event-rate, or mechanism claim.
>
> **Regulatory state, read first.** Bremelanotide (Vyleesi®) is a **genuine FDA-approved pharmaceutical** — approved June 21, 2019 under NDA 210557 for acquired, generalized HSDD in premenopausal women. This is a real regulatory pathway with a labeled indication, an autoinjector delivery product, DailyMed-indexed prescribing information, and a commercial specialty pharmacy distribution channel (BlinkRx). The approved prescribing pathway is real and narrow. **Two things are simultaneously true:** a legitimate prescription pathway exists, and the reality of how "PT-141" is actually used in clinical practice extends far beyond that pathway — into compounded alternative formulations for off-label prescribing (men, postmenopausal women) and into an entirely separate grey-market research-chemical ecosystem operating without any prescription oversight. **One critical hazard that practitioners must address at first contact:** MT-II (Melanotan II), PT-141 (bremelanotide), and afamelanotide (Scenesse) are three structurally related but pharmacologically distinct compounds that circulate under overlapping names in the grey market. They are not interchangeable; sourcing one while believing you have another is a documented, consequential confusion.

## Approved Prescribing: Vyleesi (Bremelanotide Injection)

### Approved Indication

The FDA-approved Vyleesi label (NDA 210557, approved June 2019; currently marketed by Cosette Pharmaceuticals) states the following indication [1, regulatory]:

**Premenopausal women with acquired, generalized hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD)** characterized by low sexual desire causing marked distress or interpersonal difficulty, where the condition is not due to:
- A co-existing medical or psychiatric condition
- Problems within the relationship
- Effects of a medication or other drug substance

**Limitations of use (label-explicit):** [1, regulatory]
- **Not indicated** for HSDD due to medical/psychiatric conditions or relationship problems
- **Not indicated** for postmenopausal women
- **Not indicated** for men
- **Not indicated** to enhance sexual performance

**What "acquired, generalized" means precisely:** Acquired = the disorder developed after a period of normal sexual desire (distinguishes from lifelong low desire). Generalized = occurs regardless of type of stimulation, situation, or partner (distinguishes from situational or partner-specific low desire). Both qualifiers are required by the approved indication.

**What this approval is, precisely:** A standard FDA Rx approval for a single, narrow indication. Prescribers can legitimately prescribe Vyleesi autoinjector to premenopausal women meeting the label criteria. This is distinct from grey-market PT-141 in every respect: labeled product, GMP manufacturing, autoinjector delivery, specialty pharmacy channel.

**What this approval is not:** Authorization to use bremelanotide in men, postmenopausal women, or for performance enhancement. The off-label use by compounded alternative formulations (nasal spray, sublingual troches) occupies a legally distinct space — permissible under 503A compounding doctrine but outside the approved label.

### Dosing and Administration (Approved Label)

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|-----------|-------|---------|
| Dose | 1.75 mg subcutaneously via autoinjector | [1, regulatory] |
| Timing | At least 45 minutes before anticipated sexual activity | [1, regulatory] |
| Frequency limit | Maximum 1 dose per 24-hour period | [1, regulatory] |
| Monthly limit | No more than 8 doses per month | [1, regulatory] |
| Route | Subcutaneous injection to abdomen or thigh | [1, regulatory] |
| Formulation | 1.75 mg/0.3 mL prefilled autoinjector | [1, regulatory] |

| Storage | ≤25°C (77°F); do not freeze; protect from light | [1, regulatory] |
| Discontinuation trigger | Discontinue after 8 weeks if no symptom improvement | [1, regulatory] |

**Alcohol interaction:** The label does not contraindicate concurrent alcohol use. The HCP website explicitly states Vyleesi is "safe with alcohol consumption." [2, regulatory]

**How patients in the trials actually used it:** In the RECONNECT Phase 3 trials, most patients used Vyleesi 2–3 times per month, not approaching the 8-dose ceiling. [1, regulatory]

### Contraindications (Approved Label)

1. **Uncontrolled hypertension** — Bremelanotide causes transient blood pressure increases; use in uncontrolled hypertension risks additive elevations. [1, regulatory]
2. **Known cardiovascular disease** — Transient hemodynamic effects are incompatible with pre-existing CV disease. [1, regulatory]

### Warnings and Precautions (Approved Label)

| Warning | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Blood pressure / heart rate changes | Transient increases in systolic BP (max ~6 mmHg) and diastolic BP (max ~3 mmHg) with heart rate decrease up to ~5 bpm; typically resolve within 12 hours post-dose | [1, regulatory] |
| High-CV-risk patients | Not recommended for patients at high cardiovascular risk even without the absolute contraindications | [1, regulatory] |
| Focal hyperpigmentation | 1% incidence in patients receiving ≤8 doses/month; 38% incidence with daily dosing ×8 days; affects face, gingiva, and breasts; **resolution not confirmed in all patients after discontinuation** — increased risk in darker skin tones | [1, regulatory] |
| Nausea | 40% of patients; 8% discontinued due to nausea; improved for most after second dose; pre-treatment with ondansetron did not prevent nausea in trials | [1, regulatory] |
| Pregnancy | Fetal harm shown in animal studies (dogs ≥16× MRHD; mice ≥125× MRHD); discontinue if pregnancy suspected; use effective contraception; pregnancy exposure registry: 1-800-922-1038 | [1, regulatory] |
| Renal impairment | No dose adjustment for mild–moderate impairment; caution in severe impairment (eGFR <30) | [1, regulatory] |
| Hepatic impairment | No dose adjustment for mild–moderate (Child-Pugh A/B); caution in severe impairment (Child-Pugh C) | [1, regulatory] |
| Gastric emptying | May slow gastric emptying; reduces absorption of oral medications dependent on threshold concentrations (e.g., some antibiotics) | [1, regulatory] |

**Hyperpigmentation counseling — critical point:** The label warns that hyperpigmentation "may not resolve after drug discontinuation." This is not a transient cosmetic effect — practitioners must counsel patients about this risk before initiating, particularly patients with darker skin tones (who are at higher risk). Resolution was not confirmed in all patients even after stopping Vyleesi. [1, regulatory]

**Nausea management:** Ondansetron pretreatment was ineffective in trials. Dose timing adjustments and expectation-setting (improves after second dose for most) are the available mitigation strategies, not antiemetic prophylaxis. [1, regulatory]

### Drug Interactions (Approved Label)

| Interaction | Mechanism | Clinical Consequence | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oral naltrexone (ReVia, Vivitrol) | Bremelanotide significantly decreases systemic exposure of oral naltrexone | **Avoid concurrent use** — risk of naltrexone treatment failure for opioid or alcohol use disorder | [1, regulatory] |
| Oral medications dependent on threshold concentrations | Slowed gastric emptying reduces absorption rate and peak | Clinical relevance case-by-case; counsel on timing of critical oral medications | [1, regulatory] |
| Indomethacin | No clinically relevant interaction identified in label studies | No dose adjustment needed | [1, regulatory] |

**The naltrexone interaction is the only absolute avoid listed.** Practitioners treating patients on naltrexone maintenance for opioid or alcohol use disorder must not co-prescribe bremelanotide — the interaction can undermine addiction treatment.

|---|---|---|---|
| Nausea | 40% | 1% | [1, regulatory] |
| Flushing | 20.3% | 1.5% | [1, regulatory] |
| Injection site reactions | 13.2% | 12.9% | [1, regulatory] |
| Headache | 11.3% | 5.0% | [1, regulatory] |
| Vomiting | 4.8% | <1% | [1, regulatory] |

**Serious AEs:** Reported in 1.1% of treated patients. One case of acute hepatitis with serum transaminases 40× ULN was documented. [1, regulatory]

**Discontinuation rate:** 18% of patients discontinued due to adverse reactions; nausea was the primary reason (8% of total). [1, regulatory]

**Modest efficacy context:** The RECONNECT trial primary endpoints — FSFI desire domain score and FSDS-DAO distress scale — achieved statistical significance (p<0.0001 to 0.0053) but with modest absolute effect sizes. The satisfying sexual events endpoint did not reach significance. Practitioners should set realistic expectations: meaningful symptom reduction for a subset, modest average improvement, substantial nausea burden. [1, regulatory]

### Approved Prescribing: Commercial Reality

**Manufacturer history:** Bremelanotide was developed by Palatin Technologies. AMAG Pharmaceuticals licensed North American rights in 2017 and launched Vyleesi in 2019, but terminated the partnership in 2020 after commercially disappointing performance. Palatin reacquired rights and managed commercial operations until December 19, 2023, when Cosette Pharmaceuticals acquired worldwide rights for $12 million upfront plus up to $159 million in sales-based milestone payments. [3, anecdote_aggregate]

**Current commercial status:** Cosette Pharmaceuticals is the current NDA holder and marketer of Vyleesi. Prescription growth has shown consecutive double-digit gains since the Cosette acquisition. The product is commercially available through specialty pharmacy BlinkRx with home delivery. [3, anecdote_aggregate; 4, vendor_label]

**Cost without insurance:** List price approximately $290–$324 per autoinjector dose (sold in 4-dose boxes at ~$1,296 per box). At the 8-dose/month ceiling, cash cost exceeds $2,000/month. [3, anecdote_aggregate]

**Manufacturer assistance:**
- Commercially insured patients: most pay $0 copay (manufacturer program)
- Uninsured patients: 4-dose box for $99 through manufacturer savings
- Government-insured (Medicaid, TRICARE): not eligible for manufacturer copay assistance
- BlinkRx specialty pharmacy: 1-833-799-5028; home delivery; same-day prescription processing
[4, vendor_label]

**Insurance coverage:** Prior authorization commonly required. Mass General Brigham health plan policy (effective March 1, 2025) confirms active prior authorization requirements for commercial/exchange plans. [5, regulatory]

## Compounding Status: The "Essentially a Copy" Analysis

### The Doctrine and Its Application to Bremelanotide

Under Section 503A of the FDCA, a compounding pharmacy may not compound a drug that is "essentially a copy" of a commercially available drug product. FDA's operative guidance [6, regulatory] defines "essentially a copy" as: a drug product that has the same active pharmaceutical ingredient(s), route of administration, dosage form, and strength as the commercially available product, unless a prescriber determines and documents that the compounded product contains a change that produces a clinically significant difference for an identified individual patient.

**Vyleesi's approved form:** 1.75 mg subcutaneous injection, autoinjector delivery.

**Compounded bremelanotide formulations currently marketed by 503A pharmacies:** Nasal spray (most common), sublingual troche, intranasal solution at concentrations including 10 mg/mL. These differ from the approved product in route of administration (intranasal vs. subcutaneous; sublingual vs. subcutaneous) and dosage form.

**Regulatory consequence:** Compounded bremelanotide nasal spray and sublingual troches are **not** "essentially a copy" of Vyleesi under current FDA doctrine, because they differ in route of administration. A 503A compounding pharmacy can compound these formulations for patient-specific prescriptions without being blocked by the "essentially a copy" provision. [6, regulatory]

**The key practical result:** Unlike the situation with FDA-approved drugs where the SC injection is being repackaged as the same SC injection (which would be blocked), alternative-route compounded bremelanotide occupies legitimate 503A territory. This is why the compounding market for PT-141 nasal spray is robust despite Vyleesi's approval — the different route creates legal space for compounding.

**Compounded SC injection of bremelanotide 1.75 mg:** Would be "essentially a copy" of Vyleesi. A 503A pharmacy compounding the identical SC injection at the same strength would face "essentially a copy" scrutiny.

## The Three-Compound Hazard: MT-II vs. PT-141 vs. Afamelanotide

**This is the most critical identity hazard for practitioners and patients in the grey-market context.** Three structurally related melanocortin agonists circulate under overlapping names and are often conflated — by vendors, patients, and occasionally by clinicians. They are pharmacologically distinct, have different regulatory histories, and present different clinical risk profiles.

| Property | PT-141 (Bremelanotide) | MT-II (Melanotan II) | Afamelanotide (Scenesse) |
|---|---|---|---|
| INN / trade name | Bremelanotide / Vyleesi | None (no approved INN; never approved) | Afamelanotide / Scenesse |
| Structure | Cyclic heptapeptide; C-terminal free carboxyl group; de-amidated form of MT-II | Cyclic heptapeptide; C-terminal amide; parent compound | 13-amino acid linear peptide analogue of α-MSH; Nle4, D-Phe7 substituted |
| Receptor selectivity | Selective MC3R/MC4R agonist; minimal MC1R activity → minimal tanning effect | Broad MC1R/MC3R/MC4R/MC5R agonist → tanning + sexual arousal + appetite suppression | Selective MC1R agonist → melanin production for photoprotection; minimal sexual arousal effect |
| Primary clinical use | HSDD in premenopausal women (FDA-approved); off-label ED/libido in men | Grey-market "tanning + libido" use; no approved indication in any jurisdiction | Erythropoietic protoporphyria (EPP) — photoprotection; FDA-approved (Clinuvel) |
| FDA approval | YES — Vyleesi, NDA 210557, June 2019 | NO — never approved by FDA or EMA | YES — Scenesse, NDA 210797, October 2019 |
| Route of approved product | Subcutaneous injection autoinjector | N/A — not approved | Subcutaneous biodegradable implant (16 mg controlled-release) |
| Tanning effect | Minimal (low MC1R activity) | Strong skin darkening; mole darkening risk (melanoma concern in susceptible individuals) | Strong MC1R activation → protective melanin production (the therapeutic purpose) |
| Nausea | 40% in clinical trials at 1.75 mg SC | Higher at effective grey-market doses due to broader receptor activation | Lower (not the primary profile) |
| BP effect | Transient increase (label-documented) | Less characterized; cardiovascular risk in grey-market unscreened use | Different mechanism; minimal cardiovascular effect |
| WADA status | Not on 2026 Prohibited List; verify before athletic use | Not explicitly named; but S0 may apply (never approved drug) | Not applicable (EPP indication, no performance-enhancing profile) |
| Confusion hazard | Most relevant: MT-II sold as PT-141; or user believes less tanning = less risk | Melanoma risk from mole activation; CV risk; identity confusion with PT-141 | Rarely confused in practice; EPP indication is highly specific |

[Sources: 1, regulatory; 10, vendor_label; 11, anecdote_aggregate]

**Why the MT-II/PT-141 confusion is documented and consequential:** MT-II was developed first (University of Arizona, 1980s); PT-141 was derived from it by de-amidation (C-terminal amide → free carboxyl). They share the cyclic heptapeptide scaffold. Grey-market vendors sell both as "libido/tanning peptides" with overlapping marketing language. A patient buying "PT-141 nasal spray for libido" may receive:
- Legitimate compounded bremelanotide from a licensed 503A pharmacy (prescription required; clinical oversight possible)
- Research-chemical PT-141 from a grey-market vendor (no prescription; purity unverified)
- MT-II marketed as PT-141 (different compound with stronger tanning + broader AE profile + mole-darkening risk)

**Operational rule:** Practitioners seeing patients who self-identify as using "PT-141" or "bremelanotide" must confirm whether the product was obtained via a licensed compounding pharmacy with a prescription, or from a grey-market vendor. These are different safety situations. Request a vendor Certificate of Analysis confirming the compound identity and purity via HPLC before advising on any grey-market product. Never accept "melanocortin peptide" or "libido peptide" as adequate product identification.

## Off-Label and Grey-Market Reality

### Off-Label Use in Men

**The approved indication covers premenopausal women only.** The label explicitly states bremelanotide is "not indicated for men." Male use is off-label; the male evidence base consists of early-phase trials without a completed Phase 3 program for any male indication.

**Available male-specific evidence:**

*Safarinejad et al. (2008, J Urology):* Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial; n=342 married men with sildenafil-resistant ED. PT-141 (bremelanotide, intranasal formulation used in this trial era) showed 62% improvement in the PT-141 arm vs. 21% placebo (p<0.001). Title: "Salvage of sildenafil failures with bremelanotide." [12, rct] This is one of the only male-specific Phase 2 trial citations in the PT-141 literature; it used an intranasal route; the study predates the FDA approval of the SC formulation; no Phase 3 followed for male ED. **INTEGRITY FLAG: This paper carries an active Expression of Concern issued by J Urol in 2023 (PMID 36626345) on data-integrity grounds. Efficacy claims from this study should NOT be treated as reliable; it is the sole large male RCT in this literature and it is integrity-flagged.**

*Palatin Technologies (2024-2025 Phase II):* Palatin initiated a Phase II study in men with ED who are PDE5 inhibitor non-responders, using a co-formulation of bremelanotide plus a PDE5 inhibitor. Topline results were expected by end of 2024. If positive, a Phase 3 was planned for 2025. This represents ongoing formal development but not an approved pathway. [13, anecdote_aggregate]

**Honest assessment of the male evidence base:** The male use case for PT-141 rests primarily on small, early-phase trials without a completed Phase 3 RCT program. The Safarinejad study establishes biological plausibility and a preliminary efficacy signal but not the evidence standard for an approved indication — and critically, this sole large male RCT carries an active Expression of Concern issued by J Urol in 2023 (PMID 36626345) on data-integrity grounds; its efficacy claims must not be treated as reliable. The male evidence base is therefore both thin and integrity-flagged. Male HSDD (distinct DSM-5 diagnosis from ED) has no Phase 3 bremelanotide trial. Off-label prescribing to men by compounded nasal spray or troche formulations is a practitioner decision made without a validated male indication.

### Grey-Market "PT-141" Ecosystem

A substantial grey-market exists for "PT-141" — research-chemical injectable and nasal spray products sold without prescription for libido and ED, in both sexes. This market operates entirely outside the Vyleesi approved pathway and the licensed 503A compounding pathway.

**Product forms in the grey market:**
- Nasal spray vials, typically 10 mg (research-chemical vendors: PraxPeptides, BehemothLabz, Direct Peptides UK, and others) [14, vendor_label]
- Injectable lyophilized powder vials
- Some vendors claim 98–99% purity with certificate of analysis (CoA); these are self-reported, not FDA-verified

**Dose conventions in the grey market (tagged anecdote/vendor — grounds NO clinical claim):**

| Source | Dose | Route | Timing | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peptide Protocol Wiki [15, anecdote_aggregate] | 1–2 mg | SC or nasal | 30–60 min pre-activity | "Starting dose 1 mg; titrate to 2 mg" |
| Enavvi practitioner guide [16, practitioner_protocol] | Nasal: 1–2 sprays (0.5–2 mg per spray) | Nasal | 30–60 min pre-activity | "Titrate based on response and tolerability" |

**Critical note on the "7 mg" convention:** This dose — cited by some practitioners for male use — is substantially higher than the approved 1.75 mg dose and far exceeds the dose range studied in the RECONNECT trials. No Phase 3 safety or efficacy data exists for 7 mg in any population. The cardiovascular AE profile (BP elevation, HR decrease) at supratherapeutic doses is not characterized. Tag: anecdote_aggregate; grounds no clinical claim.

**Vendor number ground rule:** No vendor, research-chemical site, or anecdotal aggregate grounds a dose as evidence-based for off-label use. The only validated dose (in any indication) is 1.75 mg SC from the RECONNECT Phase 3 trials.

## Practitioner Safety Points

### Pre-Treatment Evaluation (Derived from Label + Clinical Trial Monitoring)

| Domain | Assessment | Rationale | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blood pressure (measured) | BP measurement; rule out uncontrolled hypertension | Label contraindication: transient BP elevation with each dose | [1, regulatory] |
| Cardiovascular history | Prior MI, stroke, heart failure, arrhythmia, high CV risk | Label contraindication: known CVD | [1, regulatory] |
| Naltrexone use | Medication review for oral naltrexone (ReVia), extended-release naltrexone (Vivitrol) | Drug interaction: bremelanotide significantly reduces oral naltrexone systemic exposure | [1, regulatory] |
| Reproductive status (women) | Premenopausal vs. postmenopausal confirmation | Approved indication: premenopausal women only | [1, regulatory] |
| Pregnancy / contraception | Active pregnancy excluded; effective contraception required | Fetal harm risk; discontinue if pregnancy suspected | [1, regulatory] |
| HSDD characterization (women, on-label) | Acquired vs. lifelong; generalized vs. situational; rule out medical, psychiatric, relationship causation | All four required to meet approved indication criteria | [1, regulatory] |
| Skin tone / hyperpigmentation baseline | Document at baseline; counsel before initiation | Higher risk of focal hyperpigmentation in darker skin tones; not confirmed reversible | [1, regulatory] |
| Renal / hepatic function | Assess if severe impairment suspected | Caution at eGFR <30 and Child-Pugh C | [1, regulatory] |
| Product identity (grey-market use) | Request vendor CoA; confirm bremelanotide vs. MT-II vs. afamelanotide | Three distinct compounds; MT-II mole-darkening risk; identity confusion documented | [10, vendor_label; 11, anecdote_aggregate] |

### Monitoring During Treatment

| Timing | Assessment |
|---|---|
| After first dose | Nausea tolerability; BP if patient at any elevated CV risk |
| 4 weeks | Re-assess HSDD symptom response (women, on-label); skin inspection (hyperpigmentation baseline vs. current) |
| 8 weeks | **Label-specified discontinuation decision point:** if no symptom improvement after 8 weeks, discontinue |
| Ongoing | Re-assess BP control at each visit; inspect for focal hyperpigmentation; confirm no pregnancy |

### Stopping Criteria (Label-Derived)

- Pregnancy confirmed or suspected: discontinue immediately
- Uncontrolled hypertension develops: discontinue; assess CV risk before any re-initiation
- Focal hyperpigmentation noted that is cosmetically unacceptable: shared decision; document that resolution is not guaranteed
- No HSDD symptom improvement at 8 weeks: discontinue (label-explicit)
- Initiation of oral naltrexone treatment: discontinue bremelanotide or substitute a different addiction pharmacotherapy; these cannot be co-administered
- Signs of serious hypersensitivity: discontinue; emergency intervention if systemic

### Honest Expectation-Setting for Patients

1. **Nausea is common, not rare:** 40% of patients experience nausea; 8% stop for this reason. Most improve after the second dose. There is no effective antiemetic prophylaxis (ondansetron failed in trials). Starting with the first dose accompanied by awareness of nausea likelihood is standard.
2. **Hyperpigmentation may be permanent:** The label says resolution is "not confirmed in all patients." This is not "usually resolves." Patients with darker skin are at higher risk. This is a before-you-start disclosure, not an as-it-happens discussion.
3. **Effect size is modest:** Clinical trials showed statistical improvement but not large absolute effect sizes; the satisfying sexual events endpoint did not reach significance. Vyleesi is not a guaranteed response.
4. **Monthly limit exists for a reason:** The 8-dose/month limit and the daily dose restriction exist because hyperpigmentation rate rises sharply with daily dosing (38% at 8 consecutive daily doses vs. 1% at ≤8/month). This is not a pharmacist-inserted caution — it is a label safety parameter.
