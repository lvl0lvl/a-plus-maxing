# Tirzepatide — Prescribing-Practice Layer

> **Scope discipline.** This document records *how tirzepatide is actually dosed, titrated, compounded, and accessed in practice*. For tirzepatide — unlike a gray-market peptide — the authoritative prescribing source is the **FDA label**, so the titration schedules here are `regulatory` (authoritative), not extrapolated convention. The compounding/access, cost, and muscle-preservation items are reported as **practice/regulatory context** and are admissible **only** for dose/route/access conventions. **None of them grounds an efficacy or adverse-event-rate claim** — those live in the tirzepatide research report (§2/§3/§5) as Tier-1 trial evidence and are unaffected by anything below.
>
> **Regulatory state, read first.** Tirzepatide is **FDA- and EMA-approved**: **Mounjaro** (type 2 diabetes) and **Zepbound** (chronic weight management; moderate-to-severe OSA in obesity) — the same molecule, two trade names, separate NDAs and labels with separate maximum/maintenance doses; they are **not interchangeable products**. The 2022–2024 shortage opened a large compounded-tirzepatide market; FDA reaffirmed the shortage **resolved December 19, 2024**, and the lawful compounding window has since largely closed (full arc below). Nothing here endorses current compounding legality.

## Dose / route conventions (label-driven — `regulatory`, authoritative)

Both labels share one design logic: a **low, sub-therapeutic starting dose escalated slowly** to mitigate gastrointestinal adverse reactions (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea), which cluster during escalation. This — a non-therapeutic starting dose and stepwise 4-week escalation — is the single most clinically important practitioner-facing fact, and it is fully label-driven, not improvised.

**The label-mandated ladder.** **2.5 mg once weekly for 4 weeks → 5 mg → 7.5 mg → 10 mg → 12.5 mg → 15 mg**, increasing in **2.5 mg increments after at least 4 weeks** at each dose, to a maximum of **15 mg once weekly** `regulatory` [1][2]. The Mounjaro label states the starting dose is "2.5 mg injected subcutaneously once weekly" and that "the 2.5 mg dosage is for treatment initiation and **is not intended for glycemic control**" [1] `regulatory`. The Zepbound label uses the same ladder and states the 2.5 mg dosage "is for treatment initiation and **is not approved as a maintenance dosage**," with maximum dosage "15 mg injected subcutaneously once weekly … for all indications" [2][3] `regulatory`. The **2.5 mg step is thus a non-therapeutic initiation rung** — its purpose is GI acclimation, not efficacy.

**Tolerability framing.** Both labels frame the schedule explicitly around tolerability: dose escalation is to follow the prescribed steps "to reduce the risk of gastrointestinal adverse reactions" [1][3] `regulatory`. Administration is once weekly on the same day each week, any time of day, with or without meals, by SC injection in the abdomen, thigh, or upper arm with site rotation [1] `regulatory`. Available single-dose strengths are **2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, and 15 mg per 0.5 mL** (pen or vial) [1][2] `regulatory`.

**Indication-specific maintenance (Zepbound).** For chronic weight management the approved maintenance doses are **5, 10, or 15 mg** weekly; for moderate-to-severe OSA in adults with obesity, **10 or 15 mg** weekly (the lowest maintenance dose is not approved for OSA) [2] `regulatory`. In pediatric type 2 diabetes (≥10 years) the Mounjaro maximum is **10 mg** weekly rather than 15 mg [1] `regulatory`. Clinically, prescribers commonly hold patients at the lowest effective dose and do not auto-advance to 15 mg if response and tolerability are adequate at a lower rung; the label permits any of the maintenance doses, so up-titration is individualized rather than obligatory.

## Compounded / access landscape (practice/regulatory context — NOT efficacy)

During the 2022–2024 FDA-listed shortage, compounded tirzepatide became widely available through **503A pharmacies and 503B outsourcing facilities**, frequently dispensed via telehealth platforms as multi-dose vials and often co-formulated with additives (e.g., vitamin B12 / niacinamide). This is reported here as **practice and regulatory context, not as evidence of efficacy or safety equivalence** — compounded product is not FDA-reviewed for efficacy, potency, or AE rates [7] `practitioner_protocol`.

**Shortage resolution and wind-down.** On **December 19, 2024**, after a court-ordered re-evaluation, FDA again determined the tirzepatide shortage resolved, concluding "supply meets or exceeds current demand … and will also meet or exceed projected demand" [4] `regulatory`. FDA set enforcement wind-down periods: **60 days for 503A pharmacies (ending ~February 18, 2025)** and **90 days for 503B outsourcing facilities (ending ~March 19, 2025)**, during which it did not intend to act against compounding "essentially a copy" of branded tirzepatide [4] `regulatory`. (This tirzepatide wind-down **preceded** the parallel semaglutide compounding wind-down, which resolved February 2025.) After these periods, 503B facilities generally cannot compound tirzepatide (it must be on the shortage list or the 503B Bulks List), while 503A pharmacies may compound only under narrow circumstances such as documented patient-specific medical necessity — **not** as routine copies of the approved product [4] `practitioner_protocol`. The shortage removal was litigated (the Outsourcing Facilities Association and a custom-compounding plaintiff sued FDA in the N.D. Texas, October 2024); the court **remanded** for re-evaluation, FDA reaffirmed resolution, and the compounding wind-down proceeded [4][7] `regulatory`. **This section does not endorse current legality of compounded tirzepatide; readers should treat large-volume "compounded tirzepatide" offerings post-2025 as outside the routine compounding exemption.**

**Counterfeit and salt-form caution.** FDA warns that gray-market and unapproved GLP-1 products carry real risk. The agency states that **tirzepatide salt forms — tirzepatide acetate and tirzepatide sodium — are not approved and are not the active ingredient** in Mounjaro/Zepbound, and that it "is not aware of any basis for compounding using these salts" [5] `regulatory`. Many products marketed online appear to be counterfeit (often imported), may contain incorrect or harmful ingredients or inconsistent dosing, and FDA has issued large numbers of warning letters and received adverse-event reports tied to compounded versions [5] `regulatory`. Dosing-error risk is elevated with multi-dose vials and "units"/mg conversions versus the fixed-dose pens [6] `vendor_label` (dose/format caution only).

**Cost (qualitative).** Brand tirzepatide is expensive at list price — roughly **~$1,000+ per 28-day supply** for both Mounjaro and Zepbound pens [6][8] `practitioner_protocol`. Manufacturer self-pay channels (e.g., LillyDirect single-dose vials) lowered out-of-pocket cost for cash payers — reported tiers on the order of **~$299–$499/month** depending on dose/refill timing — and commercial-insured patients with coverage may pay far less via savings cards [8] `practitioner_protocol`. During the shortage, compounded tirzepatide was typically marketed at a fraction of brand price, which drove much of its uptake; with the compounding wind-down, that cost gap for legitimate supply has narrowed, and remaining low-cost gray-market offerings carry the counterfeit/salt-form risks above [6][7] `practitioner_protocol`. (Qualitative, time-sensitive; pricing varies by source and is not anchored to a regulatory primary.)

## Muscle-preservation adjuncts (practitioner convention)

Tirzepatide produces large total-weight loss, and a portion of that loss is lean (fat-free) mass — a generic feature of substantial caloric-deficit weight loss rather than a tirzepatide-specific toxicity. In the **SURMOUNT-1 DXA substudy**, roughly **~75% of weight lost was fat mass and ~25% lean mass** (mean lean-mass change about −5.6 kg, −10.9% over 72 weeks), and the proportion of body weight that was lean tissue actually rose; an MRI analysis within SURPASS-3 found fat-free muscle-volume reductions "small and within expected ranges" [9] `practitioner_protocol` (Tier-1/composition findings cited here only to frame the convention, NOT to ground an efficacy claim in this layer). Because the pivotal trials provided lifestyle advice but did **not** mandate structured resistance training, these lean-mass numbers reflect minimal-exercise conditions [9].

The conventional practitioner adjuncts to attenuate lean-mass loss during the deficit are **progressive resistance training plus adequate dietary protein**. This is a standard recommendation rather than a tirzepatide-label requirement, and the controlled evidence for the *adjuncts specifically combined with tirzepatide* is still limited (dedicated trials are ongoing). It is reported here as practice convention; it does not modify the efficacy/AE claims in the research report `practitioner_protocol` (convention — not efficacy-grounding).

## Named-prescriber / guideline protocols

No single named-prescriber "off-label protocol" supersedes the FDA label for tirzepatide, which is the unusual case for this library: because tirzepatide is an approved Rx with label-mandated titration, the authoritative dosing is the label itself ([1]–[3] above), and prescribers titrate within it. The principal practitioner-level *deviations* from the label that circulate are the **compounded-supply** phenomena described above — reported as practice/regulatory phenomena, not grounded as a validated protocol. The one perioperative-practice item with a named multi-society source — 2024 multi-society guidance favoring shared decision-making, with a suggested ~1-week hold for weekly formulations if a hold is chosen — is a safety/handling convention carried in the research report (§5.5), not a dosing protocol. No additional named-prescriber or society protocol with a distinct, citable tirzepatide dose/cycle beyond the label was relied upon here; this is reported honestly rather than fabricated.
