## Section E — Regulatory & sport status

Tirzepatide is a once-weekly dual GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide) and GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) receptor agonist marketed by Eli Lilly. It is important to understand at the outset that tirzepatide is sold under **two distinct brand names corresponding to two separate, independently approved sets of indications**: **Mounjaro** (type 2 diabetes) and **Zepbound** (obesity/chronic weight management, and obstructive sleep apnea in obesity). These are the same molecule under different trade names, NDAs, and approved-use labels — a distinction that matters for prescribing, coverage, and any claim about "what tirzepatide is approved for." `[regulatory]`

### FDA approvals (United States)

**1. Mounjaro — type 2 diabetes (May 2022).** The FDA first approved tirzepatide as **Mounjaro** on **May 13, 2022**, making it the first and only GIP/GLP-1 dual receptor agonist approved for type 2 diabetes. The approved indication is as **"an adjunct to diet and exercise to improve glycemic control in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus."** The pediatric indication was subsequently extended to patients aged 10 years and older. Mounjaro is administered by once-weekly subcutaneous injection. `[regulatory]` `[1][2]`

**2. Zepbound — chronic weight management / obesity (November 2023).** The FDA approved a second brand, **Zepbound**, on **November 8, 2023**, for chronic weight management. The approved indication is for use **"in combination with a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity"** in **adults with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) or overweight (BMI ≥ 27 kg/m²) with at least one weight-related comorbid condition** (e.g., hypertension, type 2 diabetes, or dyslipidemia). This was a **separate NDA and a separate approval from Mounjaro** — the same active ingredient, but a distinct brand for the obesity indication. `[regulatory]` `[3]`

**3. Zepbound — moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea in obesity (December 2024).** On **December 20, 2024**, the FDA approved an additional indication for **Zepbound: the treatment of moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) in adults with obesity**, again as an adjunct to a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity. This made tirzepatide **the first-ever drug approved by the FDA for obstructive sleep apnea**, which had previously been managed only with devices (e.g., CPAP) and surgery. The approval followed the **SURMOUNT-OSA** trial program, in which roughly half of treated participants achieved disease remission or mild OSA with resolution of symptoms after 52 weeks, with statistically significant reductions in the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) versus placebo. The approval received Fast Track, Breakthrough Therapy, and Priority Review designations. `[regulatory]` `[4][5]`

The current FDA-approved Zepbound prescribing label (DailyMed) confirms both indications and specifies dosing differences: maintenance doses of 5, 10, or 15 mg once weekly for weight management, versus **10 mg or 15 mg once weekly for OSA** (the lowest maintenance dose is not approved for OSA). The label also carries a boxed warning for thyroid C-cell tumors and is contraindicated in patients with a personal/family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2. `[regulatory]` `[6]`

**Summary of the brand/indication split:**
1. Mounjaro → type 2 diabetes (May 2022)
2. Zepbound → chronic weight management/obesity (Nov 2023)
3. Zepbound → moderate-to-severe OSA in obesity (Dec 2024)

### EMA / Europe

Tirzepatide is approved in the European Union. The **European Commission granted marketing authorisation for Mounjaro (tirzepatide) on September 26, 2022**, following a positive CHMP opinion in July 2022, with an indication **"as an adjunct to diet and exercise for the treatment of adults with insufficiently controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus."** In Europe, Mounjaro's label was subsequently broadened to include weight management (CHMP positive opinion November 2023) and, more recently, type 2 diabetes in children and adolescents aged 10 and older. Note that, unlike the U.S., the EU markets the molecule primarily under the single **Mounjaro** brand across indications rather than splitting into a separate obesity brand. `[regulatory]` `[7]`

### The shortage and compounded tirzepatide (the 2022–2025 arc)

Because tirzepatide demand massively outstripped supply, the FDA listed tirzepatide on its **drug shortage list beginning in late 2022**. Under U.S. law, a drug's presence on the FDA shortage list permits **503A pharmacies** and **503B outsourcing facilities** to compound "essentially a copy" of the commercial product — which is what fueled the large gray market of compounded tirzepatide sold through telehealth and weight-loss clinics. `[regulatory]` `[8]`

The wind-down arc proceeded as follows:
1. **October 3, 2024** — FDA first removed tirzepatide from the shortage list, declaring the shortage resolved.
2. **October 7–11, 2024** — The **Outsourcing Facilities Association (OFA)** and North American Custom Laboratories **sued the FDA**; a court **remanded** the decision for re-evaluation.
3. **December 19, 2024** — After re-evaluation, the FDA **reaffirmed that the tirzepatide shortage was resolved**, concluding that **"supply meets or exceeds current demand, and . . . will also meet or exceed projected demand."**
4. **Enforcement wind-down:** the FDA gave **503A pharmacies a 60-day grace period ending February 18, 2025**, and **503B outsourcing facilities a 90-day grace period ending March 19, 2025**, after which it intended to resume enforcement against compounded copies. `[regulatory]` `[8][9]`

This tirzepatide wind-down **preceded the parallel semaglutide compounding wind-down** (the semaglutide shortage was declared resolved in February 2025). The OFA continued litigation against both determinations, but courts denied preliminary injunctions and the enforcement deadlines held. After the grace periods, 503A pharmacies may compound tirzepatide only under narrow circumstances (e.g., documented individual medical necessity, such as a clinically justified non-commercial dose or formulation), and may not compound "essentially a copy" of the commercial product; 503B facilities generally cannot compound it at all unless it returns to shortage or is added to the 503B bulks list. `[regulatory]` `[8][9]`

The FDA has issued repeated **safety warnings about compounded and counterfeit tirzepatide**. Concerns include products with the wrong or no active ingredient, **dosing errors** from patients self-measuring from multidose vials (some requiring hospitalization), and unapproved products falsely labeled "for research purposes only" / "not for human consumption." As of mid-2025, the FDA reported on the order of **1,150 adverse-event reports** related to compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide combined (with several hundred specifically tied to compounded tirzepatide). The agency urges consumers to obtain tirzepatide only as the brand-name FDA-approved product (Mounjaro/Zepbound) through state-licensed pharmacies. `[regulatory]` `[10]`

### WADA / sport status

**Tirzepatide is NOT on the WADA Prohibited List.** It is not a prohibited substance in or out of competition and does not appear in any prohibited class on the World Anti-Doping Agency's 2026 Prohibited List. `[regulatory]` `[11]`

However, GLP-1/GIP receptor agonists have entered WADA's **Monitoring Program**, which is a surveillance mechanism — **not a ban**. Per the WADA 2026 list materials, **"From 1 January 2026, markers of semaglutide and additionally of tirzepatide will be monitored to observe possible misuse of semaglutide and tirzepatide in-competition and out-of-competition."** Monitoring Program substances remain **fully permitted** for athletes; WADA simply tracks usage prevalence to inform whether future prohibition might be warranted. This was widely noted in connection with the 2026 Winter Olympics. The distinction is important: presence on the **Monitoring Program ≠ prohibition**, and athletes are not sanctioned for tirzepatide use under current rules. `[regulatory]` `[11][12]`

Bottom line for sport: an athlete using tirzepatide for an approved medical indication is **not committing an anti-doping rule violation** under the 2026 framework, but their use may be detected via WADA's monitoring of biomarkers. `[regulatory]` `[11][12]`

### Cost and access (qualitative)

Tirzepatide is an expensive, brand-only patented medicine; U.S. list prices for Mounjaro and Zepbound are on the order of roughly a thousand dollars per month before insurance, rebates, or manufacturer savings programs. Insurance coverage is uneven: diabetes (Mounjaro) coverage is more established than obesity (Zepbound) coverage, and many U.S. payers — including, historically, Medicare for the obesity indication — restrict or exclude coverage for weight management, creating substantial out-of-pocket burden. The OSA indication may broaden coverage where sleep apnea is a documented medical condition. The combination of high cost, patchy coverage, and prior shortages is precisely what drove demand into the now-curtailed compounded market. Lilly has also offered lower-cost self-pay single-dose vial channels for Zepbound to widen access.
