## Section F — Practitioner-practice + non-English

Scope note: This section reports dosing/titration from the FDA-approved labels (authoritative `regulatory`), the shortage-era compounding and access landscape (practice/regulatory **context only**), conventional muscle-preservation adjuncts, cost (qualitative), and a non-English-literature survey. Per the §B/C/D tier rules, **nothing in this section grounds efficacy or adverse-event rates** — those remain owned by Tier-1 controlled-trial evidence elsewhere in the wiki. Practitioner and vendor sources are never used to assert numeric efficacy or AE incidence.

### Dosing & titration

Tirzepatide is a once-weekly subcutaneous dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist. Both FDA labels — **Mounjaro** (type 2 diabetes) and **Zepbound** (chronic weight management; obstructive sleep apnea) — specify an identical escalation ladder, and the titration is deliberately slow to mitigate gastrointestinal adverse reactions. `[regulatory]`

The label-mandated ladder is **2.5 mg once weekly for 4 weeks → 5 mg → 7.5 mg → 10 mg → 12.5 mg → 15 mg**, increasing in 2.5 mg increments after at least 4 weeks at each dose, to a maximum of **15 mg once weekly**. The Mounjaro label states the starting dose is "2.5 mg injected subcutaneously once weekly" and that "**the 2.5 mg dosage is for treatment initiation and is not intended for glycemic control**"; after 4 weeks the dose increases to 5 mg, then "increase the dosage in 2.5 mg increments after at least 4 weeks on the current dose." `[regulatory]` [1] The Zepbound label uses the same ladder and states "the 2.5 mg dosage is for treatment initiation and **is not approved as a maintenance dosage**," with maximum dosage "15 mg injected subcutaneously once weekly … for all indications." `[regulatory]` [2][3] The 2.5 mg step is thus a non-therapeutic initiation rung — its purpose is GI acclimation, not efficacy.

Both labels frame the schedule explicitly around tolerability: dose escalation is to follow the prescribed steps "to reduce the risk of gastrointestinal adverse reactions." `[regulatory]` [1][3] Administration is once weekly on the same day each week, any time of day, with or without meals, by SC injection in the abdomen, thigh, or upper arm with site rotation. `[regulatory]` [1] Available single-dose strengths are 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, and 15 mg per 0.5 mL (pen or vial). `[regulatory]` [1][2]

Indication-specific maintenance targets (Zepbound): for chronic weight management the approved maintenance doses are **5, 10, or 15 mg** weekly; for moderate-to-severe OSA in adults with obesity, **10 or 15 mg** weekly. `[regulatory]` [2] In pediatric type 2 diabetes (≥10 years) the Mounjaro maximum is **10 mg** weekly rather than 15 mg. `[regulatory]` [1] Clinically, prescribers commonly hold patients at the lowest effective dose and do not auto-advance to 15 mg if response and tolerability are adequate at a lower rung; the label permits any of the maintenance doses, so up-titration is individualized rather than obligatory.

### Compounded / access

During the 2022–2024 FDA-listed shortage, compounded tirzepatide became widely available through 503A pharmacies and 503B outsourcing facilities, frequently dispensed via telehealth platforms at multi-dose vials and often co-formulated with additives (e.g., vitamin B12 / niacinamide). This is reported here as **practice and regulatory context, not as evidence of efficacy or safety equivalence** — compounded product is not FDA-reviewed for efficacy, potency, or AE rates. `[practitioner_protocol]` [7]

The legal basis shifted sharply once the shortage resolved. On **December 19, 2024**, after a court-ordered re-evaluation, the FDA again determined the tirzepatide shortage resolved, concluding "supply meets or exceeds current demand … and will also meet or exceed projected demand." `[regulatory]` [4] FDA set enforcement wind-down periods: **60 days for 503A pharmacies (ending ~February 18, 2025)** and **90 days for 503B outsourcing facilities (ending ~March 19, 2025)**, during which it did not intend to act against compounding "essentially a copy" of branded tirzepatide. `[regulatory]` [4] After these periods, 503B facilities generally cannot compound tirzepatide (it must be on the shortage list or the 503B Bulks List), while 503A pharmacies may compound only under narrow circumstances such as documented patient-specific medical necessity — **not** as routine copies of the approved product. `[practitioner_protocol]` [4] The shortage removal was litigated (Outsourcing Facilities Association and a custom-compounding plaintiff sued FDA in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas, October 2024); the court remanded for re-evaluation, FDA reaffirmed resolution, and subsequent reporting indicates the agency's position was upheld and the compounding wind-down proceeded. `[regulatory]` [4][7] **This section does not endorse current legality of compounded tirzepatide; readers should treat large-volume "compounded tirzepatide" offerings post-2025 as outside the routine compounding exemption.**

Counterfeit and salt-form caution: FDA warns that gray-market and unapproved GLP-1 products carry real risk. The agency states that **tirzepatide salt forms — tirzepatide acetate and tirzepatide sodium — are not approved and are not the active ingredient** in Mounjaro/Zepbound; FDA "is not aware of any basis for compounding using these salts" and lacks data that they share the chemical/pharmacologic properties of the approved base. `[regulatory]` [5] Many products marketed online appear to be counterfeit (often imported), may contain incorrect or harmful ingredients or inconsistent dosing, and FDA has issued large numbers of warning letters to compounders/telehealth marketers and received adverse-event reports tied to compounded versions. `[regulatory]` [5] Dosing-error risk is elevated with multi-dose vials and "units"/mg conversions versus the fixed-dose pens. `[vendor_label]` (dose/format caution only) [6]

Cost (qualitative): brand tirzepatide is expensive at list price — roughly **~$1,000+ per 28-day supply** for both Mounjaro and Zepbound pens. `[practitioner_protocol]` [6][8] Manufacturer self-pay channels (e.g., LillyDirect single-dose vials) lowered out-of-pocket cost substantially for cash payers — reported tiers on the order of **~$299–$499/month** depending on dose/refill timing — and commercial-insured patients with coverage may pay far less via savings cards. `[practitioner_protocol]` [8] During the shortage, compounded tirzepatide was typically marketed at a fraction of brand price, which drove much of its uptake; with the compounding wind-down, the qualitative cost gap between legitimate brand access and (now-restricted) compounded supply has narrowed, and remaining low-cost gray-market offerings carry the counterfeit/salt-form risks above. `[practitioner_protocol]` [6][7]

### Muscle-preservation adjuncts

Tirzepatide produces large total-weight loss, and a portion of that loss is lean (fat-free) mass — a generic feature of substantial caloric-deficit weight loss rather than a tirzepatide-specific toxicity. In the **SURMOUNT-1 DXA substudy**, roughly **~75% of weight lost was fat mass and ~25% lean mass** (mean lean-mass change about −5.6 kg, −10.9% over 72 weeks), and the proportion of body weight that was lean tissue actually rose. `[practitioner_protocol]` [9] An MRI analysis within SURPASS-3 found fat-free muscle-volume reductions "small and within expected ranges" relative to UK Biobank reference data. `[practitioner_protocol]` [9] Because the pivotal trials provided lifestyle advice but did **not** mandate structured resistance training, the lean-mass numbers reflect minimal-exercise conditions. `[practitioner_protocol]` [9]

The conventional practitioner adjuncts to attenuate lean-mass loss are **progressive resistance training plus adequate dietary protein** during the deficit. This is a standard recommendation rather than a tirzepatide-label requirement, and the controlled evidence for the *adjuncts specifically combined with tirzepatide* is still limited (dedicated trials are ongoing). It is reported here as practice convention; it does not modify the efficacy/AE claims in earlier sections. `[practitioner_protocol]` [9]

### Non-English literature

**Result: no distinct non-English-LANGUAGE primary efficacy/safety trials were identified.** Tirzepatide's pivotal program is global and **English-published**. Trials enrolling non-English-speaking / Asian populations exist and are clinically important, but they are reported in English-language journals by (in part) non-English-speaking author groups — they are *not* non-English-language primaries:

- **SURMOUNT-J** — Japanese adults with obesity disease; multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 — published in **English** in *The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology* (2024). `[regulatory-adjacent / Tier-1 venue, not used here for numbers]` [10]
- **SURMOUNT-CN** — Chinese adults with obesity, 52-week phase 3 RCT (10 mg, 15 mg, placebo) — published in **English** in *JAMA* (2024; lead author L. Zhao, Fudan/Zhongshan) — body-weight change −13.6% (10 mg) and −17.5% (15 mg) vs −2.3% placebo. `[Tier-1 venue, cited only to characterize the literature, not to ground §B/C/D claims]` [11]
- **SURPASS J-mono / J-combo** — tirzepatide in Japanese T2D — English-published phase 3 trials.

Genuinely non-English-language *documents* that do exist are **regulatory/practitioner** materials, not new primary evidence: Japanese-language package-insert / interview-form (添付文書/インタビューフォーム) material for マンジャロ／ゼップバウンド, and Chinese-language drug-information pages for 替尔泊肽. These mirror the same label content and add no independent efficacy or AE data. `[regulatory]` [12]

Honest bottom line: the non-English signal is one of **localized regulatory documentation and English-published trials in non-Western populations**, not a body of distinct non-English-language primary literature. No non-English source was used to support any efficacy or adverse-event-rate claim.
