# Section A — Physiology & What Serum Albumin Measures

## Overview

Serum albumin is the most abundant protein in human plasma, synthesized exclusively by hepatocytes, and serves as the principal determinant of plasma oncotic pressure as well as the body's primary transport and antioxidant scaffold in the extracellular compartment. Its long plasma half-life (~19–21 days) means that a measured serum level reflects weeks of integrated hepatic output, not acute change — a fact that governs every interpretive judgment made at the lab bench or bedside [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review].

---

## Molecular Identity and Structure

Human serum albumin (HSA) is a single polypeptide of 585 amino acids with a molecular weight of approximately 66.5 kDa [1, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review]. It is non-glycosylated, carries a net negative charge at physiological pH, and folds into three homologous alpha-helical domains (I, II, III) stabilized by 17 disulfide bridges formed among 34 of its 35 cysteine residues. The single remaining cysteine — Cys34, located in domain I — is not engaged in a disulfide bond and exists as a free thiol, giving it unique chemical reactivity (see antioxidant section below) [3, mechanism_review].

---

## Synthesis: Exclusively Hepatic, Large Functional Reserve

Albumin is synthesized exclusively by the parenchymal cells of the liver (hepatocytes) [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review]. Under normal conditions only 20–30% of hepatocytes are actively producing albumin at any given moment, yet healthy adults generate approximately 10–15 g of albumin per day — roughly 10–25% of total hepatic protein synthesis [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review; 4, mechanism_review]. This arrangement constitutes a large functional reserve: synthetic output can increase by 200–300% in response to sustained demand (e.g., protein loss states) [2, mechanism_review]. The precursor is pre-pro-albumin, processed to pro-albumin in the rough endoplasmic reticulum and then to mature albumin in the Golgi apparatus before secretion into the hepatic sinusoid.

Synthesis rate is regulated by nutritional supply (amino acid availability, particularly essential amino acids), hormonal signals (insulin, cortisol, and growth hormone are stimulatory), and colloid osmotic pressure of the interstitium (a drop in interstitial oncotic pressure — signaling protein loss — increases synthesis) [1, mechanism_review; 5, mechanism_review]. The inhibitory signals are covered in the acute-phase section below.

---

## Half-Life and the "Weeks-Not-Days" Interpretive Rule

The plasma half-life of albumin is approximately 19–21 days in healthy adults [1, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review]. This long half-life arises because albumin is not filtered by the glomerulus under physiological conditions and undergoes slow, distributed catabolism (40–60% degraded in skeletal muscle, liver, and kidney) [4, mechanism_review]. A key clinical corollary: a single acute insult — inflammation, surgery, brief fasting — will not produce a detectable fall in serum albumin for days to weeks. Conversely, serum albumin does not "bounce back" quickly even when the underlying cause is corrected; recovery tracks at the pace of new synthesis over weeks. This makes serum albumin an excellent marker of chronic nutritional status and sustained hepatic synthetic function, but a poor marker of acute events [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review].

The commonly cited "circulatory half-life" of 16–18 hours refers to the exchange rate between vascular and extravascular compartments, not the half-life of the protein itself [1, mechanism_review].

---

## Distribution: Mostly Extravascular

Only 30–40% of total body albumin resides within the vascular compartment (roughly 118 g intravascular in a 70 kg adult); the remaining 60–70% (approximately 242 g) is distributed in the interstitial space of tissues, primarily skeletal muscle and skin [3, mechanism_review; 4, mechanism_review]. Albumin crosses the capillary wall continuously via transcapillary escape at a rate of approximately 4–5% of the intravascular pool per hour, and is returned to the circulation via lymphatic drainage [2, mechanism_review; 4, mechanism_review]. This large extravascular reservoir means that redistribution — capillary leak in sepsis, resuscitation-related plasma dilution, or protein shift into third spaces — can sharply lower serum albumin concentrations independently of any change in hepatic synthesis. The serum albumin level therefore = synthesis (liver) − losses (renal, gastrointestinal) − catabolism − dilution (plasma volume expansion) − redistribution (transcapillary leak) [1, mechanism_review; 4, mechanism_review].

## Principal Physiological Functions

### 1. Plasma Colloid Osmotic (Oncotic) Pressure

Albumin is the principal determinant of plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure, contributing approximately 70–80% of the total [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review]. This dominance arises from three factors: its high molar concentration (albumin is present at ~600 µmol/L, far exceeding globulins), its strong net negative charge (Gibbs-Donnan effect), and its molecular size, which restricts passage across the endothelium under normal conditions. A fall in serum albumin from 4.0 g/dL (40 g/L) to 2.5 g/dL (25 g/L) reduces oncotic pressure enough to favor net capillary filtration over reabsorption, resulting in edema [1, mechanism_review; 4, mechanism_review].

### 2. Transport of Endogenous Ligands

Albumin's three-domain architecture provides multiple distinct binding sites for a wide range of hydrophobic and amphipathic molecules:

- **Free fatty acids (FFA):** Albumin binds long-chain fatty acids at up to 7 high-affinity sites; at physiological FFA concentrations it carries the large majority of circulating unesterified fatty acids, shuttling them between adipose tissue, liver, and muscle [3, mechanism_review; 5, mechanism_review].
- **Unconjugated bilirubin:** Albumin is the primary transport vehicle for bilirubin from peripheral tissues to the liver for conjugation and excretion; tight binding keeps free (toxic) bilirubin at nanomolar levels [3, mechanism_review].
- **Calcium:** Approximately 40–45% of total serum calcium circulates bound to albumin [4, mechanism_review]. This albumin-bound fraction is biologically inactive but constitutes the reservoir from which ionized (free, active) calcium is maintained. Changes in serum albumin concentration shift total calcium without altering ionized calcium — requiring the "corrected calcium" adjustment when interpreting calcium results in hypoalbuminemic patients.
- **Thyroid hormones and cortisol:** A fraction of circulating thyroxine (T4) and cortisol is albumin-bound; the remainder binds specific carriers (TBG, CBG). Albumin binding is lower-affinity but high-capacity and contributes meaningfully to the total pool [3, mechanism_review].
- **Drugs:** Albumin is the dominant pharmacokinetic binding protein for the majority of drugs in clinical use, including warfarin, NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen), furosemide, antibiotics, and many others [3, mechanism_review; 5, mechanism_review]. Albumin binding sequesters the drug from the receptor compartment — only the free (unbound) fraction is pharmacologically active. Hypoalbuminemia therefore raises free drug concentrations and potentiates toxicity for highly protein-bound narrow-therapeutic-index agents.

### 3. Antioxidant and Redox Buffer: The Cys34 Thiol

Albumin is the major plasma antioxidant by mass, accounting for approximately 70–80% of total extracellular antioxidant capacity [5, mechanism_review]. This function is localized primarily to the single free thiol of Cys34, which acts as a "trap" for reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) — stoichiometrically scavenging hydrogen peroxide (forming sulfenic acid), hypochlorous acid, peroxynitrite, and superoxide anion [5, mechanism_review]. Because Cys34 is the largest free-thiol pool in whole blood, it functions as the principal plasma redox buffer. In oxidative stress states (sepsis, chronic kidney disease, liver failure), an increasing proportion of albumin Cys34 is found in irreversibly oxidized forms (sulfinic acid, sulfonic acid), impairing this protective function [5, mechanism_review].

Beyond Cys34, albumin contributes to antioxidant defense through: metal-binding (Cu²⁺, Fe³⁺, sequestered from Fenton chemistry); quenching of lipid peroxidation via methionine residues; and binding of hydrophobic oxidants [3, mechanism_review; 5, mechanism_review].

### 4. Acid-Base Contribution

Albumin bears multiple titratable histidine residues and carries a net negative charge; it accounts for approximately 75% of the unmeasured anion gap in plasma. This is quantitatively significant: a fall in serum albumin of 1 g/dL (10 g/L) lowers the anion gap by approximately 2.5–3 mEq/L. Failure to correct the anion gap for hypoalbuminemia is a known clinical error that masks elevated-anion-gap acidoses [1, mechanism_review; 4, mechanism_review].

## Albumin as a Negative Acute-Phase Reactant

During infection, injury, or systemic inflammation, the liver undergoes a wholesale reprioritization of protein synthesis — the acute-phase response. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, principally interleukin-6 (IL-6), travel from the site of inflammation to hepatocytes where they activate JAK/STAT3 and NF-κB signaling cascades [6, mechanism_review]. The result is a sharp upregulation of positive acute-phase proteins (C-reactive protein, serum amyloid A, fibrinogen, haptoglobin, α1-antitrypsin) at the direct expense of constitutive secretory proteins including albumin, transferrin, and fibronectin [6, mechanism_review; 1, mechanism_review]. Albumin is therefore classified as a **negative acute-phase reactant**: its serum level falls within hours to days of inflammatory activation, entirely independently of nutritional status or liver parenchymal integrity.

The practical consequence is a confounding layer that cannot be ignored when interpreting a low serum albumin: any acute or chronic inflammatory condition (CRP elevation, active infection, malignancy, autoimmune disease) will suppress albumin synthesis and lower the serum level regardless of protein intake or hepatocyte mass [6, mechanism_review; 1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review]. Investigators and clinicians must treat serum albumin as a composite signal: a low value reflects some combination of impaired synthesis (malnutrition, liver disease), excess losses (nephrotic syndrome, protein-losing enteropathy), plasma volume expansion, redistribution, and — often dominantly in hospitalized patients — inflammation-driven suppression.

---

## Reference Range and Units

In healthy adults, serum albumin is typically reported as **3.5–5.0 g/dL** (35–50 g/L) [1, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review]. Hypoalbuminemia is conventionally defined as a serum albumin < 3.5 g/dL (< 35 g/L); severe hypoalbuminemia < 2.5 g/dL (< 25 g/L) carries substantial mortality risk across diagnostic categories. Values are routinely reported in g/dL in the US; g/L is the SI unit (conversion: g/dL × 10 = g/L).
