# Section B: Reference Ranges, Units & Interpretation

## Units and Conversion

Serum albumin is reported in **g/dL in the United States** (conventional units) and in **g/L in SI-unit countries**. The conversion is exact: multiply g/dL by 10 to get g/L (e.g., 4.0 g/dL = 40 g/L). Both notations appear throughout the clinical literature and in composite scoring formulas; always confirm which unit a calculator or scoring system expects before entering a value.

## Reference Interval in Adults

The conventional adult reference interval is approximately **3.5–5.0 g/dL (35–50 g/L)**, though the exact limits are method- and laboratory-dependent [1, mechanism_review]. Survey data from clinical laboratories using bromocresol green (BCG) and bromocresol purple (BCP) dye-binding methods show lower limits ranging from 30 to 37 g/L and upper limits from 47 to 55 g/L depending on the platform and whether the range was locally derived or adopted from the manufacturer [1, mechanism_review]. BCG systematically overestimates albumin relative to immunoturbidimetric reference methods — particularly at low concentrations — which can shift apparent reference limits upward and cause patients with true hypoalbuminemia to be missed [2, mechanism_review]. BCP agrees more closely with immunological reference methods and is preferred in nephrology settings [2, mechanism_review]. Ionized-calcium-corrected or immunoassay-based albumin measurements are the reference standard for research; in routine practice, any reported interval should be interpreted against the method used by the issuing laboratory.

**Age-related variation.** Albumin decreases modestly but measurably with advancing age. A prospective study of 2,342 healthy, non-institutionalized adults aged 50–89 found that albumin declined with increasing age in both men and women independently of health status, and that most older adults (70–89 years) still fell within the reference range of the 50–69 age group — yet each standard-deviation decline in albumin carried an independently elevated mortality odds ratio of 1.24 (p = 0.04) [3, cohort]. Clinically, the lower end of normal is often taken as approximately 3.4–3.5 g/dL (34–35 g/L) in elderly individuals; some laboratories apply an age-stratified interval.

**Pregnancy.** Albumin falls progressively through gestation as a direct consequence of plasma volume expansion — a 40–50% rise in plasma volume dilutes circulating proteins even while hepatic albumin synthesis increases by approximately 50% [4, mechanism_review]. Mean values decline from ~4.5 g/dL (45 g/L) pre-pregnancy to around 3.2–3.5 g/dL (32–35 g/L) by the third trimester; one reference interval study in a pregnant cohort found 3.4–4.8 g/dL for the first trimester, with further decline in the second and third trimesters [11, mechanism_review]. A value that is unremarkable in a non-pregnant adult may therefore reflect true hypoalbuminemia in the context of pregnancy, and pre-eclampsia can drive albumin 15% below levels seen in healthy pregnancy.

## Hypoalbuminemia Thresholds

| Category | Threshold (g/dL) | Threshold (g/L) | Clinical correlate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower limit of normal | < 3.5 | < 35 | Hypoalbuminemia by definition |
| Mild hypoalbuminemia | 3.0–3.5 | 30–35 | Increased risk of poor surgical outcomes, impaired wound healing |
| Moderate hypoalbuminemia | 2.5–3.0 | 25–30 | Oncotic pressure meaningfully impaired |
| Marked hypoalbuminemia | < 2.5 | < 25 | High risk of edema and ascites formation; oncotic pressure unable to retain fluid intravascularly |

At albumin < 2.5 g/dL (< 25 g/L), the colloid oncotic pressure generated by plasma proteins falls below the threshold necessary to counteract hydrostatic forces at the capillary wall, producing dependent edema and, in portal hypertension, ascites. This threshold is encoded in clinical composite scores (see below).

## The Multi-Factorial Interpretation Framework

**A low serum albumin is almost never primarily a reflection of dietary protein deficiency alone.** Albumin is a negative acute-phase reactant: its hepatic synthesis is downregulated by the same cytokines — IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1 — that upregulate C-reactive protein and other positive acute-phase proteins [5, mechanism_review]. In inflammatory states, capillary permeability also increases, shifting albumin from the intravascular to the interstitial compartment and increasing its degradation rate, so that plasma albumin falls even when synthesis may be normal or elevated. The four primary determinants of serum albumin are:

1. **Liver synthetic function** — reduced in cirrhosis, acute hepatitis, hepatic failure
2. **Inflammatory state** — cytokine-driven suppression of albumin gene transcription plus capillary escape (infection, surgery, trauma, cancer, autoimmune disease)
3. **Protein loss** — renal (nephrotic syndrome, where urinary albumin losses can be massive), gastrointestinal (protein-losing enteropathy, extensive burns), or external
4. **Volume/dilutional status** — fluid overload dilutes albumin; plasma volume expansion in pregnancy; conversely, dehydration concentrates it (see Hyperalbuminemia below)

In practice, most hospitalized patients with hypoalbuminemia have an inflammatory component as the dominant driver; nutrition support alone will not normalize albumin while significant inflammation persists. True isolated nutritional hypoalbuminemia is unusual outside of prolonged starvation or severe malabsorption and is always a diagnosis of exclusion [5, mechanism_review].

## Hyperalbuminemia

Albumin above the upper reference limit (> 5.0 g/dL / > 50 g/L) is almost invariably caused by **dehydration or hemoconcentration**, not by excess protein intake or a disease-driven increase in hepatic synthesis. A prolonged venous tourniquet at blood draw is a frequent pre-analytical artifact that can falsely elevate albumin by several grams per deciliter. True pathological hyperalbuminemia does not exist as a clinical entity; if a value exceeds the upper reference limit after ruling out specimen artifact, dehydration is the expected cause and should be assessed clinically.

## Albumin-Corrected Calcium

Approximately 40–45% of total serum calcium circulates bound to albumin. When albumin is low, a measured total calcium will underestimate the physiologically active ionized fraction; when albumin is high (dehydration), total calcium will overstate it. The **Payne formula** (1973) is the historically most used correction:

> **Corrected calcium (mg/dL) = measured total calcium (mg/dL) + 0.8 × [4.0 − albumin (g/dL)]**

In SI units the analogous expression is:

> **Corrected calcium (mmol/L) = measured total calcium (mmol/L) + 0.02 × [40 − albumin (g/L)]**

**Important caveat.** Multiple large retrospective studies have shown that albumin-corrected calcium performs poorly as a proxy for ionized calcium, particularly in patients with renal failure, critical illness, or severe hypoalbuminemia, where the correction systematically overestimates free calcium [6, mechanism_review]. Studies reviewed in the literature report that uncorrected total calcium correlates with ionized calcium (ICC 0.78) better than the Payne-corrected value (ICC 0.73), and classifies calcium status correctly in approximately 82% of cases versus the formula's lower performance (reported in the literature reviewed by [6, mechanism_review]). The current recommended approach is to measure **ionized calcium directly** whenever a clinical decision depends on it; the Payne formula may be used as a rapid bedside screen but should not replace direct measurement in ambiguous cases [6, mechanism_review].

## Albumin Correction of the Anion Gap

Albumin carries a substantial net negative charge and is the largest single contributor to the anion gap (AG). Hypoalbuminemia therefore **lowers the apparent anion gap**, potentially masking a coexisting anion-gap metabolic acidosis. The correction derived by Figge et al. from critically ill patients is:

> **Adjusted AG = observed AG + 2.5 × (4.0 − albumin [g/dL])**

Equivalently, using g/L: **Adjusted AG = observed AG + 0.25 × (40 − albumin [g/L])**

Each 1 g/dL (10 g/L) drop in albumin below 4.0 g/dL (40 g/L) reduces the expected AG by approximately 2.5 mEq/L (r² = 0.94 in the original ICU dataset) [7, cohort]. In a hypercatabolic patient where albumin is 2.0 g/dL, an "apparently normal" AG of 10 mEq/L may reflect a true AG of ~15 mEq/L when corrected — clinically significant and consistent with a masked lactate acidosis or ketoacidosis.

## Composite Scores Using Albumin

**Child-Pugh score.** The Child-Turcotte-Pugh (CTP) score quantifies cirrhosis severity from five components: serum albumin, serum bilirubin, prothrombin time/INR, severity of ascites, and grade of hepatic encephalopathy. Albumin is scored 1 (> 3.5 g/dL / > 35 g/L), 2 (2.8–3.5 g/dL / 28–35 g/L), or 3 (< 2.8 g/dL / < 28 g/L). Total scores map to grades A (compensated, score 5–6), B (significantly compromised, 7–9), and C (decompensated, 10–15) [8, mechanism_review]. Note that albumin < 2.8 g/dL (< 28 g/L) attracts the maximum CTP albumin score, reflecting severe synthetic failure [8, mechanism_review].

**Albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) grade.** Proposed by Johnson et al. as a more objective alternative to CTP for hepatocellular carcinoma and liver disease prognosis, the ALBI score uses only two continuous laboratory variables:

> **ALBI score = 0.66 × log₁₀[total bilirubin (μmol/L)] − 0.085 × [albumin (g/L)]**

Grades: 1 (score ≤ −2.60), 2 (−2.60 to −1.39), 3 (> −1.39). Grade 1 indicates preserved liver function; Grade 3 indicates severe impairment. ALBI eliminates the subjective ascites and encephalopathy components of CTP and has demonstrated superior or equivalent prognostic discrimination across multiple liver disease contexts [9, mechanism_review] [12, mechanism_review].

**Nutritional indices.** The **Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI)** uses albumin and body weight relative to ideal body weight: GNRI = (1.489 × albumin [g/L]) + (41.7 × actual weight / ideal body weight). GNRI < 82 indicates severe nutritional risk; 82–91 moderate risk; 92–98 mild risk; > 98 no nutritional risk. The **Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI)** combines albumin with lymphocyte count: PNI = albumin (g/L) + 5 × total lymphocyte count (×10⁹/L), with PNI < 35 indicating severe nutritional risk. Both indices appear in surgical, oncological, and geriatric prognostication literature [10, cohort].
