# Section D: Determinants & Clinical Significance

Serum vitamin B12 status is determined by the interplay of dietary intake, a multi-step gastrointestinal absorption cascade, and tissue delivery via transcobalamin. Deficiency is common and mechanistically heterogeneous. Its neurological consequences can be permanent and can develop in the complete absence of anemia. Supraphysiologic B12 that is not exogenous is a paraneoplastic and hepatic red flag.

---

## Deficiency Causes

### Autoimmune: Pernicious Anemia

The archetype of B12 deficiency is **pernicious anemia (PA)** — loss of gastric parietal cells via anti-parietal-cell antibodies (~90% of PA) and anti-intrinsic-factor (IF) antibodies (50–70% of PA, high specificity). Without IF, neither food-bound nor crystalline B12 can be absorbed at ileal cubilin receptors [1, mechanism_review; 4, regulatory].

PA arises on a background of autoimmune (metaplastic) atrophic gastritis. Population prevalence is approximately 0.1%, rising to ~2% in adults over 60 and to 15–25% in those with confirmed autoimmune atrophic gastritis [1, mechanism_review]. PA clusters with other autoimmune diseases and is more common in women and Northern European ancestry populations.

### Atrophic Gastritis and Food-Bound Malabsorption

Non-autoimmune atrophic gastritis produces **food-bound cobalamin malabsorption**: gastric hypochlorhydria impairs B12 release from dietary protein, but IF is preserved — so crystalline (supplement) B12 is still absorbed normally [1, mechanism_review; 4, regulatory]. This is the dominant mechanism in older adults: atrophic gastritis and food-bound cobalamin malabsorption are common causes of B12 deficiency in older adults, together accounting for a substantial majority of cases in those ≥65 [2, mechanism_review; 3, cohort]. Other anatomic/surgical causes include total or partial gastrectomy and bariatric bypass (loss of parietal-cell mucosa), terminal ileal resection or Crohn's disease (cubilin receptor loss), exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (impaired haptocorrin cleavage), and bacterial overgrowth or *Diphyllobothrium latum* (competing luminal B12 uptake) [1, mechanism_review; 4, regulatory].

### Drugs: Metformin (Under-Recognized)

Metformin causes B12 malabsorption via **calcium-dependent ileal membrane antagonism** that impairs uptake of the B12–IF complex at cubilin receptors — an effect partially reversed by supplemental calcium [5, rct]. The association is dose- and duration-dependent.

de Jager et al. (2010, BMJ; N = 390, 4.3 years) randomized insulin-treated type 2 diabetes patients to metformin 850 mg three times daily vs. placebo. Metformin reduced serum B12 by a mean of 19%. Absolute risk of B12 deficiency (<150 pmol/L) was 7.2 percentage points higher in the metformin group (NNH 13.8 over 4.3 years) [5, rct].

The Diabetes Prevention Program Outcomes Study (Aroda et al. 2016, J Clin Endocrinol Metab; N = 2,155, up to 13 years) confirmed duration dependence: combined low/borderline-low B12 was 20.3% in the metformin group vs. 15.6% in placebo at 13 years (P = .02); OR for B12 deficiency per year of metformin use was 1.13 (95% CI 1.06–1.20). Peripheral neuropathy was elevated among metformin users with concurrent low B12 [6, rct]. Routine B12 monitoring during long-term metformin use is now guideline-endorsed [4, regulatory].

**PPIs and H2-blockers** replicate food-bound malabsorption through acid suppression; crystalline B12 is unaffected by these agents [1, mechanism_review; 4, regulatory].

### Dietary Deficiency

Cobalamin is found exclusively in animal-source foods. Strict vegans with zero animal-product intake and non-supplementing lacto-vegetarians are at progressively elevated risk as body stores (2–5 mg, primarily hepatic) deplete over months to years [1, mechanism_review]. A critical downstream risk is **breastfed infants of B12-deficient mothers**: Pawlak et al. (2018, Am J Clin Nutr; N = 74 lactating US women) demonstrated that vegan mothers not supplementing with individual B12 had significantly lower breast-milk B12 concentrations, directly threatening infant neurological development [7, cohort].

## Consequences of Deficiency

### Hematologic: Megaloblastic Anemia

B12 is required for DNA synthesis (via the methyl-folate trap and thymidylate synthesis). Deficiency produces **megaloblastic anemia**: macrocytic red cells (elevated MCV), hypersegmented neutrophils (the pathognomonic finding), elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin from ineffective erythropoiesis, and pancytopenia in severe cases [1, mechanism_review; 4, regulatory]. But anemia may be absent or mild — the critical clinical trap addressed below.

### Neurological: Subacute Combined Degeneration and Beyond

The neurological consequences of B12 deficiency are the most dangerous because they can be **irreversible if treatment is delayed**. As adenosylcobalamin, B12 is required for myelin synthesis via methylmalonyl-CoA mutase; deficiency produces progressive demyelination of the **dorsal columns** (proprioception and vibration sense) and **lateral corticospinal tracts** — the syndrome of **subacute combined degeneration (SCD) of the spinal cord** [1, mechanism_review; 4, regulatory]. Clinical features: paresthesias of the extremities (earliest), loss of vibration and joint-position sense, positive Romberg sign, sensory ataxia, spasticity, and in advanced cases paraparesis and bladder dysfunction. B12 deficiency also causes peripheral neuropathy, cognitive impairment, dementia, depression, and psychosis [1, mechanism_review].

### CRITICAL: Neurological Disease Without Anemia

**A normal CBC — including a normal MCV — does NOT exclude B12 deficiency or its neurological sequelae.** This is the most consequential clinical point in the entire biomarker entry.

In a prospective case series of 63 patients with B12 deficiency-related neurological syndromes (Aaron et al. 2005, Neurol India), 17.5% had both hemoglobin and MCV within the normal range at presentation [8, cohort]. After ≥6 months of parenteral B12 therapy, only 54% improved — confirming that delay permits irreversible damage [8, cohort]. The Stabler NEJM clinical practice review states explicitly: vitamin B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia, demyelinating disease, or both — and the two are dissociated in a clinically significant minority [1, mechanism_review].

**Because neurological injury from untreated deficiency can be permanent, clinical suspicion in the appropriate setting justifies empiric treatment without waiting for the hematological picture to declare itself.**

### THE FOLATE-MASKING TRAP

**High folate intake — from folic acid food fortification, multivitamins, or supplements — can correct the megaloblastic anemia of B12 deficiency while neurological damage silently progresses.** This is a load-bearing safety point and one of the most consequential pitfalls in nutritional medicine [1, mechanism_review; 4, regulatory; 9, cohort; 10, mechanism_review].

The mechanism: B12 deficiency traps dietary folate as 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (the "methyl-folate trap"), creating functional folate deficiency that impairs DNA synthesis and produces megaloblastic anemia. Exogenous folic acid bypasses this trap and restores DNA synthesis and red cell maturation — eliminating the hematological signal. But demyelination — driven by the separate adenosylcobalamin pathway — is not rescued by folate and continues regardless [1, mechanism_review].

This was recognized empirically in the 1940s–1950s when high-dose folic acid (>5 mg/day) was used to treat PA anemia; clinicians observed neurological deterioration following hematological remission. By the 1960s, treating B12 deficiency with folate alone was abandoned on these grounds (Reynolds 2002, J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry) [10, mechanism_review].

In the modern context of mandatory folic acid fortification (in force in ≥80 countries), Bailey et al. (2020, Am J Clin Nutr; NHANES, US adults ≥60) found that older adults with low B12 status and concurrent high folate had significantly worse performance on one cognitive domain (Digit Symbol Substitution; OR 2.87) than those with low B12 without excess folate — a potential but domain-specific and inconsistent association across the full cognitive battery [9, cohort].

**The operational rules:** (1) Never treat macrocytic anemia as folate deficiency alone without ruling out B12 deficiency first — folate monotherapy can unmask or accelerate neurological disease. (2) In any patient on routine folate supplementation who develops neurological symptoms, consider masked B12 deficiency. (3) Functional markers (MMA, holoTC) — not total serum B12 alone — are the appropriate tests when the folate-masking trap is a concern.

## High Serum B12

In a supplementing patient, elevated serum B12 is exogenous and benign: cobalamin is water-soluble with renal excretion, and no toxicity from dietary or supplemental intake has been documented at any realistic dose [1, mechanism_review].

**Persistently elevated B12 in a non-supplementing patient is a red flag.** The mechanism is overproduction or impaired clearance of haptocorrin (transcobalamin I/III) — the storage-binding proteins derived from granulocytes and released by hepatic disease. Three major categories:

1. **Liver disease** — cirrhosis, acute hepatitis, hepatocellular carcinoma: hepatocyte damage releases stored B12 and impairs haptocorrin clearance [11, meta_analysis].
2. **Myeloproliferative neoplasms** — CML, polycythemia vera, myelofibrosis: expanded granulocyte pool produces massive haptocorrin; CML can produce B12 levels 10-fold above normal [11, meta_analysis].
3. **Solid tumors (paraneoplastic)** — Urbanski et al. (2020, J Clin Med; 785 cases B12 ≥1000 ng/L vs. 785 matched controls) found aOR 4.21 (95% CI 2.67–6.64) for metastatic solid cancer, with the strongest signals in pancreatic (aOR 4.00), urothelial (aOR 7.40), and colorectal cancer (aOR 3.02) [12, cohort]. Lacombe et al. (2021, Sci Rep; N = 688) demonstrated that **persistent** B12 elevation ≥1000 ng/L on two measurements was associated with HR 5.90 (95% CI 2.79–12.45, P < 0.001) for subsequent solid cancer, while a single transient elevation showed no significant association [13, cohort]. A systematic review (Amado-Garzon et al. 2024, Cancer Invest) summarized risk ratios of 1.88–5.9 across studies [14, meta_analysis].

At the population level, Liu et al. (2024, Arch Gerontol Geriatr; 22 cohort studies, 92,346 individuals) found each 100 pmol/L increase in serum B12 associated with 4% higher all-cause mortality risk (6% in older adults); B12 >600 pmol/L was associated with adjusted HR 1.50 (95% CI 1.29–1.74) for all-cause mortality [11, meta_analysis].

**Clinical rule:** An unexplained persistently elevated serum B12 in a non-supplementing patient warrants evaluation for liver disease (LFTs, imaging), myeloproliferative neoplasm (CBC with differential, JAK2 mutation testing), and solid malignancy guided by clinical context. A threshold of ≥1000 ng/L warrants active investigation.

---

## Limitations

Total serum B12 has poor sensitivity for tissue-level deficiency (the grey zone of 150–300 pmol/L; functional confirmation with MMA and holoTC is often required). The anti-IF antibody assay has a false-negative rate of 30–50% in confirmed PA, so a negative result does not exclude the diagnosis [1, mechanism_review; 4, regulatory]. Neither MCV nor hemoglobin reliably excludes neurologically significant deficiency — concurrent iron deficiency (masking macrocytosis) and high folate (resolving megaloblastosis) both obscure the CBC signal. Interpretation requires integration of clinical risk factors, medication history, functional markers, and — when neurological symptoms are present — early treatment rather than diagnostic waiting.
