# Section A: Physiology & What Serum B12 Measures

## Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin): Structure and Biological Uniqueness

Vitamin B12 — cobalamin — is a cobalt-containing corrinoid and the largest, most structurally complex of all vitamins, with a molecular weight of approximately 1,355 Da. Its corrin ring coordinates a central cobalt atom, which can exist in three oxidation states (Co+, Co2+, Co3+) and carries upper (β) and lower (α) axial ligands. The biologically active cofactor forms are **methylcobalamin** (MeCbl) and **5′-deoxyadenosylcobalamin** (AdoCbl); the common supplemental and food-derived forms — cyanocobalamin and hydroxocobalamin — must be enzymatically converted into these active species intracellularly [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review]. Cobalamin is synthesized exclusively by bacteria and archaea; no plant, fungal, or animal organism can produce it de novo. Dietary sources are therefore entirely animal-origin: meat, fish, shellfish, dairy, and eggs — a fact with direct implications for vegetarians, vegans, and populations with low animal-food access [3, regulatory].

## The Two Human B12-Dependent Enzymes

Only **two enzymes** in the human proteome require cobalamin as a cofactor, yet each sits at a hub of central metabolism.

### 1. Methionine Synthase (Cytosolic; Methylcobalamin Cofactor)

Methionine synthase (MTR; EC 2.1.1.13) catalyzes the transfer of a methyl group from **5-methyltetrahydrofolate (5-methyl-THF)** to **homocysteine**, producing **methionine + tetrahydrofolate (THF)**:

> 5-methyl-THF + homocysteine → methionine + THF

This reaction is the biochemical node that links vitamin B12 to the **folate one-carbon cycle** and to the **methionine/SAM (S-adenosylmethionine) methylation network**. Methionine is the immediate precursor to SAM, the universal methyl donor for nearly 100 substrates including DNA, RNA, histones, lipids, and neurotransmitters. When B12 is deficient, methionine synthase stalls — and the cellular folate pool becomes locked in its methylated form. Because the reduction of 5,10-methyleneTHF to 5-methyl-THF is physiologically irreversible, this trapped methylfolate cannot re-enter the pathways supplying thymidylate (dTMP) and purine synthesis needed for DNA replication. The result is **impaired DNA synthesis despite adequate folate intake** — the "methyl-folate trap," the biochemical mechanism underlying the megaloblastic anemia that is clinically identical in B12 and folate deficiency [4, mechanism_review; 5, mechanism_review].

### 2. Methylmalonyl-CoA Mutase (Mitochondrial; Adenosylcobalamin Cofactor)

Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (MMUT; EC 5.4.99.2) converts **methylmalonyl-CoA → succinyl-CoA**, an isomerization requiring the AdoCbl cofactor:

> L-methylmalonyl-CoA → succinyl-CoA

Succinyl-CoA enters the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, supporting energy metabolism. Methylmalonyl-CoA accumulates whenever B12 is insufficient — derived from the catabolism of branched-chain amino acids (valine, isoleucine), odd-chain fatty acids, and cholesterol side chains. This accumulation is the biochemical basis of **methylmalonic acid (MMA)** elevation in serum and urine, which is why MMA is the most specific functional marker of tissue B12 depletion [1, mechanism_review; 4, mechanism_review].

## Dietary Sources and Hepatic Reserves: Why Deficiency Takes Years

Because all dietary B12 comes from animal-source foods, vegans and strict vegetarians face the highest dietary risk. The adult RDA is 2.4 µg/day (2.6 µg during pregnancy, 2.8 µg during lactation) [3, regulatory]. The liver concentrates and stores the body's B12, with total hepatic reserves estimated at **2–5 mg** (some sources cite 1–5 mg) in replete adults — roughly 2,000–5,000 times the daily requirement [3, regulatory; 6, mechanism_review]. Daily obligatory losses through bile and urine are small (approximately 1–3 µg/day), and biliary B12 is substantially reabsorbed via the enterohepatic circulation. The consequence: **dietary deficiency in a previously replete adult can take 3–5 years to manifest clinically**, since the stores must be exhausted before tissue deficiency emerges. This also means strict vegans who supplement inconsistently may deplete slowly and present insidiously [7, mechanism_review].

## The Absorption Cascade: Where Deficiency Arises

The absorption of food-bound B12 is an elaborate, multi-step process involving four distinct carrier proteins and two anatomically separate sites. Each step is a potential failure point, which maps directly onto the etiology of clinical deficiency.

**Step 1 — Gastric acid and pepsin liberate food-bound B12.**  
Dietary B12 is protein-bound; gastric acid (HCl) and protease (pepsin) are required to release free cobalamin from food. This step is impaired by proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs), H2-blockers, and atrophic gastritis [7, mechanism_review].

**Step 2 — Haptocorrin (R-binder) captures free B12 in the stomach.**  
Haptocorrin (HC; also called R-binder or transcobalamin I) is secreted by salivary glands and gastric mucosa. It binds free B12 with high affinity in the acidic stomach environment, protecting it from degradation. At this stage, HC also binds cobalamin analogues (non-human corrinoids ingested via food) [1, mechanism_review; 6, mechanism_review].

**Step 3 — Pancreatic proteases degrade haptocorrin in the duodenum.**  
On entry to the duodenum, the alkaline pH and pancreatic proteases (trypsin, elastase) degrade HC, releasing B12 [6, mechanism_review]. This step is impaired by exocrine pancreatic insufficiency.

**Step 4 — Intrinsic factor (IF) binds free B12.**  
Intrinsic factor, a ~45 kDa glycoprotein secreted exclusively by **gastric parietal cells**, now binds the released cobalamin with very high specificity. Unlike HC, IF binds true cobalamin with strong selectivity over analogues, providing a specificity filter. IF secretion is lost in **pernicious anemia** (autoimmune destruction of parietal cells or anti-IF antibodies) — the most common cause of B12 deficiency in developed countries — and in total gastrectomy [7, mechanism_review; 3, regulatory].

**Step 5 — IF–B12 complex binds the cubam receptor in the terminal ileum.**  
The IF–B12 complex transits the small intestine to the **terminal ileum**, where it is recognized by the heterodimeric receptor **cubam** — a complex of **cubilin** and **amnionless** — on the apical brush border of enterocytes. Receptor-mediated endocytosis internalizes the complex; lysosomal proteolysis then degrades IF, releasing free cobalamin intracellularly for export via the basolateral membrane [1, mechanism_review; 6, mechanism_review]. This step is lost after ileal resection or in Crohn's disease affecting the terminal ileum. The cubam system also mediates reabsorption of biliary B12, completing the enterohepatic cycle.

**Non-IF passive diffusion** (approximately 1% efficiency) occurs across the entire intestinal surface at pharmacological doses (≥500 µg), which is the rationale for high-dose oral B12 replacement even in pernicious anemia [7, mechanism_review].

## Transport in Blood: Why Total Serum B12 Is an Imperfect Marker

After absorption, B12 is exported from ileal enterocytes and enters portal blood, where it is bound by two circulating carrier proteins — **haptocorrin** and **transcobalamin** — with strikingly different physiological roles.

**Haptocorrin (~80% of circulating B12):**  
Haptocorrin carries the large majority of total serum cobalamin. However, the only known receptor for haptocorrin is on **hepatocytes**; no peripheral tissues can extract B12 from the haptocorrin-bound pool. Haptocorrin is therefore considered **metabolically inert** for systemic B12 delivery. Its function in plasma is not fully understood; it may serve as a reservoir or a clearance vehicle for cobalamin analogues [6, mechanism_review; 8, mechanism_review].

**Transcobalamin (TC; holotranscobalamin / holoTC; "active B12," ~20% of circulating B12):**  
Transcobalamin II (TC) carries only ~20% of total serum B12, yet this fraction is the **only form delivered to tissues**. TC is taken up by virtually all nucleated cells via the **CD320 receptor** (transcobalamin receptor), which triggers receptor-mediated endocytosis of the holoTC complex [1, mechanism_review]. After internalization, ABCD4 and LMBD1 transport free cobalamin out of lysosomes into the cytoplasm, where it is routed to the two enzyme cofactor forms. HoloTC — the TC-saturated fraction — is therefore designated "active B12" and has a very short serum half-life (t½ ≈ 1 hour for holoTC vs. ~17 days for haptocorrin-bound B12) because it is promptly taken up by peripheral tissues via the CD320 receptor [9, mechanism_review].

**The measurement problem — what "total serum B12" actually measures:**  
Standard immunoassays for serum B12 measure the sum of haptocorrin-bound + TC-bound B12 — overwhelmingly dominated (~80%) by the metabolically inert haptocorrin pool. This creates a structural disconnect between the assay result and tissue B12 status. Conditions that alter haptocorrin concentrations — pregnancy, estrogen therapy, hereditary haptocorrin deficiency, myeloproliferative disorders, and liver disease — will shift total serum B12 without necessarily changing tissue delivery [8, mechanism_review; 5, mechanism_review]. Holotranscobalamin (holoTC), MMA, and homocysteine provide functional corroboration that total serum B12 alone cannot.

## Reference Ranges

Serum B12 is measured in **pg/mL** (= ng/L) or **pmol/L** (conversion: pg/mL × 0.738 = pmol/L; or equivalently pmol/L ÷ 0.738 = pg/mL):

| Category | pg/mL | pmol/L |
|---|---|---|
| Normal (commonly accepted) | ≥200–250 | ≥148–185 |
| Deficiency | <200 | <148 |
| Indeterminate / gray zone | 200–400 | 148–295 |
| Functional depletion threshold (MMA-based) | ~200 | ~148 |

The indeterminate "gray zone" reflects the imprecision of total serum B12: functional deficiency (elevated MMA, elevated homocysteine) has been documented at levels above 200 pg/mL, and false-low total B12 occurs in haptocorrin deficiency without tissue depletion. **HoloTC** deficiency threshold: <25–40 pmol/L (varies by assay); indeterminate: 25–70 pmol/L. **MMA** >280–300 nmol/L (adjusted for renal function) is the most specific functional marker of cellular B12 depletion [3, regulatory; 4, mechanism_review; 8, mechanism_review].

---

## Summary of What Serum B12 Measures — and Its Limits

Serum total B12 captures the pool bound primarily to haptocorrin — a metabolically unavailable fraction. Only holoTC (the transcobalamin-bound ~20%) is available to tissues and reflects the biologically active pool. The total serum value is a useful first-pass screen, but its sensitivity and specificity for functional tissue depletion are limited: deficiency can exist with "normal" total B12, and low total B12 can reflect haptocorrin reduction without impaired tissue delivery. Functional markers (MMA, homocysteine) and/or holoTC measurement are required when the total B12 result falls in the indeterminate zone or when clinical suspicion is high despite a borderline-normal result [3, regulatory; 7, mechanism_review; 8, mechanism_review].
