# Section B — Cardiovascular compounds: evidence / safety / regulatory / prescribing

**Compound-reasoning discipline this agent must carry.** Cardiovascular pharmacology is the deepest RCT base in all of medicine, but that depth is a trap if reasoning is sloppy. The agent must hold four disciplines simultaneously:

1. **Agent × indication specificity.** A drug's evidence is conditional on the *population* it was tested in. Statins lower events in secondary prevention AND primary prevention, but aspirin's benefit/harm balance *inverts* between those two settings. Icosapent ethyl (EPA-only) reduced events in REDUCE-IT while an EPA/DHA formulation was null in STRENGTH — same "omega-3" class label, opposite outcome. The agent never reasons from a class label alone; it reasons from the specific molecule × the specific tested population.
2. **Mechanism ≠ human outcome.** "PCSK9 inhibition lowers LDL-C" is a mechanism. "Evolocumab reduces major vascular events by 15%" is a human outcome from FOURIER. The agent keeps these in separate registers and never lets a plausible mechanism stand in for an outcome RCT — and never lets a surrogate (LDL-C, BP, triglycerides) substitute for a hard MACE endpoint.
3. **Rx boundary (hard).** Every compound in this section is a prescription drug (or, for omega-3, has a prescription form). The agent **names-and-routes**: it explains what a class is, what the evidence shows, what is monitored, and that a decision to start/stop/dose belongs to a prescribing clinician. The agent **NEVER** initiates, titrates, or doses a prescription cardiovascular drug. It surfaces the guideline-directed convention and routes the operator to their physician.
4. **Risk-tier.** Each family carries a risk tier driven by its adverse-effect ceiling, contraindication sharpness, and monitoring burden — not by how "natural" it sounds. A statin (well-characterized, monitorable) sits at a different tier than dual RAAS blockade (a named-harmful combination).

## 1. Statins

**Efficacy (outcome register).** The Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' (CTT) collaboration's individual-participant meta-analysis (170,000 participants, 26 RCTs) is the anchor: each **1 mmol/L (≈39 mg/dL) reduction in LDL-C** produced a **~22% proportional reduction in major vascular events** (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.76–0.80) per year sustained, with all-cause mortality reduced ~10% per mmol/L (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.87–0.93) [1, meta_analysis]. The benefit is **log-linear and population-independent in direction** — it holds in both primary and secondary prevention, and the CTT low-risk analysis (27 trials) confirmed proportional benefit even in people at <10% 5-year vascular-event risk [2, meta_analysis]. Higher-intensity vs lower-intensity regimens add further benefit (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.82–0.89 for major vascular events) [1, meta_analysis].

**Intensity tiers (mechanism/dosing register — convention, NOT efficacy claim).** Guidelines stratify statins by LDL-lowering potency: *high-intensity* (atorvastatin 40–80 mg, rosuvastatin 20–40 mg; LDL ↓≥50%) vs *moderate-intensity* (LDL ↓30–<50%) [3, practitioner_protocol]. The agent reports these tiers as prescribing conventions and does not select a dose.

**SAMS and the nocebo effect.** Statin-associated muscle symptoms (SAMS) are the dominant reason for discontinuation, but the blinded n-of-1 evidence shows most are **nocebo**, not pharmacological. In **SAMSON** (Howard JP et al, n=60, 49 completers), mean daily symptom intensity (0–100) was 8.0 on no-tablet months, **15.4 on placebo**, and **16.3 on statin** — i.e. ~90% of the symptom burden experienced on statin was also present on placebo ("nocebo ratio" 0.90); placebo and statin were statistically indistinguishable (P=0.388) [4, rct]. **StatinWISE** (Herrett E et al, 200 randomized n-of-1 participants, 151 analyzable) found **no difference** in muscle-symptom score between atorvastatin 20 mg and placebo periods (mean difference −0.11, 95% CI −0.36 to 0.14, P=0.40); two-thirds restarted statins after the trial [5, rct]. True myopathy/rhabdomyolysis is real but rare — CTT recorded ~4 excess rhabdomyolysis cases per 10,000 with more-intensive regimens [1, meta_analysis].

**New-onset type-2 diabetes.** Sattar N et al's collaborative meta-analysis (13 trials, 91,140 participants) found a **modest 9% increased risk** of incident diabetes (OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.02–1.17); NNH ≈ 255 treated for 4 years for one extra case — small relative to vascular benefit [6, meta_analysis].

> **Risk line — Statins.** Adverse effects: SAMS (mostly nocebo per [4,5]), rare myopathy/rhabdomyolysis, transaminase elevation, ~9% relative ↑ incident T2DM [6]. Contraindications: active liver disease; pregnancy/breastfeeding (relative — historically labeled contraindicated). Monitoring: baseline lipids + ALT; CK only if symptomatic (not routine); lipid response 4–12 wk after start/change [3, practitioner_protocol]. Stopping/holding: hold for unexplained CK >10× ULN or rhabdomyolysis signs; investigate alternative cause for SAMS before permanent discontinuation. **Rx boundary: agent does not start/dose; routes to clinician.**

## 2. Non-statin LDL-lowering add-ons (ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, inclisiran, bempedoic acid)

**Ezetimibe — IMPROVE-IT.** Cannon CP et al added ezetimibe 10 mg to simvastatin 40 mg in 18,144 post-ACS patients. The combination drove LDL-C to **53.7 mg/dL (1.4 mmol/L)** vs **69.5 mg/dL (1.8 mmol/L)** on statin alone, and reduced the 7-year primary composite from 34.7% to **32.7%** (HR 0.936, 95% CI 0.89–0.99, P=0.016) [7, rct]. This was the first proof that a **non-statin** LDL-lowering agent reduces events — validating the "LDL hypothesis" (lower is better, by any mechanism).

**PCSK9 inhibitors (monoclonal antibodies, subcutaneous).**
- **FOURIER (evolocumab)** — Sabatine MS et al, 27,564 patients with ASCVD on statin. Evolocumab lowered LDL-C ~59% (median 92 → **30 mg/dL / 0.78 mmol/L**) and cut the primary composite from 11.3% to **9.8%** (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.79–0.92, P<0.001); key secondary HR 0.80 (0.73–0.88) [8, rct].
- **ODYSSEY OUTCOMES (alirocumab)** — Schwartz GG et al, 18,924 post-ACS patients with residually elevated atherogenic lipoproteins despite high-intensity statin; alirocumab 75 mg SC q2wk reduced MACE (overall HR 0.85 class-consistent with FOURIER; the trial reported a significant reduction in the primary composite) [9, rct].

**Inclisiran — ORION-10/ORION-11.** Ray KK et al studied a hepatocyte-targeted **siRNA** against PCSK9 mRNA, dosed SC at day 1, day 90, then **twice yearly** (n=3,178 across the two trials). Placebo-corrected LDL-C reduction at day 510 was **52.3%** (95% CI 48.8–55.7) in ORION-10 and **49.9%** (46.6–53.1) in ORION-11 [10, rct]. NOTE: ORION-10/11 are **LDL-lowering (surrogate) trials, not MACE-outcome trials** — the cardiovascular-outcomes trial (ORION-4) is the hard-endpoint test; the agent must not claim event reduction from ORION-10/11.

**Bempedoic acid — CLEAR Outcomes.** Nissen SE et al studied an oral ATP-citrate-lyase inhibitor in **13,970 statin-intolerant** patients (a population statin RCTs largely exclude). LDL-C fell ~21% / ~22 mg/dL; the 4-component MACE was reduced **13%** (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.79–0.96), with MI (HR 0.77) and coronary revascularization (HR 0.81) driving the effect [11, rct].

> **Risk line — Add-on LDL agents.** *Ezetimibe:* very clean; rare transaminase rise; monitor lipid response. *PCSK9 mAbs / inclisiran:* injection-site reactions; no consistent hepatotoxicity/myopathy signal; monitor LDL response (and adherence to the 2×/yr schedule for inclisiran). *Bempedoic acid:* ↑ uric acid/gout, small ↑ tendon-rupture and gallstone signals, transaminase rise; monitor uric acid and LFTs; **does not cause SAMS** (acts upstream of muscle, hence its statin-intolerant indication). Contraindications: PCSK9 agents and bempedoic acid — pregnancy/breastfeeding (insufficient data; routed to clinician). **Rx boundary: all are clinician-initiated; agent names the option and the LDL/risk context, does not prescribe.**

## 3. Antihypertensives

**First-line classes (convention register).** Major guidelines converge on four first-line families: **thiazide/thiazide-like diuretics, ACE inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin-receptor blockers (ARBs), and dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers (CCBs)** [3, practitioner_protocol]. Beta-blockers are not first-line for uncomplicated hypertension absent a compelling indication.

**Intensive control — SPRINT.** The SPRINT trial (9,361 high-CV-risk, non-diabetic adults) randomized to systolic target **<120 vs <140 mmHg**. The intensive arm cut the primary composite (MI, ACS, stroke, heart failure, CV death) — **1.65%/yr vs 2.19%/yr** (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.64–0.89, P<0.001) — and was stopped early at median 3.26 yr; all-cause mortality was also lower [12, rct]. This established that lower BP targets reduce events in high-risk adults (at a cost of more hypotension/AKI signals — a benefit/harm trade routed to the clinician).

**Named-harmful combination — ONTARGET.** Yusuf S et al (25,620 high-vascular-risk patients) showed that **ACEi + ARB combined** did NOT reduce CV/renal outcomes vs either alone and **worsened major renal outcomes** and increased hypokalemia/hyperkalemia and hypotension [13, rct]. The agent must flag ACEi+ARB co-prescription as a documented-harm combination to route to a clinician, never to suggest.

**Pregnancy contraindication.** ACEi and ARBs are **contraindicated in pregnancy** (fetal renal/developmental toxicity) — a hard, non-negotiable flag [3, practitioner_protocol].

> **Risk line — Antihypertensives.** Adverse effects by family: ACEi — cough, hyperkalemia, angioedema, AKI; ARB — hyperkalemia, AKI (no cough); thiazide — hypokalemia/hyponatremia, hyperuricemia/gout, glucose ↑; dihydropyridine CCB — peripheral edema, flushing. Contraindications: ACEi/ARB in **pregnancy** (hard) and bilateral renal-artery stenosis; **ACEi+ARB combination = documented harm** [13]. Monitoring: BP (treatment target), **renal function + serum potassium** within 1–2 wk of starting/up-titrating ACEi/ARB or diuretic; electrolytes for diuretics [3, practitioner_protocol]. Stopping/holding: hold ACEi/ARB for AKI, hyperkalemia, angioedema, or pregnancy. **Rx boundary: agent names class + monitoring; clinician selects/doses.**

## 4. Antiplatelets — aspirin

**The primary-prevention reversal.** Three large 2018 RCTs converged to overturn routine aspirin for low-/moderate-risk primary prevention:
- **ASPREE** (McNeil JJ et al, 19,114 healthy elderly): aspirin did **not** reduce CV disease (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.83–1.08) but **increased major hemorrhage** (HR 1.38, 95% CI 1.18–1.62, P<0.001) [14, rct].
- **ASCEND** (15,480 with diabetes, no CVD): aspirin reduced serious vascular events ~12% but increased major bleeding ~29%; absolute benefit was **largely cancelled by the bleeding hazard** (NNT 91 to prevent a vascular event vs NNH 112 for a major bleed) [15, rct].
- **ARRIVE** (Gaziano JM et al, 12,546 moderate-risk): no reduction in the primary composite (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.81–1.13) and **>2× GI bleeding** (HR 2.11, 95% CI 1.36–3.28) [16, rct].

**Net.** In low-/moderate-risk **primary** prevention, aspirin's bleeding harm offsets or exceeds its ischemic benefit. **Secondary** prevention (established ASCVD) remains a clear indication — the trade-off flips because baseline event risk is far higher. The agent must keep the primary/secondary distinction explicit and never generalize the primary-prevention reversal to secondary prevention.

> **Risk line — Aspirin.** Adverse effects: GI bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, hemorrhagic stroke. Contraindications: active bleeding, bleeding diathesis, high bleeding risk; caution with anticoagulants/NSAIDs. Monitoring: bleeding signs, hemoglobin if suspected GI loss; periodic benefit/harm reassessment. Stopping/holding: hold for active/major bleeding or before high-bleeding-risk procedures (clinician-directed). **Rx boundary: even OTC-available, the start/stop decision for primary prevention is clinician-adjudicated by individual bleeding-vs-ischemic risk.**

## 5. Omega-3 fatty acids

**The molecule-specific divergence.** "Omega-3" is NOT one intervention:
- **REDUCE-IT** (Bhatt DL et al, 8,179 statin-treated, elevated triglycerides): **icosapent ethyl (purified EPA, 2 g twice daily)** cut the primary composite from 22.0% to **17.2%** (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.68–0.83, P<0.001) — a ~25% relative reduction [17, rct].
- **STRENGTH** (Nicholls SJ et al, 13,078 high-risk): an **EPA/DHA carboxylic-acid** formulation (4 g/day) was **null** for MACE (HR 0.99) vs corn-oil comparator [18, rct].
- **VITAL** (Manson JE et al, 25,871 primary-prevention): **1 g/day marine n-3** did not reduce the major-CV composite (HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.80–1.06); a secondary MI signal (HR ~0.71, 95% CI 0.57–0.88) is hypothesis-generating, not an endpoint result [19, rct].

**Mineral-oil-placebo controversy (REDUCE-IT).** REDUCE-IT used **mineral oil** as placebo; the placebo arm showed LDL-C ↑~10% and hsCRP ↑~30%, raising the question of whether part of the apparent benefit reflects placebo-arm *harm* rather than EPA benefit [20, mechanism_review]. The agent must surface this as an unresolved methodological caveat. FDA and EMA reviews concluded mineral oil was unlikely to materially explain the result, but the EPA-only-vs-mixed-formulation divergence (REDUCE-IT positive, STRENGTH null) means the agent treats omega-3 cardiovascular benefit as **formulation-specific (purified EPA, hypertriglyceridemic population), not a general "fish oil" effect** [20, mechanism_review].

> **Risk line — Omega-3 (icosapent ethyl / prescription EPA).** Adverse effects: **increased atrial fibrillation** (consistent across REDUCE-IT and STRENGTH), increased bleeding risk (especially with antiplatelet/anticoagulant), GI upset. Contraindications: fish/shellfish allergy (caution); bleeding risk. Monitoring: triglyceride response; watch for new AF symptoms/palpitations and bleeding. Stopping/holding: hold for clinically significant bleeding or new AF (clinician-directed). **Rx boundary: prescription icosapent ethyl is clinician-initiated for a specific hypertriglyceridemia/risk profile; OTC fish-oil supplements lack the EPA-only outcome evidence and are not interchangeable — agent must not equate them.**

## 6. Prescribing-practice conventions (FOLDS the standard-mode prescribing layer)

These are `regulatory`/`practitioner_protocol` items — **who-to-treat thresholds and management conventions, NOT efficacy claims.** Efficacy lives in §§1–5 above with `rct`/`meta_analysis` cites.

**Statin intensity + treatment thresholds.**
- **ACC/AHA 2018 cholesterol guideline:** clinical ASCVD (secondary prevention) → high-intensity statin (LDL ↓≥50%); in very-high-risk ASCVD, add ezetimibe (then PCSK9i) if LDL-C remains **≥70 mg/dL (≥1.8 mmol/L)**. Primary prevention uses 10-year ASCVD risk estimation + risk enhancers (incl. coronary artery calcium) to gate statin initiation [3, regulatory].
- **ESC/EAS 2019 dyslipidaemia guideline:** for very-high-risk patients, LDL-C target lowered to **<55 mg/dL (<1.4 mmol/L)** AND ≥50% reduction from baseline (the 2021 ESC CVD-prevention guideline retained this target framework) [21, regulatory].

**Monitoring + stopping conventions by family** (summarized in §§1–5 risk lines; sourced to [3, practitioner_protocol] / [21, regulatory]): statins → lipids + ALT, CK if symptomatic; ACEi/ARB/diuretics → renal function + electrolytes after initiation/up-titration; aspirin → bleeding surveillance + periodic benefit/harm reassessment; PCSK9i/inclisiran → LDL response + adherence to dosing interval; bempedoic acid → uric acid + LFTs; omega-3 (EPA) → triglyceride response + AF/bleeding watch.

**Rx boundary restated.** The guideline thresholds above tell the agent *what convention exists and what is monitored* — they do **not** authorize the agent to start, titrate, or stop a prescription. The agent names the threshold, explains the monitoring rationale, and routes the decision to the prescribing clinician.
