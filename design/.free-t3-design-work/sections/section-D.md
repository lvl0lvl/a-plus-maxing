# Section D — Determinants & Clinical Significance

## D.1 Primary Clinical Role: Confirming and Grading Hyperthyroidism

Free T3 (fT3) occupies a specific and well-defined diagnostic position: it is most useful in the workup and monitoring of **hyperthyroidism**, not hypothyroidism. In overt thyrotoxicosis, both fT4 and fT3 are elevated with a suppressed TSH (<0.01 mU/L on a third-generation assay). Because the hyperthyroid thyroid preferentially secretes T3 relative to T4, fT3 is often disproportionately elevated and its magnitude roughly tracks with disease severity. The 2016 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines for hyperthyroidism establish that elevated fT3, fT4, or both — in the context of a subnormal TSH — constitutes overt hyperthyroidism [1, regulatory].

**T3-toxicosis** is a clinically important pattern that fT3 is uniquely positioned to detect: isolated elevation of fT3 with normal fT4 and suppressed TSH. According to the ATA guidelines, this represents either the earliest phase of Graves' disease (after TSH becomes suppressed but before fT4 rises) or a characteristic feature of autonomously functioning toxic nodules, where it occurs in an estimated 5–46% of cases [1, regulatory]. In relapsing Graves' disease, T3-toxicosis is also a recognized early-recurrence pattern. The guidelines note that because fT3 assays are less widely validated than fT4 assays, total T3 is frequently preferred in clinical practice for this purpose — a practical caveat that reflects assay imprecision at low concentrations rather than any conceptual limitation of the free fraction [1, regulatory].

Drugs and conditions that raise fT3 include: active hyperthyroidism (Graves', toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma), exogenous T3 ingestion (liothyronine, desiccated thyroid), recovery phase of non-thyroidal illness, and hCG-driven gestational thyrotoxicosis.

## D.2 Limited Role in Hypothyroidism

fT3 plays essentially **no diagnostic role in hypothyroidism**. In primary hypothyroidism, TSH rises first and fT4 falls before fT3 does, because the body maintains T3 production through upregulation of type 2 deiodinase (D2) in peripheral tissues — compensating until late-stage disease. As a result, fT3 remains within the normal range in all but severe, long-standing primary hypothyroidism, making it insensitive for diagnosis or treatment monitoring.

The 2014 ATA guidelines on thyroid hormone replacement (Jonklaas et al.) are explicit on this point: serum T3 or fT3 measurement is **not recommended** for the diagnosis of hypothyroidism, and TSH remains the primary monitoring tool during levothyroxine therapy [2, regulatory]. The core reason is quantitative: the serum T3 pool is buffered by peripheral deiodination, so serum levels do not reliably track tissue thyroid status in hypothyroid patients on LT4.

## D.3 Non-Thyroidal Illness (NTI) / Low-T3 Syndrome

In any significant acute or chronic illness — infection, cardiac failure, surgery, malignancy, starvation, major trauma — circulating fT3 falls, sometimes dramatically, without primary thyroid pathology. This is called **non-thyroidal illness syndrome** (NTIS) or "low-T3 syndrome." The mechanism involves downregulation of type 1 deiodinase (D1) in liver and kidney (which normally supplies ~80% of circulating T3 from T4), concurrent upregulation of type 3 deiodinase (D3, which inactivates T3 to T2 and T4 to reverse T3), and suppressed hypothalamic–pituitary TRH/TSH signaling. The net result is low fT3, low-to-normal fT4, and a TSH that is inappropriately normal or mildly suppressed — a pattern that can superficially resemble central hypothyroidism [3, mechanism_review].

### Prognostic signal

Low fT3 in illness carries robust prognostic weight across multiple cardiac cohorts:

- **Iervasi et al. (2003), n = 573 cardiac patients:** In a prospective cohort, fT3 was the single strongest predictor of cumulative one-year mortality by Cox regression (HR 3.582, P < 0.0001). Patients with fT3 < 3.1 pmol/L had substantially higher mortality than those with normal fT3 [4, cohort].
- **Sato et al. (2019), n = 911 hospitalized HF patients:** Low fT3 (< 2.3 pg/mL) predicted all-cause death (HR 2.304, 95% CI 1.736–3.058, P < 0.001) and cardiac death (HR 1.926, 95% CI 1.268–2.927) over a median 991-day follow-up [5, cohort].
- **Zhao et al. (2021), n = 594 acute decompensated HF patients:** Low T3 syndrome was independently associated with 1-year all-cause mortality (adjusted HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.21–2.82, P = 0.005) [6, cohort].
- **Asai et al. (2020), n = 1,190 acute HF patients:** Low fT3 independently predicted 365-day mortality (HR 1.429, 95% CI 1.013–2.015) and HF events [7, cohort].
- **Vidart et al. (2022), meta-analysis of 25 studies, n = 6,869 critically ill patients:** NTIS was independently associated with in-hospital mortality (pooled OR 2.21, 95% CI 1.64–2.97) [8, meta_analysis].

**Caution on interpretation:** This prognostic signal does not imply a causal path from low fT3 to death, nor does it establish that T3 replacement improves outcomes. Low fT3 in critical illness is best understood as a **marker of illness severity** — the sicker the patient, the more depressed the deiodinase system. No large RCT has demonstrated that T3 or T4 supplementation in NTIS reduces mortality. The adaptive-vs-maladaptive debate remains unsettled: while the low-T3 state may be physiologically adaptive (protein sparing), prolonged critical-illness NTIS is increasingly characterized as a maladaptive neuroendocrine failure [3, mechanism_review]. Current guidelines from major endocrine societies do not recommend T3 or T4 supplementation for NTIS outside of specific investigational settings.

## D.4 The LT4 + LT3 Combination-Therapy Debate

A clinically relevant consequence of the serum-tissue fT3 disconnect emerges in levothyroxine (LT4)-treated hypothyroid patients. The thyroid normally secretes both T4 and T3 in a ~20:1 molar ratio; LT4 monotherapy relies entirely on peripheral deiodination to supply T3. In the athyreotic patient, this conversion may be insufficient to restore normal serum fT3, even when TSH is well-controlled — a finding documented in studies of post-thyroidectomy patients.

The **DIO2 Thr92Ala polymorphism** — present in ~12–16% of the population — reduces D2 activity and has been associated with impaired psychological well-being on LT4 monotherapy and differential response to combination LT4+LT3 (liothyronine) therapy. In genotyping studies, preference for combination therapy rose from 42% in non-carriers to 100% in homozygous carriers [9, mechanism_review].

Despite this biologically plausible mechanism, **RCT evidence for combination therapy has been largely neutral.** A meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials found no significant difference between LT4 monotherapy and combination therapy on quality-of-life, mood, or cognitive outcomes. Wiersinga (2021) characterizes this as "an unsolved problem of increasing magnitude" and cautions that enthusiasm has outpaced evidence [9, mechanism_review]. The 2012 European Thyroid Association guidelines permit combination therapy only on an experimental basis in selected patients with persistent symptoms who have confirmed euthyroid TSH and no comorbid explanation. The 2014 ATA guidelines (Jonklaas et al.) similarly affirm LT4 as the standard of care while acknowledging ongoing uncertainty about combination therapy for a subset of patients [2, regulatory].

**The clinical relevance of fT3 in this context:** patients on LT4 with persistent hypothyroid symptoms and low-normal fT3 (with normal TSH and fT4) form the candidate population for this debate. fT3 measurement is one of the tools used to identify this pattern, though it is not currently a decision-making criterion in mainstream guidelines.

## D.5 Factors That Alter fT3

**Raise fT3:**
- Graves' disease, toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma
- Exogenous T3 (liothyronine, desiccated thyroid extract)
- T3-toxicosis (early/relapsing Graves', toxic nodule)
- Recovery phase of NTI (rebound deiodination)
- hCG stimulation (gestational thyrotoxicosis, hyperemesis)

**Lower fT3:**
- Late / severe primary hypothyroidism
- Non-thyroidal illness / NTIS (any acute or chronic illness)
- Caloric restriction and fasting — a 53% drop in serum T3 was observed with total fasting, with rT3 rising reciprocally; diets providing ≥ 50 g carbohydrate daily did not significantly alter T3 [10, rct]
- **Drugs inhibiting type 1 deiodinase (D1):** propylthiouracil (PTU, a specific uncompetitive D1 inhibitor, used therapeutically in hyperthyroidism), amiodarone (strong D1 inhibitor in liver, routinely lowers T3 and raises rT3), glucocorticoids (suppress TRH/TSH and D1 activity), beta-blockers (propranolol inhibits peripheral T3 production by a partially characterized mechanism distinct from beta-blockade per se), and iodinated radiographic contrast agents (iopanoic acid, sodium ipodate — competitive inhibitors of D1 and D2)

## D.6 Assay Limitations and Interpretive Pitfalls

Several factors limit the clinical utility of fT3 beyond its core hyperthyroid application:

- **Assay imprecision at low concentrations:** fT3 circulates at roughly 1/40th the total T3 concentration (~2–6 pg/mL / 3.1–9.2 pmol/L). Most immunoassay platforms have coefficients of variation that are disproportionately large near the lower reference bound, reducing confidence in borderline results.
- **NTI confounding:** Because low fT3 is ubiquitous in acute illness, an fT3 result in a hospitalized or acutely unwell patient reflects NTI physiology at least as much as thyroid status. Interpreting fT3 in isolation without clinical context risks misdiagnosis.
- **Poor serum-to-tissue correlation:** Circulating fT3 is a surrogate for tissue thyroid hormone action, not a direct measure of it. Intracellular T3 depends on local deiodinase activity, transporter expression (MCT10, MCT8), and receptor isoform distribution — none of which are captured by serum fT3.
- **Not indicated for routine hypothyroid workup:** The 2014 ATA guidelines explicitly exclude fT3 from hypothyroid diagnosis and standard monitoring [2, regulatory]. Ordering fT3 reflexively in hypothyroid patients is a common practice that adds cost without changing management in the vast majority of cases.
