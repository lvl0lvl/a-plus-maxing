# Section B: Reference Ranges, Units & Thresholds — Free T3 (Triiodothyronine)

## Reference Intervals: Typical Adult Values

The conventional adult free T3 (fT3) reference interval is approximately **2.3–4.2 pg/mL (3.5–6.5 pmol/L)**. This range is not a fixed biological constant but a platform-specific construct: every immunoassay manufacturer establishes its own reference interval from its own population sample, and intervals shift materially across methods. In a Spanish population study using the Abbott ARCHITECT i2000 analyzer, the calculated healthy-adult interval was 1.48–3.37 pg/mL (2.27–5.18 pmol/L) [1, cohort]. A Turkish clinical-chemistry group using Roche Cobas electrochemiluminescence immunoassay reported a combined adult interval of 3.38–6.35 pmol/L in males and 3.39–5.99 pmol/L in females, with the manufacturer's stated range of 3.1–6.8 pmol/L differing noticeably from their population-derived values — and both diverging from the ARCHITECT numbers above [2, cohort]. A Chinese elderly-population study (DiaSorin LIAISON XL chemiluminescence) found fT3 of 3.40–6.44 pmol/L for adults under 65, declining to 2.82–5.57 pmol/L in those aged 80–100 [3, cohort].

These discordances are not minor rounding differences. They reflect genuine method-dependence: direct analogue immunoassays for free hormones are vulnerable to artifacts from altered binding proteins, assay matrix effects, and antibody cross-reactivity [4, mechanism_review]. The practical consequence is that a result of 3.2 pg/mL may be "normal" on one platform and flagged low on another. Clinicians should always interpret fT3 against the reference interval issued by the laboratory performing the assay, never a generic textbook number.

## Unit Conversion

Free T3 is reported in **pg/mL** (mass units) or **pmol/L** (molar units). The conversion factor is **×1.536**: 1 pg/mL = 1.536 pmol/L (molecular weight of T3 = 650.97 g/mol).

| pg/mL | pmol/L |
|-------|--------|
| 2.3   | 3.5    |
| 3.0   | 4.6    |
| 3.5   | 5.4    |
| 4.0   | 6.1    |
| 4.2   | 6.5    |

Some platforms (particularly European automated analyzers) report exclusively in pmol/L; US platforms commonly use pg/mL. Both units appear in the literature for the same analyte, and unit confusion is a known source of clinical error.

## Diagnostic Role: Where Free T3 Adds Value

### Hyperthyroidism — the core use case

Free T3 is **diagnostically useful in hyperthyroidism**, specifically for identifying **T3 toxicosis**: a state of clinical thyrotoxicosis in which fT3 is elevated while fT4 remains within or only borderline above the reference range and TSH is suppressed. This occurs because the hyperactive thyroid preferentially secretes T3, and because elevated TSH-receptor stimulation (as in Graves' disease) upregulates the thyroidal T3/T4 secretion ratio [5, regulatory].

T3 toxicosis accounts for **approximately 5% of all thyrotoxicosis cases in North America** (some older series report up to 10%, but contemporary series with sensitive immunoassays cluster around 2–5%) [5, regulatory]. It appears most often in:

- **Early Graves' disease** — T3 excess may precede fT4 elevation, making fT3 the first detectable biochemical abnormality.
- **Autonomously functioning thyroid nodules** (solitary toxic adenoma or toxic multinodular goiter) — these preferentially secrete T3, and T3 toxicosis may represent the earliest stage before the nodule grows large enough to also suppress T4 feedback [6, mechanism_review].
- **Iodine deficiency goiter** becoming autonomous.

The key interpretive grid for hyperthyroidism evaluation is:

| TSH | fT4 | fT3 | Interpretation |
|-----|-----|-----|---------------|
| Low/suppressed | High | High | Overt hyperthyroidism |
| Low/suppressed | Normal | High | T3 toxicosis |
| Low/suppressed | Normal | Normal | Subclinical hyperthyroidism (fT3 helps rule out T3 toxicosis) |
| Normal | Normal | Normal | Euthyroid |

The 2016 ATA Guidelines for hyperthyroidism note that "a suppressed or subnormal TSH level should be followed by assay of both free T4 and free T3 to distinguish subclinical from overt thyrotoxicosis and to identify T3 toxicosis." They also note that total T3 is often preferred over free T3 in this context because free T3 assays are "less widely validated and less robust" than free T4 assays, and total T3 measurement is frequently more reproducible across platforms [5, regulatory].

### Hypothyroidism — not a useful test

Free T3 is **not recommended for diagnosing or monitoring hypothyroidism**. The ATA 2014 hypothyroidism treatment guidelines establish TSH as the primary monitoring parameter; fT3 is not a target for dose adjustment [7, regulatory]. The Endocrine Society, through its Choosing Wisely recommendations, explicitly advises against ordering free or total T3 when assessing levothyroxine dosing [8, cohort].

The physiological rationale is straightforward: fT3 is preserved until late in the course of hypothyroidism. Even with significantly impaired thyroidal output, peripheral deiodination of T4 to T3 by type 2 deiodinase (D2) — particularly in the brain and pituitary — maintains circulating fT3 within the reference range long after TSH has become elevated and fT4 has fallen. Free T3 is thus an insensitive hypothyroid marker. Conversely, patients on levothyroxine monotherapy who achieve a normal TSH often have a serum T3 at the lower end of, or slightly below, the reference range because endogenous thyroidal T3 secretion has been lost — this does not necessarily represent a deficiency requiring intervention.

## Non-Thyroidal Illness (Low-T3 Syndrome)

Low fT3 in the context of acute or chronic systemic illness is extremely common and does **not** indicate primary thyroid disease. This "low T3 syndrome" (also called non-thyroidal illness syndrome, NTIS, or euthyroid sick syndrome) affects an estimated 60–70% of critically ill patients [9, mechanism_review].

The mechanism is peripheral rather than thyroidal:
- Proinflammatory cytokines downregulate type 1 deiodinase (D1), reducing T4→T3 conversion.
- Type 3 deiodinase (D3) activity increases, shunting T4 toward reverse T3 (rT3) instead of active T3.
- Hepatic uptake of T4 falls, further reducing substrate for peripheral T3 production.
- The net result is low serum T3, elevated rT3, and — crucially — **normal or only modestly altered TSH**, because the pituitary-thyroid axis itself is not the primary site of dysfunction [10, mechanism_review].

The hallmark distinguishing low-T3 syndrome from genuine primary hypothyroidism is the TSH: in NTIS, TSH is typically normal (or transiently suppressed in the acute phase), whereas primary hypothyroidism raises TSH. A patient with pneumonia, cardiac surgery, chronic kidney disease, or malignancy who has a low fT3 in the range of 2.5 pmol/L almost always has NTIS rather than thyroid disease — and their fT3 will recover as the underlying illness resolves [10, mechanism_review].

## Age, Sex, and Pregnancy Variation

**Age:** fT3 shows a modest but consistent age-related decline. In the Roche ECLIA population study, upper-reference limits in subjects over 70 were approximately 0.6–1.0 pmol/L below those in adults aged 18–30 [2, cohort]. The elderly Chinese Han population study confirmed a similar progressive decline: subjects aged 65–79 had a reference upper limit of 5.91 pmol/L vs. 6.44 pmol/L for those under 65 [3, cohort]. Age-specific reference intervals should be used when evaluating thyroid function in older adults.

**Sex:** Most immunoassay-based studies show no clinically significant sex difference in fT3 in the adult range, though some population studies find slightly lower upper limits in females [2, cohort].

**Pregnancy:** fT3 reference ranges shift during gestation and are **highly method-dependent** — a point made emphatically in trimester-specific interval studies. A Greek cohort study (IMMULITE 2000 immunoassay) reported first-trimester fT3 of 1.54–5.22 pg/mL (2.37–8.02 pmol/L) and second-trimester fT3 of 1.78–5.29 pg/mL (2.73–8.13 pmol/L), with a marginal trend toward declining fT3 as gestation advances [11, cohort]. A correlational analysis of immunoassay vs. mass spectrometry for fT3 in pregnant women found a correlation of only r = 0.397 — indicating that different platforms can give substantially different numeric results for the same sample [4, mechanism_review]. The 2012 Endocrine Society pregnancy guideline recommends using assay- and trimester-specific reference intervals when interpreting thyroid function in pregnancy and notes that fT3 measurement adds value primarily when TSH is suppressed and fT4 is normal, raising concern for T3 toxicosis (e.g., gestational thyrotoxicosis or Graves' disease) [12, regulatory].

The late-pregnancy fT3 decline (third trimester relative to first) is well-described across multiple populations and is attributed to increased thyroid hormone binding, expanded plasma volume, and altered deiodinase activity — not to a fall in thyroidal secretion.
