# Section A: Physiology & What Free T3 Measures

## Free Triiodothyronine (fT3): The Active Thyroid Hormone

Triiodothyronine (T3; 3,5,3'-triiodothyronine) is the biologically active form of thyroid hormone. While the thyroid gland secretes thyroxine (T4) as its primary product — at roughly 10-fold the rate of T3 by mass — T4 functions predominantly as a prohormone [1, mechanism_review]. T3 is the molecule that actually occupies nuclear thyroid hormone receptors and drives gene expression changes responsible for nearly all classical thyroid hormone effects: regulation of basal metabolic rate, cardiac output, thermogenesis, skeletal development, and neurological function [2, mechanism_review].

### Receptor Binding: Why T3 Is the Active Form

T3 exerts its genomic effects by binding nuclear thyroid hormone receptors, TRα (predominantly in brain, heart, and skeletal muscle) and TRβ (liver, pituitary, cochlea). T3 binds these receptors with approximately 7- to 15-fold greater affinity than T4 in direct binding assays; the functional potency gap is even larger — EC50 data show T4 requires ~30- to 50-fold higher concentrations than T3 to achieve equivalent transcriptional activation at TRα1 and TRβ1, respectively [3, mechanism_review]. Receptor binding triggers a conformational change in helix-12 of the TR ligand-binding domain, displacing corepressor complexes and recruiting coactivators that initiate polymerase-driven gene transcription [4, mechanism_review]. It is T3 occupancy — not T4 occupancy — that constitutes the predominant transcriptional signal under euthyroid conditions, with approximately 50% of liver and kidney thyroid hormone receptors occupied by T3 at normal circulating concentrations [1, mechanism_review].

### Source of Circulating T3: Peripheral Conversion Dominates

The thyroid gland contributes only a minority of circulating T3 directly. In a 70-kg adult, total daily T3 production is approximately 30 µg/day, of which only ~5 µg/day (~17%) derives from direct thyroidal secretion [1, mechanism_review]. The remaining ~25 µg/day (~83%) is generated by peripheral deiodination of T4 in extrathyroidal tissues [1, mechanism_review]. This means the body's T3 supply is tightly coupled to the adequacy of T4 production and the activity of the tissue deiodinase enzymes.

### The Deiodinase System: Activation and Inactivation

Three selenoenzyme deiodinases control thyroid hormone bioavailability at the tissue level [5, mechanism_review]:

**Type 1 deiodinase (D1)** is expressed primarily in liver, kidney, and thyroid. It performs outer-ring (5') deiodination to convert T4→T3, and also inner-ring deiodination to convert T4→reverse-T3 (rT3) and clear conjugated T3 metabolites. D1 has low T4 affinity (Km ~1–2 µM) and is induced by thyroid hormone itself. It is the main source of T3 appearing in the bloodstream, contributing ~5 µg/day to circulating T3 [1, mechanism_review].

**Type 2 deiodinase (D2)** is expressed in brain, pituitary, brown adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, heart, and vascular smooth muscle. D2 performs exclusively outer-ring deiodination (T4→T3) with very high T4 affinity (Km ~1 nM), supplying ~20 µg/day of T3 production — predominantly for intracellular use in D2-expressing cells rather than export to the bloodstream [1, mechanism_review]. D2's short half-life (~20 minutes in the presence of T4 substrate) makes it exquisitely responsive to local T4 availability and allows tissue-level fine-tuning of T3 supply independent of circulating T3 levels [1, mechanism_review]. Critically, D2 is the enzyme responsible for providing T3 to pituitary thyrotrophs and hypothalamic neurons, making it central to HPT-axis feedback sensing.

**Type 3 deiodinase (D3)** is the primary inactivating enzyme. It performs inner-ring deiodination, converting T4→reverse-T3 (inactive) and T3→3,3'-diiodothyronine (T2, inactive) [5, mechanism_review]. D3 is expressed in brain, skin, placenta, and fetal tissues, and is upregulated in critical illness (where it contributes to the "euthyroid sick syndrome" pattern of low T3). The balance between D2 (activating) and D3 (inactivating) in any given tissue is the primary local determinant of intracellular T3 concentration, independent of what circulating fT3 measures.

### Free T3: The Unbound, Bioavailable Fraction

Like other steroid and thyroid hormones, T3 circulates almost entirely bound to plasma carrier proteins — primarily thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG), with minor contributions from transthyretin (TTR) and albumin. T3 binds TBG with substantially lower affinity than T4, which is reflected in its larger free fraction: approximately 0.2–0.3% of circulating T3 is unbound (free), compared with ~0.01% for T4 [6, mechanism_review]. In absolute terms, a typical euthyroid adult has a serum free T3 concentration of approximately **2.3–4.2 pg/mL (3.5–6.5 pmol/L)**, with specific reference intervals varying by assay platform and population studied [6, mechanism_review]. (Unit conversion: pg/mL × 1.536 = pmol/L.)

The free fraction is the only form capable of crossing cell membranes (via thyroid hormone transporters, including MCT8 and OATP1C1), entering the nucleus, and activating thyroid hormone receptors. Protein-bound T3 acts as a circulating reservoir that buffers against rapid fluctuations in free hormone. Because T3's lower TBG binding affinity means it distributes more freely than T4, approximately two-thirds of total body T3 resides in tissues at any given moment, versus two-thirds of T4 remaining in plasma [2, mechanism_review].

### Why Serum fT3 Imperfectly Reflects Tissue Thyroid Status

A fundamental limitation of serum fT3 measurement is that it captures only the circulating pool — not intracellular T3, which in D2-expressing tissues can substantially exceed what serum concentrations would predict. Nuclear T3 concentrations have been estimated at 58- to 251-fold above cytosolic concentrations in liver, kidney, heart, and brain, reflecting active nuclear uptake and local D2 activity [1, mechanism_review]. This means thyroid hormone action at the tissue level is substantially shaped by local deiodinase activity that serum fT3 cannot capture.

Additionally, serum fT3 is a less sensitive primary signal for the HPT axis than fT4. The pituitary thyrotroph relies on intracellular D2-mediated T4→T3 conversion to generate the T3 that occupies pituitary TRβ2 receptors and suppresses TSH secretion. Because the pituitary "senses" T4 and converts it locally, TSH correlates more tightly with fT4 than with serum fT3 [7, mechanism_review]. This also explains why serum fT3 is often the last parameter to fall in evolving hypothyroidism — the pituitary and peripheral D2 upregulate in response to declining T4, sustaining T3 production at the expense of increased TSH drive, until T4 substrate becomes insufficient.
