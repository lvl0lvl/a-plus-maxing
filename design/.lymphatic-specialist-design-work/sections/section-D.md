# Section D — Drainage Modalities & Interventions: Evidence Grading, the GRADE Two-Axis Case, Pseudoscience Refutation, and Compound Route-Out

*This section grounds the DOMAIN SCIENCE the `lymphatic-specialist` agent must reason over. It is goal-agnostic library research — it grades the real interventions, refutes the marketing claims, and maps the lymphatic-relevant compounds — without reference to any operator's body, goals, or protocol. Personalization is downstream and out of scope here. The lymphatic-specialist owns protocols and biomarkers; it does NOT author compound entries or emit doses, and it must NOT launder a strong clinical recommendation into a claim of strong evidence for every component of that recommendation.*

Type-tag convention: every load-bearing claim carries an inline `[N, tag]` where `tag` is exactly one of `rct | meta_analysis | cohort | open_label | animal | in_vitro | mechanism_review | regulatory`. GRADE two-axis is given as **certainty** (high/moderate/low/very-low) **× strength** (strong/weak/conditional) wherever the literature supports the pairing. Each claim is flagged **ESTABLISHED** or **PROVISIONAL**. Pseudoscience marketing claims carry a bolded **Agent rule:** line.

## D.1 Complete Decongestive Therapy (CDT) — the standard of care and the GRADE anchor

CDT (also called Complex/Combined Decongestive Therapy or Decongestive Lymphatic Therapy) is the bundle of: (a) manual lymphatic drainage (MLD), (b) multilayer compression bandaging followed by compression garments, (c) exercise/movement, and (d) skin care/education, delivered in an intensive Phase I (reduction) then a maintenance Phase II [1, regulatory; 2, mechanism_review]. The 2020 Consensus Document of the International Society of Lymphology (ISL) frames CDT/DLT combined with compression as the management mainstay for chronic peripheral lymphedema across the world's protocols [1, regulatory]. Phase-I expert protocols converge on intensive daily sessions with bandaging worn ~23 h/day during the reduction phase [3, regulatory].

The load-bearing finding for the agent: **compression is the component carrying the effect.** Systematic-review and consensus framing identifies multilayer bandaging in Phase I and compression garments in Phase II as the components with the highest-quality supporting evidence, with compression garments described as the most important element of the maintenance phase because they raise interstitial pressure, reduce net capillary filtration, and reduce lymph formation [1, regulatory; 2, mechanism_review]. ESTABLISHED (mechanism + guideline convergence) that compression is the dominant active ingredient of CDT.

**This is the canonical GRADE two-axis anchor for the agent.** The recommendation *"use CDT (with compression at its core) for diagnosed lymphedema"* sits at roughly **moderate-to-low certainty × strong recommendation** — strong because the direction of benefit is consistent, low harm, large patient-important effect, and guideline-endorsed; certainty held back because the component trials are small, heterogeneous, and rarely blinded [1, regulatory; 4, meta_analysis]. GRADE explicitly permits a strong recommendation on modest certainty when benefits clearly outweigh harms; the agent privileges the strong action (compression-anchored CDT) while stating the certainty caveat in the same breath — especially that the MLD component added on top of compression is itself low-to-moderate certainty — rather than papering over the gap. **Any OTHER strong-on-low pairing it encounters — a confident recommendation resting on low-certainty evidence that is NOT this well-characterized standard-of-care case — should HALT or be downgraded, not mirrored.** "Strong despite low certainty" is a privilege earned by CDT through guideline consensus and favorable benefit-harm asymmetry; it is not a general license. ESTABLISHED as the reference pattern.

## D.2 Manual Lymphatic Drainage (MLD) — the contested component

MLD is the gentle, directional skin-stretch massage component of CDT. It is the place the agent is most tempted to oversell, and the place the evidence most clearly says: do not.

The anchor citation is the Cochrane review, Ezzo et al. 2015 (CD003475.pub2): 6 randomized trials, 208 participants total, breast-cancer-related lymphedema (BCRL) [4, meta_analysis]. Findings, stated precisely:

- When MLD was **added to intensive compression bandaging**, swelling reduced by an *additional* ~7.11% beyond bandaging alone (95% CI 1.75–12.47%, p=0.009), against a backdrop where compression bandaging alone already produced ~30–38.6% volume reduction [4, meta_analysis]. So the marginal MLD effect is real but small relative to the compression effect doing most of the work.
- The absolute-volume (mL) reduction was only borderline (p≈0.06), and total lymphedema volume change was not statistically significant [4, meta_analysis].
- Quality-of-life findings were **inconclusive** (one trial reported no usable results, another only within-group); for pain/symptoms, 60–80% of participants "felt better" regardless of which arm they were in, with **no between-group difference** [4, meta_analysis].
- Subgroup signal: the benefit of *adding* MLD reached significance mainly in **mild-to-moderate BCRL** (roughly <23% baseline excess volume); moderate-to-severe disease showed no significant added response [4, meta_analysis].
- Authors' conclusion verbatim in spirit: "MLD is safe and may offer additional benefit to compression bandaging for swelling reduction," with the modest effect concentrated in mild-to-moderate disease and needing confirmation [4, meta_analysis].

GRADE two-axis: MLD-as-an-add-on sits at **low-to-moderate certainty × weak/conditional recommendation** [4, meta_analysis]. PROVISIONAL. The agent rule that falls out of this: MLD is a defensible adjunct within CDT, but it is **not the active ingredient**, its added benefit over compression is small and concentrated in milder disease, and it should never be presented as a standalone therapy or as the mechanistic "engine" of decongestion. Route-fidelity note: this evidence is BCRL-specific; extrapolating MLD efficacy numbers to lower-limb, primary, or non-cancer lymphedema is not supported by these trials.

## D.3 Compression therapy and intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC)

**Static compression (bandaging + garments)** is, per D.1, the load-bearing CDT component, supported by guideline consensus and the comparative arms of the MLD trials in which bandaging alone delivered the bulk of volume reduction [1, regulatory; 4, meta_analysis]. ESTABLISHED that compression reduces and maintains limb volume in lymphedema; GRADE roughly **moderate certainty × strong recommendation** for diagnosed lymphedema.

**Intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC)** is a *device* — a pump that inflates a multi-chamber sleeve. As an adjunct to CDT it has supportive but inconsistent evidence: a systematic review of 7 RCTs in lower-extremity lymphedema reported pre-vs-post limb-volume reduction in all 7, and adding IPC to CDT produced further short-term reduction within ~4 weeks [5, meta_analysis]. A breast-cancer IPC meta-analysis found efficacy concentrated under specific conditions — pressure ≤40 mmHg, treatment >2 weeks, shorter lymphedema duration — and characterized efficacy in established lymphedema as "inconsistent and uncertain" [6, meta_analysis]. GRADE: IPC adjunct ≈ **low-to-moderate certainty × weak/conditional recommendation.** PROVISIONAL.

**DEVICE_FUNCTION boundary (load-bearing for the agent):** IPC is a regulated device with prescribed pressure settings, cycle times, and patient-selection criteria. The agent **informs** about IPC's evidence and role; it does **not** prescribe device pressures, cycle parameters, or sleeve configuration — those are clinician/device-protocol decisions. Reporting "pressure ≤40 mmHg associated with better outcomes" is permitted as a *literature finding*; it is NOT a setting the agent may direct a user to dial in.

**Contraindication cross-reference (must be surfaced whenever compression/IPC is discussed):** compression is contraindicated or requires medical clearance in acute DVT, severe peripheral arterial disease (any sustained compression contraindicated when ABI < 0.5), acute cellulitis/infection of the limb, decompensated heart failure, and active leg ulceration of arterial origin [7, regulatory; 8, regulatory]. A nuance the agent should hold but NOT act on as a prescriber: intermittent (vs sustained) compression has a pressure-free interval that some sources discuss in arterial-occlusive contexts — this is exactly the kind of device-titration judgment that routes to a clinician, not the agent [7, regulatory]. ESTABLISHED that these contraindications gate any compression recommendation.

## D.4 Exercise and the muscle pump — what's real vs. overstated

**The mechanism is ESTABLISHED.** The lymphatic system has no central pump; lymph propulsion depends on intrinsic lymphangion contraction plus *extrinsic* pumps — chiefly rhythmic skeletal-muscle contraction and the respiratory/diaphragmatic pump compressing lymphatic vessels, with one-way valves preventing backflow [9, mechanism_review]. In exercising human skeletal muscle, lymph flow measured by scintigraphy rises markedly over rest [10, cohort]. So "movement aids lymph return via the muscle pump" is a genuine, well-characterized physiological claim. ESTABLISHED (mechanism + human imaging).

**Exercise is safe and beneficial in lymphedema — the old "don't exercise the affected limb" advice is debunked.** The PAL trial (Schmitz et al., NEJM 2009): RCT, 141 breast-cancer survivors with stable arm lymphedema, twice-weekly slowly progressive weight lifting over 1 year. Exacerbations requiring a lymphedema specialist were **lower** in the lifting group: **14% vs 29%** (p=0.04); proportion with ≥5% limb-volume increase was similar (11% vs 12%); the lifting group reported greater improvement in lymphedema symptom severity (p=0.03) and greater strength gains, with no serious intervention-related adverse events [11, rct]. The companion at-risk PAL trial (Schmitz et al., JAMA 2010): 154 at-risk survivors; incident BCRL onset 11% (lifting) vs 17% (control), meeting an equivalence/non-inferiority criterion (p for equivalence = .04) [12, rct]. GRADE: progressive resistance + aerobic exercise in/ at-risk-of lymphedema ≈ **moderate certainty × strong recommendation for safety** (it does not worsen, and reduces exacerbations). ESTABLISHED.

**Where it gets overstated.** "Lymphatic exercise routines / lymphatic yoga that drain toxins" overshoot the mechanism: the muscle pump moves lymph, but lymph is not a toxin-disposal stream the body otherwise fails to clear, and there is no evidence that a special "lymphatic" exercise class outperforms ordinary movement for healthy people. The one exercise-recovery signal in healthy adults is thin: a manual "pedal pump" lymphatic technique vs passive recovery after maximal exercise (17 physically active college students) lowered blood lactate only at the 20-minute mark (4.7 vs 6.3 mmol/L, p=0.04), no difference at 4/7/10 min, authors calling for further study [13, rct]. That is a single small crossover trial in healthy athletes — PROVISIONAL, route/population-limited, and not a basis for "lymphatic exercise detoxes you."

**Deep diaphragmatic breathing as a CDT adjunct — weak/PROVISIONAL.** The respiratory pump is a real extrinsic driver [9, mechanism_review], and small trials report immediate post-session limb-volume reductions and symptom improvement when diaphragmatic breathing is added to CDT/limb exercise [14, rct]. But the trials are small, short, and not blinded; the agent should treat diaphragmatic breathing as a low-risk, low-cost adjunct of **low certainty**, not an established standalone intervention. PROVISIONAL.

## D.5 The pseudoscience refutation (load-bearing for this agent)

This domain carries a high marketing load. For **healthy people with no diagnosed lymphatic pathology**, the unifying verdict is: these interventions may produce *transient cosmetic* effects (de-puffing from fluid redistribution and increased superficial circulation) but do **not** "detox," cause fat/weight loss, or "boost immunity" — the liver, kidneys, and a normally-functioning lymphatic system already perform clearance and do not need mechanical assistance. UCLA Health states directly that for detox/weight-loss/immune claims in healthy people "there is no substantial scientific evidence supporting these claims," "there is no evidence that MLD causes weight loss," and "your lymphatic system does not necessarily need your help unless it's not working well" [15, mechanism_review; 16, mechanism_review].

Per-claim characterization (vendor/wellness pages cited only as `vendor_label`/`anecdote_aggregate` to *characterize* the claim, never to ground a number):

- **"Lymphatic drainage massage for detox / weight loss / cellulite / immune boosting" (healthy people).** Marketing claim: massage flushes toxins, slims, boosts immunity. Evidence: no credible support; transient swelling-appearance change ≠ fat loss or detox [15, mechanism_review; 16, mechanism_review]. **Agent rule:** in the absence of diagnosed lymphatic pathology, MLD-for-wellness is **BASIS_NOT_REVIEWABLE / not-evidence-based**; transient cosmetic de-puffing must never be relabeled "detox."
- **Dry brushing "for lymph / detox / cellulite."** Marketing claim: stimulates lymph, removes toxins, reduces cellulite. Evidence: Cleveland Clinic — exfoliation benefit is real; cellulite reduction "isn't supported by any scientific evidence," and apparent improvement is "temporary plumping... from increased blood circulation"; it does not detox in the medical sense [17, mechanism_review]. **Agent rule:** dry brushing = exfoliation (real) + lymphatic/detox/cellulite claims (not-evidence-based).
- **Rebounding / vibration plates "for lymph."** Marketing claim: bouncing/vibration uniquely pumps the lymphatic system. Evidence: movement does drive the muscle/respiratory pump [9, mechanism_review], but there is no evidence a vibration plate or trampoline confers a *lymph-specific* benefit beyond ordinary exercise; consumer-press coverage of vibration devices addresses build quality, not validated lymphatic outcomes [16, mechanism_review]. **Agent rule:** treat as ordinary exercise at best; any "uniquely drains lymph / detoxes" framing is not-evidence-based.
- **"Lymphatic cleanse" supplements / teas.** Marketing claim: ingestible products that "cleanse the lymph." Evidence: no clinical evidence base; these are `vendor_label` claims with no controlled human data [16, mechanism_review]. **Agent rule:** not-evidence-based; additionally any *ingestible* sits outside the lymphatic-specialist's authorship lane (see D.6 routing) — do not endorse and do not author.
- **Gua sha / "facial lymphatic drainage."** Marketing claim: sculpts the face by draining lymph, with lasting contour/health benefit. Evidence: produces *transient* reduction in facial puffiness via fluid redistribution; no evidence of durable contour change or systemic benefit [16, mechanism_review]. **Agent rule:** transient cosmetic de-puffing is the honest ceiling; anything beyond that (lasting sculpting, "drains toxins," "more than transient") is not-evidence-based.

**The single most-overstated claim** (named in self-check) is *"lymphatic drainage massage detoxifies the body"* in healthy people — it conflates a transient fluid-shift cosmetic effect with hepatic/renal detoxification the body already performs, and is the framing the agent must most reliably refuse.

## D.6 Lymphatic-relevant compounds — identify and ROUTE OUT

The lymphatic-specialist reads and cross-references compound entries but does **not author** them and does **not emit doses.** Anything ingestible or risk-bearing routes to the owner: `vault/compounds/`-class entries are owned by the **supplement-specialist**, and any compound at **risk_tier medium+ or any Rx/prescription-status item** routes to the **medical-liaison / prescriber.** For each, one-line evidence status + routing.

- **Benzopyrones / coumarin (5,6-benzo-α-pyrone).** Evidence/safety: the Loprinzi 1999 NEJM RCT (140 women, crossover, 400 mg/day coumarin vs placebo, 6+6 months) found **no benefit on any lymphedema outcome** and a higher hepatotoxicity signal — ~6% (9 women) with significant transaminase elevation [18, rct]; the Cochrane benzopyrones review (Badger 2004) could draw **no reliable conclusion** of effectiveness owing to poor trial quality [19, meta_analysis]; oral coumarin has been **withdrawn / unapproved** in some markets (UK, Australia) over potentially fatal hepatotoxicity, and the FDA has long banned coumarin as a food additive [20, regulatory; 21, regulatory]. **Route-out:** Rx/regulated, hepatotoxic → **prescriber / medical-liaison**; lymphatic-specialist must NOT recommend or dose it. (NEGATIVE recommendation it CAN voice: not evidence-supported and carries a hepatotoxicity signal.)
- **Diosmin + hesperidin / micronized purified flavonoid fraction (MPFF, "Daflon").** Evidence: 90% diosmin / 10% hesperidin phlebotropic agent with RCT support for *chronic venous insufficiency* (reduced ankle/calf circumference, symptom improvement) [22, meta_analysis]; lymphedema is only a listed *accessory* indication with **weaker** evidence, and the Cochrane benzopyrones review's negative verdict extends to flavonoid/benzopyrone-class agents in lymphedema [19, meta_analysis; 22, meta_analysis]. **Route-out:** ingestible compound → **supplement-specialist** authors the entry; risk_tier per that entry governs whether prescriber sign-off is needed.
- **Selenium (sodium selenite).** Evidence: limited — small open-label/early-phase work (e.g., ~36 head-and-neck and secondary-lymphedema patients) reported subjective and Miller-score improvement, but the base is thin and not high-certainty [23, open_label]. Population note: head-and-neck / radiation-associated secondary lymphedema — do not extrapolate to other populations. **Route-out:** ingestible, narrow-therapeutic-window mineral → **supplement-specialist / prescriber**; not authored or dosed by the lymphatic-specialist.
- **Diuretics.** Evidence: **generally NOT effective** for chronic lymphedema (they mobilize water, not the protein-rich interstitial fluid driving the swelling) and are **potentially harmful** (dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, possible aggravation of fibrosis); guidance broadly recommends *against* routine use [24, mechanism_review]. **Route-out:** Rx → **prescriber / medical-liaison.** The lymphatic-specialist may voice the NEGATIVE evidence position (diuretics are not a lymphedema treatment) but must not initiate, adjust, or endorse diuretic therapy.

Routing rule the agent encodes: **protocols + biomarkers = lymphatic-specialist's lane; compounds = NOT its lane** — read/cross-reference yes, author/dose no. Ingestible → supplement-specialist; medium+ risk_tier or Rx → medical-liaison/prescriber.
