# Section C: Measurement & Standardization

## C.1 Measurement Methods: Immunoassay vs. LC-MS/MS

Two analytical approaches dominate clinical DHEA-S measurement: automated competitive immunoassay and liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Each carries distinct accuracy and precision profiles, and the choice of method has direct consequences for result interpretation.

**Automated immunoassay** (ECLIA, CMIA, and enzyme immunoassay formats) runs on platforms such as the Roche cobas e, Abbott Alinity, and Beckman Access. These handle large volumes at low per-test cost and predominate in routine laboratories. Their limitation is antibody specificity: a competitive immunoassay's single antibody cannot distinguish DHEA-S from structurally similar sulfated steroids, so cross-reactivity with free DHEA, pregnenolone sulfate, androsterone sulfate, androstenedione, and estrone-3-sulfate introduces platform-specific positive bias [1, mechanism_review].

**LC-MS/MS**, particularly isotope-dilution LC-MS/MS (ID-LC-MS/MS), separates DHEA-S by chromatographic retention time and identifies it by mass transitions specific to its molecular structure. Cross-reactivity from co-eluting steroids is either absent or flagged by atypical ion ratios. It is the reference-grade method against which immunoassay performance is benchmarked [1, mechanism_review; 2, cohort].

## C.2 Inter-Method Variation: Quantitative Evidence

The practical gap between platforms is not trivial. Büttler et al. (2013) compared seven commercial DHEAS immunoassays to ID-LC-MS/MS across 75 serum samples spanning 0.06–20.6 µmol/L (approximately 2.2–748 µg/dL). Three assays agreed acceptably (regression slopes 0.92–1.07; R = 0.93–0.99); four showed standardization failures with slopes of 0.84, 1.14, 1.20, and 1.28 — representing biases ranging from −16% to +28% relative to the reference. One immunoassay exhibited an intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) of 18%, far exceeding the ≤5.5% seen in the remaining methods and the 4.1% of ID-LC-MS/MS itself. The authors found these findings "in agreement" with data from three national External Quality Assessment Schemes (EQAS) — Dutch, German, and British — indicating the discordance is not a local laboratory artefact but a systemic property of the assay landscape [1, cohort].

Bias is larger at the extremes. In a multisteroid LC-MS/MS validation against the Roche Elecsys ECLIA, DHEA-S immunoassay showed approximately +21.5% mean positive bias — relatively constant across the measurement range, suggesting miscalibration rather than proportional cross-reactivity [5, mechanism_review]. In neonates the divergence is dramatic: Livie et al. (2023) found the Abbott Alinity DHEA-S immunoassay ran at +166% positive bias versus LC-MS/MS in infants under 52 weeks, as fetal adrenal and placental steroid sulfates cross-react with assay antibodies. The bias decreased with age [2, cohort]. LC-MS/MS is the method of choice for neonatal DHEA-S measurement.

## C.3 The Standardization Gap

Unlike cholesterol (CRMLN network) or testosterone and estradiol (CDC Hormone Standardization Program, HoSt), DHEA-S does not have a widely adopted reference-method-based standardization program with mandatory or widely certified participation [3, regulatory]. The CDC HoSt program — which issues reference measurement procedures traceable to certified reference materials — covers testosterone and estradiol; DHEA-S appears in CDC materials only as an orientation value (provided without a certified reference method), not as a harmonized measurand [3, regulatory; 6, mechanism_review].

This gap has direct consequences. Without a single accepted higher-order calibration anchor, each manufacturer calibrates against its own working standards. Traceable calibration in FDA 510(k) submissions for DHEA-S immunoassays has been documented as relying on "gravimetrically produced master calibrators" or internally assigned secondary standards, explicitly noting the absence of "an internationally recognized reference standard or method for DHEA-S analysis." [4, regulatory]. The result is that reference intervals are method-specific and platform-specific — a result of 200 µg/dL on a Roche Elecsys may not be the same clinically as 200 µg/dL on an Abbott Alinity or a Siemens Centaur, because the methods are calibrated independently. Clinicians should interpret DHEA-S results using the reference range established by the specific analyzer in their reporting laboratory, not population norms from another platform.

## C.4 A Genuine Pre-Analytic Advantage: Sampling Time Is Not Critical

One significant practical advantage of DHEA-S over many other adrenal hormones is its pharmacokinetic stability in the circulation. DHEA-S has a long plasma half-life — on the order of approximately 7–10 hours (versus only minutes to a few hours for unconjugated DHEA) — arising from high albumin binding, very low renal clearance, and a slow metabolic clearance rate of approximately 13 L/day compared to DHEA's ~2,000 L/day. Because DHEA-S turns over slowly, serum concentrations do not fluctuate acutely in response to the pulsatile ACTH secretion that drives diurnal patterns in cortisol and, to a lesser extent, free DHEA.

The clinical implication contrasts directly with cortisol: **DHEA-S has no clinically significant diurnal variation**. Levels are stable throughout the day, do not exhibit a morning peak, and do not respond acutely to ACTH administration. Rosenfeld et al. (1975) established this directly: while DHEA and cortisol were episodic and synchronous across 24 hours, DHEA-S displayed a flat, non-pulsatile pattern [7, mechanism_review]. Sampling time is therefore not critical — a morning draw is conventional but a late-afternoon or random-time specimen is equally valid. Fasting is not required. Serum stability is excellent: 5 days at ambient, 14 days refrigerated, 12 months frozen (ARUP Laboratories; Mayo Clinic Laboratories).

## C.5 Interferences

Several analytic interferences warrant attention when reviewing DHEA-S immunoassay results:

**Biotin (high-dose).** Automated DHEA-S immunoassays use streptavidin-biotin solid-phase chemistry. Exogenous biotin from high-dose supplements (commonly 5–10 mg/serving) competes with biotinylated reagents, producing falsely elevated results in the competitive format. Specimens should not be collected until ≥12 hours after the last biotin dose [6, mechanism_review].

**Heterophile antibodies.** Human anti-animal antibodies (HAMA and related HAAs) can bridge assay reagent antibodies and produce spuriously elevated or depressed results. The interference is unpredictable and patient-specific; LC-MS/MS is the definitive confirmatory approach [8, mechanism_review].

**Cross-reactivity with steroid sulfates.** Free DHEA, pregnenolone sulfate, androsterone sulfate, and androstenedione all produce variable degrees of cross-reactivity depending on the assay platform [1, cohort; 9, mechanism_review]. A quantitative study of the Roche Elecsys DHEA-S assay found no compound exceeded 5% cross-reactivity, with pregnenolone sulfate (clinically relevant in pregnancy) and estropipate the only compounds above 0.5% [9, mechanism_review] — but platform generalization is unsafe; manufacturer package insert data apply only to that assay.

**DHEA-S as an interferent in testosterone assays.** Elevated DHEA-S can cross-react in unextracted direct testosterone immunoassays, causing falsely elevated testosterone results in women and children where DHEA-S levels are high relative to testosterone. Middle (2007) documented this across multiple platforms including Abbott Architect, Roche Elecsys, Beckman Access, and Immulite 2000 — a finding that illustrates the general specificity problem in direct steroid hormone immunoassay [10, cohort].

## C.6 Age- and Sex-Matched Reference Intervals: The Critical Interpretive Factor

DHEA-S secretion declines approximately 2% per year after peak production in the third decade, producing a 5- to 8-fold span across adult life strata. Male reference ranges from a major US reference laboratory illustrate the scale: 164–530 µg/dL (4.45–14.4 µmol/L) at ages 20–24, narrowing to 21–226 µg/dL (0.57–6.1 µmol/L) at age ≥75. Applying the wrong age band produces false impression of deficiency or excess. Neonatal DHEA-S is dramatically elevated at birth (fetal zone of the adrenal cortex) and falls precipitously in the first months — physiological, but indistinguishable from pathologic elevation if adult ranges are applied [2, cohort].

Because reference intervals are method-specific (Section C.3), serial monitoring should use the same platform and the same platform's age-stratified intervals. US labs report µg/dL; European labs often use µmol/L. Conversion: 1 µg/dL = 0.02714 µmol/L.
