# Section A: Physiology & What DHEA-S Measures

## The Sulfated Storage Form of the Body's Most Abundant Steroid

Dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S) is the sulfated, water-soluble ester of dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), and together they constitute **the most abundant steroid hormones in human circulation** [1, mechanism_review]. DHEA-S circulates at concentrations of roughly 100–400 µg/dL (2.7–10.8 µmol/L) in young adults — roughly three orders of magnitude above cortisol and several-fold above testosterone — making it an unusually abundant circulating molecule for something that most clinical texts still classify as a "minor" adrenal androgen. This abundance is not incidental: it reflects DHEA-S's biological role as a stable, mobile reservoir of androgen and estrogen precursor material that peripheral tissues draw upon on demand.

## Synthesis: Adrenal Zona Reticularis Under ACTH Drive

The great majority of circulating DHEA-S originates in the **zona reticularis**, the innermost layer of the adrenal cortex [2, mechanism_review]. Synthesis follows the canonical steroidogenic pathway — cholesterol → pregnenolone → 17α-OH pregnenolone → DHEA — catalyzed in the zona reticularis by CYP11A1, CYP17A1, and particularly by the 17,20-lyase activity of CYP17A1, which the fasciculata largely lacks due to lower expression of the allosteric coactivator cytochrome b5. DHEA is then sulfated almost immediately by **sulfotransferase SULT2A1**, which is expressed at high levels in the adrenal reticularis (and also in the liver and small intestine), converting free DHEA to the anionic sulfate conjugate that dominates the bloodstream [1, mechanism_review]. A small additional contribution comes from the gonads (testicular and ovarian tissue) and from peripheral SULT2A1 activity in the liver; the adrenal source, however, is the dominant driver of circulating DHEA-S, particularly in women in whom ovarian androgen synthesis is limited.

ACTH is the primary trophic regulator of zona reticularis function and DHEA-S output, explaining why DHEA-S falls sharply in adrenal insufficiency and rises in states of ACTH excess (e.g., untreated congenital adrenal hyperplasia). However, ACTH alone does not explain the dramatic age-related DHEA-S decline (see below), because ACTH and cortisol secretion remain roughly preserved into old age while DHEA-S falls precipitously — an uncoupling that defines the phenomenon of **adrenopause** and remains incompletely understood at the molecular level [2, mechanism_review].

## The DHEA ⇌ DHEA-S Interconversion

DHEA and DHEA-S interconvert reversibly in vivo through two enzyme classes operating in opposite directions:

- **Sulfotransferases (SULT2A1):** sulfate DHEA → DHEA-S; active in adrenal, liver, gut
- **Steroid sulfatase (STS / arylsulfatase C):** desulfate DHEA-S → DHEA; expressed widely in peripheral tissues including breast, prostate, bone, skin, and brain

This desulfation step is critical because free DHEA — not DHEA-S — is the biologically active precursor that enters the steroidogenic pathway in target tissues. The sulfate group both increases water solubility (facilitating transport in plasma) and dramatically slows metabolic clearance; STS activity in target tissues releases DHEA on-demand, giving each tissue local control over androgen and estrogen production.

## Why DHEA-S Is Measured Instead of DHEA

Clinicians routinely measure DHEA-S rather than free DHEA, and the reasons are primarily pharmacokinetic [3, mechanism_review]:

1. **Half-life:** DHEA-S has an endogenous plasma half-life of approximately **7–10 hours** — substantially longer than free DHEA's terminal half-life of only 15–30 minutes [3, mechanism_review]. This extended half-life results from tight binding to albumin and from the slow rate of desulfation (metabolic clearance rate ~5–20 L/day vs DHEA's ~2,000 L/day). Free DHEA, by contrast, is secreted episodically in tandem with ACTH pulses and cleared rapidly. (Oral DHEA supplementation studies such as Legrain et al. [4, open_label] describe exogenous pharmacokinetics after dosing and are not the source of the endogenous half-life figure.)

2. **No significant diurnal variation:** Unlike cortisol — which peaks within 30–60 minutes of awakening and falls through the day — and unlike free DHEA, which partly mirrors the ACTH/cortisol diurnal rhythm, **DHEA-S shows minimal diurnal fluctuation** [3, mechanism_review]. A blood draw at 8 AM or 4 PM gives essentially the same result.

3. **High, stable plasma concentrations:** The high circulating DHEA-S pool (~100-fold greater than free DHEA) means measurement error is small relative to signal [5, mechanism_review].

4. **Adrenal specificity:** Because SULT2A1 activity in the adrenal reticularis is the dominant driver of circulating DHEA-S, serum DHEA-S serves as a reliable index of **adrenal androgen secretory capacity** that is not contaminated by gonadal contributions in the way free androgens might be [5, mechanism_review].

The practical consequence: a single random serum DHEA-S sample, collected at any time of day without fasting or special preparation, reliably reflects a patient's adrenal androgen output — a diagnostic convenience not shared by any other adrenal steroid.

## DHEA-S as a Prohormone Reservoir: Intracrinology

Neither DHEA-S nor its immediate precursor DHEA has known direct receptor-mediated activity at physiological concentrations. Their biological effects are executed after local conversion in target tissues to active androgens (testosterone, dihydrotestosterone) and estrogens (estradiol, estrone) [6, mechanism_review]. This tissue-based steroidogenesis — termed **intracrinology**, a concept developed by Fernand Labrie and colleagues — means that the hormonal actions of DHEA-S are not exerted systemically but are regulated cell-autonomously within each tissue [7, mechanism_review].

The scale of intracrine contribution is substantial: in premenopausal women, peripheral intracrine synthesis accounts for roughly 40–50% of active androgens; after menopause, when ovarian estrogen secretion ceases, virtually **100% of estrogens** in most target tissues (breast, uterus, bone, skin) are synthesized intracrinally from the DHEA-S → DHEA → androstenedione → estrone/estradiol pathway [7, mechanism_review]. In adult men, approximately 40–50% of active androgens are produced intracrinally. The physiological implication is that serum DHEA-S functions as a circulating "fuel tank" for peripheral steroidogenesis, with each tissue drawing from it according to its own local enzyme expression profile.

Key enzymes involved in peripheral conversion include HSD3B (3β-HSD, converts DHEA → androstenedione), HSD17B (17β-HSD family, converts androstenedione → testosterone), 5α-reductase (converts testosterone → DHT), and aromatase CYP19A1 (converts androstenedione → estrone, testosterone → estradiol). The expression patterns of these enzymes differ across tissues, which is why the same circulating DHEA-S pool yields androgen-dominant products in skin and prostate but estrogen-dominant products in breast tissue.

## The Age Trajectory: Adrenarche to Adrenopause

DHEA-S follows a dramatic and distinctive lifespan trajectory [2, mechanism_review]:

- **Early childhood (0–5 years):** Near-absent. The zona reticularis is morphologically undeveloped and SULT2A1 activity is low.
- **Adrenarche (~age 6–8 years):** DHEA-S begins to rise in a process called adrenarche, coinciding with zona reticularis maturation — characterized by reduced 3β-HSD (HSD3B2) expression relative to the zona fasciculata, which shifts flux toward C19 androgens rather than cortisol [8, mechanism_review]. Adrenarche precedes gonadarche (puberty proper) by 2–3 years and is independent of GnRH/LH/FSH.
- **Young adult peak (20s–30s):** DHEA-S reaches peak concentrations of roughly 200–400 µg/dL (5.4–10.9 µmol/L) in men and 150–300 µg/dL (4.1–8.1 µmol/L) in women. This is the period of maximal adrenal androgen output.
- **Progressive decline (30s onward):** Unlike cortisol secretion, which is largely maintained into old age, DHEA-S declines at roughly **2–3% per year** after the peak, a phenomenon termed **adrenopause** [2, mechanism_review]. The mechanisms are not fully elucidated but include progressive zona reticularis involution, altered intra-adrenal steroidogenic enzyme expression, and possibly reduced sensitivity to ACTH-independent trophic factors.
- **Old age (70s–80s):** Serum DHEA-S falls to approximately **10–20% of the young adult peak** [9, mechanism_review] — values of 20–80 µg/dL (0.5–2.2 µmol/L) are typical — representing a loss of roughly 80–90% of adrenal androgen reserve. This decline is proportionally far greater than the age-related decline in any other major adrenal or gonadal hormone and occurs uniformly across both sexes, though women tend to reach lower absolute values in old age.

Unlike menopause, adrenopause has no discrete biological event marking its onset; it is a continuous and gradual process beginning in the 30s. Its clinical significance — whether the decline in DHEA-S contributes causally to age-associated changes in body composition, bone density, cognition, immune function, and vitality — remains an active area of investigation.
