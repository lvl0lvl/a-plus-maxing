# Section D: Determinants & Clinical Significance

## D.1 Causes of Elevated DHEA-S

### Adrenal Androgen-Secreting Tumors and Adrenocortical Carcinoma

Markedly elevated DHEA-S is one of the most clinically urgent findings in androgen-excess evaluation. A serum DHEA-S above approximately 700 µg/dL in a woman warrants adrenal imaging to exclude an androgen-secreting adrenal neoplasm [1, mechanism_review]. Adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) is particularly associated with very high DHEA-S, as malignant adrenal cells retain the capacity to produce adrenal androgen precursors while losing the ability to convert them efficiently to downstream androgens such as testosterone. DHEA and DHEA-S are elevated in more than 90% of ACC cases, making them among the most sensitive biochemical markers of this neoplasm, alongside urinary 17-ketosteroids. Elevated DHEA-S is found more often in malignant adrenal tumors than in benign adenomas [1, mechanism_review].

### Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (CAH)

In 21-hydroxylase deficiency (CYP21A2), the most common form of CAH, enzymatic blockade diverts steroid precursors into the adrenal androgen pathway, producing marked elevations of androstenedione, DHEA, and DHEA-S alongside high 17-hydroxyprogesterone and low cortisol [1, mechanism_review]. In the rarer 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 deficiency, DHEA-S elevation is particularly pronounced and androstenedione may be low or normal — a pattern that can help distinguish this form from 21-hydroxylase deficiency. Both classic and non-classic CAH are important causes of elevated DHEA-S encountered in adolescents and young adults with hyperandrogenism.

### Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

DHEA-S is modestly elevated in approximately 20–33% of women with PCOS, reflecting a component of adrenal androgen excess in this heterogeneous condition [1, mechanism_review]. Adrenal hyperandrogenism is more prevalent in women with the non-classic PCOS phenotypes (B and C, defined by the Rotterdam criteria) and typically co-exists with ovarian androgen excess rather than representing an isolated finding. The degree of DHEA-S elevation in PCOS is usually mild-to-moderate and rarely reaches the thresholds associated with adrenal tumors.

### Premature Adrenarche

Premature adrenarche — early activation of the zona reticularis before age 8 in girls or age 9 in boys — produces elevated DHEA-S for chronological age. It is generally a benign variant of normal development but should be distinguished from true precocious puberty or a pathological androgen-secreting process.

### Exogenous DHEA Supplementation

Oral DHEA is sold over the counter in the United States and is widely used as a putative "anti-aging" compound. Supplementation reliably raises serum DHEA-S into or above the young-adult reference range; this source of elevation must be considered in any patient presenting with unexpectedly high DHEA-S, particularly in older adults in whom the age-expected trajectory is a gradual decline [2, meta_analysis].

## D.2 Causes of Low DHEA-S

### Adrenal Insufficiency

Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease) and secondary adrenal insufficiency (ACTH deficiency from pituitary or hypothalamic disease) both impair adrenal androgen production. In women, the adrenal cortex is the dominant source of circulating androgens, making adrenal insufficiency a principal cause of androgen deficiency in female patients. A low DHEA-S supports the diagnosis of adrenal insufficiency and tracks the loss of adrenal androgen output — a feature not captured by cortisol replacement monitoring alone. The 2016 Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline on Primary Adrenal Insufficiency (Bornstein et al.) recommends a trial of DHEA replacement (25–50 mg/day) in women with primary adrenal insufficiency who report low libido, depressed mood, or low energy despite optimized glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement, with monitoring via morning serum DHEA-S aiming at the mid-normal range [3, regulatory].

### Hypopituitarism

Loss of anterior pituitary function reduces ACTH secretion, which in turn suppresses zona reticularis activity, producing chronically low DHEA-S. Low DHEA-S in the appropriate clinical context (other pituitary hormone deficiencies, history of pituitary pathology) is a supportive finding of hypopituitarism.

### Glucocorticoid and Exogenous Steroid Suppression

Exogenous glucocorticoids suppress CRH and ACTH secretion via negative feedback on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Because DHEA-S production is ACTH-dependent, even modest doses of exogenous corticosteroids — including inhaled, topical, and intra-articular preparations absorbed systemically — can substantially reduce DHEA-S. Glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency is the most common cause of secondary HPA suppression encountered in clinical practice, and DHEA-S is a sensitive downstream marker of this suppression [1, mechanism_review].

### Aging (Adrenopause)

DHEA-S peaks in early adulthood (typically the mid-20s) and then declines progressively at approximately 2–5% per year, falling by 70–80% between ages 25 and 75 — a phenomenon termed **adrenopause** [4, cohort]. This decline is one of the most consistent and reproducible age-related hormonal changes in both sexes. Age-appropriate reference ranges are mandatory; a DHEA-S of 60 µg/dL is within the expected range for a 75-year-old woman and would not prompt further evaluation, whereas the same value in a 35-year-old warrants investigation.

### Chronic Illness and Anorexia Nervosa

Chronic inflammatory disease, critical illness, and severe nutritional restriction (particularly anorexia nervosa) are associated with suppressed DHEA-S. In these contexts, low DHEA-S may be a marker of physiological stress and HPA dysregulation rather than primary adrenal pathology.

## D.3 The Hyperandrogenism Localization Role

DHEA-S has a well-established function in the clinical workup of androgen excess in women: it helps localize the probable glandular source. Because DHEA-S is produced almost exclusively by the adrenal cortex (unlike testosterone, which can originate from both the adrenal and the ovary), its level provides a first-pass organ discriminator:

- **Elevated DHEA-S with elevated testosterone** → adrenal origin is likely; adrenal imaging and suppression testing are appropriate next steps.
- **Elevated testosterone with normal or low DHEA-S** → ovarian source is more probable (e.g., PCOS, ovarian stromal hyperthecosis, ovarian sex-cord-stromal tumor).

The 2018 Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline on Hirsutism (Martin KA, Anderson RR, Chang RJ et al.) incorporates this principle in the workup of women with rapidly progressive virilization, recommending DHEA-S measurement alongside total testosterone to evaluate for an androgen-secreting adrenal tumor when DHEA-S exceeds approximately 700 µg/dL [5, regulatory]. The dexamethasone suppression test can further refine source attribution: suppression of DHEA-S > 60% alongside cortisol, with failure of testosterone to suppress, points toward an ovarian source; persistent non-suppression of DHEA-S raises concern for adrenal carcinoma or Cushing syndrome.

## D.4 The DHEA Supplementation Evidence: An Honest Appraisal

Oral DHEA is one of the most heavily marketed dietary supplements in the United States and is commonly promoted for anti-aging, cognitive enhancement, and improved body composition. The evidence does not support these claims in healthy older adults.

A meta-analysis of 25 placebo-controlled RCTs by Corona G, Rastrelli G, Giagulli VA, Sila A, Sforza A, Forti G, Mannucci E, Maggi M (PMID 23824417) enrolling 1,353 elderly men (mean follow-up 36 weeks) found that DHEA supplementation produced a small, statistically significant reduction in fat mass, but this effect disappeared after adjusting for DHEA-related conversion to testosterone and estradiol [2, meta_analysis]. No meaningful benefit was observed for lipid or glycemic metabolism, bone health, sexual function, or quality of life. The authors concluded that any body composition effect operates indirectly via downstream hormonal metabolites rather than through DHEA itself.

The 2023 Endocrine Society Scientific Statement "Hormones and Aging" (Cappola AR, Auchus RJ, El-Hajj Fuleihan G, Handelsman DJ, Kalyani RR, McClung M, Stuenkel CA, Thorner MO, Verbalis JG; PMID 37326526) states explicitly that available studies "do not support the widespread use of DHEA supplementation as an anti-aging agent, despite claims otherwise to be found on the internet" [6, regulatory]. Moderately sized trials using 25–50 mg/day in older women demonstrated restoration of DHEA-S to young-adult levels, with minimal if any improvement in muscle mass, strength, or quality of life.

**Legitimate investigational roles** exist but are more limited: DHEA replacement in women with primary adrenal insufficiency experiencing androgen deficiency symptoms has some supportive evidence, as reflected in the Endocrine Society guideline, though even here the evidence base is modest and supplementation is not universally effective [3, regulatory]. Potential roles in selected conditions (e.g., premature ovarian insufficiency, anorexia nervosa) remain under active investigation and are not established indications for routine use.

**Androgenic side effects** — acne, hirsutism, voice deepening, clitoral enlargement — are documented with supplemental doses, particularly in women, and represent a real safety consideration that the marketing of DHEA consistently underemphasizes.

## D.5 Associations with Age-Related Outcomes: Interpreting the Epidemiology

Low DHEA-S has been associated in observational cohorts with increased all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, depression, and lower bone density. These associations must be interpreted with important caveats.

**Mortality:** In the prospective MrOS Sweden cohort (Ohlsson C, Labrie F, Barrett-Connor E, Karlsson MK, Ljunggren O, Vandenput L, Mellström D, Tivesten A; JCEM 2010, PMID 20610590), 2,644 Swedish men aged 69–81 were followed for 4.5 years; low DHEA-S (lowest quartile) predicted all-cause mortality (HR 1.54, 95% CI 1.21–1.96) and cardiovascular mortality independent of inflammatory markers [4, cohort]. This prospective cohort finding is the best-characterized whitelisted evidence linking low DHEA-S to mortality risk in elderly men.

**Depression:** In the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA), Souza-Teodoro LH, de Oliveira C, Walters K, Carvalho LA (Psychoneuroendocrinology 2016, PMID 26600009) examined 3,083 participants at baseline and 3,009 at 4-year follow-up [7, cohort]. Low baseline DHEA-S predicted incident depression at 4-year follow-up; those who developed depression showed DHEA-S approximately 19% lower than those who did not, with the association persisting after adjustment for health and behavioral confounders.

**Causal interpretation is not established.** Confounding by age and illness severity is profound — DHEA-S declines with age and with chronic disease, both of which independently predict mortality and mood disorders. The current evidence is consistent with DHEA-S as a **marker** of overall biological aging and systemic health rather than a causal protective factor. No intervention trial has demonstrated that raising DHEA-S in elderly individuals with low levels reduces mortality, improves cardiovascular outcomes, or prevents depression.

## D.6 Analytical and Clinical Limitations

- **Age-dependence is extreme.** DHEA-S concentrations span more than 10-fold across the human lifespan. Any result must be interpreted against an age- and sex-matched reference range; a value that is reassuringly normal at age 30 is elevated at age 70.
- **Assay variation.** Immunoassay-based DHEA-S measurements can diverge from LC-MS/MS reference methods, particularly near the lower and upper extremes. Retesting, or reflex to a higher-specificity platform, should be considered before clinical action on borderline values.
- **Biological marker, not a standalone diagnosis.** Elevated DHEA-S narrows the differential and guides imaging or suppression testing; it does not diagnose adrenal tumors, CAH, or PCOS in isolation. Low DHEA-S supports adrenal insufficiency but requires corroborating stimulation testing (e.g., ACTH stimulation test) and clinical context.
- **Modest elevations are nonspecific.** DHEA-S in the upper-normal or mildly elevated range is common in young adults, PCOS, and overweight individuals and does not mandate further workup in the absence of other hyperandrogenism signs.
