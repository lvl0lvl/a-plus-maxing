## Section C — Preclinical evidence & the translation gap

### C.1 Foundational mechanistic work: cardiolipin binding and cristae stabilization

The preclinical foundation for elamipretide rests on Szeto and colleagues' 2013 work in *JASN* demonstrating that SS-31 binds cardiolipin at the inner mitochondrial membrane with a dissociation constant (K~D~) of 1.87 ± 0.64 µM [1, animal]. In a rat model of 30-minute bilateral renal ischemia followed by reperfusion (male Sprague-Dawley rats, n=6 per group), saline-treated mitochondria showed a threefold increase in organelle area with near-complete cristae loss by the end of ischemia, while SS-31-treated mitochondria preserved density and cristae architecture. At 24 hours post-reperfusion, serum creatinine in saline controls was 1.72 ± 0.55 mg/dL versus 0.62 ± 0.09 mg/dL in SS-31-treated animals — a near-complete functional protection [1, animal]. The proposed mechanism: SS-31 protects the heme iron of cytochrome c from oxidation, preventing peroxidase-mediated cardiolipin degradation during ischemic stress and thereby maintaining electron transport chain supercomplex organization.

A mechanistic synthesis by Szeto (2014) in *British Journal of Pharmacology* described SS-31 as a "first-in-class cardiolipin-protective compound," synthesizing evidence from multiple rodent and cell models that cristae curvature, respiratory supercomplex stability, and ATP recovery in ischemic tissue are all downstream of cardiolipin protection [2, mechanism_review]. No explicit species n is given in that review, as it aggregates multiple earlier experiments.

### C.2 Cardiac ischemia-reperfusion injury (rat)

An independent replication using serial block-face scanning electron microscopy was performed by Allen, Pennington, Perry et al. (2020, *Communications Biology*) in a rat cardiac I/R model. This group was not the Szeto laboratory; they applied 3D cristae reconstruction to show that elamipretide mitigated disease-induced fragmentation of cristae networks after cardiac ischemia-reperfusion, and that it improved respiratory supercomplex coupling — while notably not protecting against the reduction in total cardiolipin concentration after I/R (a mechanistic nuance: structural preservation is separable from lipid quantity) [3, animal]. Exact group sizes were not recoverable through available access, but the study is published in a Nature Portfolio journal. PMID: 32680996 (DOI: 10.1038/s42003-020-1101-3).

### C.3 Cardiac I/R injury (swine) — independent laboratory

Patel, Johnson, Vapniarsky et al. (2023, *Scientific Reports*) tested elamipretide in a large-animal swine hemorrhagic shock + aortic occlusion model (Yorkshire swine, 59–84 kg; n=6 control vs. n=6 elamipretide), an ischemia-reperfusion paradigm with high translational face validity [4, animal]. Elamipretide reduced crystalloid fluid requirements (25 [5–30] vs. 62.5 [50–90] mL/kg; p=0.01), reduced serum creatinine (2.4 vs. 2.7 mg/dL; p=0.04), and dramatically reduced cardiac troponin release (0.22 vs. 3.20 ng/mL; p=0.03). Authors declared commercial interests in Certus Critical Care but no affiliation with the Szeto laboratory, making this a substantive independent replication in a clinically relevant species [4, animal].

### C.4 Acute kidney injury (rat)

The JASN 2013 paper [1] is the primary peer-reviewed anchor for renal protection, demonstrating near-complete prevention of creatinine elevation in the bilateral I/R model. Supporting evidence from Szeto laboratory patents and earlier publications (cited in multiple reviews) shows SS-31 doses of 0.5–5 mg/kg SC produce dose-dependent protection of mitochondrial structure, ATP recovery, and tubular cell preservation in rat AKI. Long-term rat follow-up data (not independently peer-reviewed as a primary study) showed suppression of inflammasome activation (IL-1β, IL-18) and attenuation of progression to chronic kidney disease at 9 months post-I/R.

### C.5 Aging: skeletal muscle and cardiac function (mouse)

Siegel, Kruse, Percival, Goh et al. (2013, *Aging Cell*) — from the Rabinovitch/Marcinek laboratory at the University of Washington, independent of Szeto — showed that a single injection of SS-31 in 27-month-old C57BL/6 mice (n=5–9 per group; young controls 5 months) rapidly restored in vivo mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupling (P/O ratio fully restored), reduced H~2~O~2~ emission, and improved fatigue resistance and endurance within 1–8 days [5, animal]. This is a distinct laboratory replication of the core mitochondrial-energetics benefit in an aging context.

The follow-on cardiac aging study by Chiao et al. (2020, *eLife*) — Rabinovitch laboratory, Seattle — treated 24-month-old C57BL/6 mice (n=7–10 per group for echocardiography; n=3–7 for female cohort) with SS-31 at 3 µg/g/day via osmotic minipump for 8 weeks [6, animal]. Key results: significant reversal of diastolic dysfunction (early-to-late diastolic velocity ratio restored), normalized proton leak, and reduced mitochondrial superoxide. Systolic function (fractional shortening) was not altered, demonstrating selective diastolic benefit. Exercise capacity improved significantly in treated aged mice. PMID: 32574516.

Long-term treatment (18 to 28 months, n=9–17 per group) by Nickel et al. (2022) in *Aging Pathobiology and Therapeutics* showed sex-specific benefits: males showed enhanced diastolic function, grip strength, and rotarod performance; females showed improved spatial learning and body composition stabilization [7, animal]. PMID: 36250163.

### C.6 Barth syndrome mouse model (tafazzin knockdown)

Russo, De Rasmo, Signorile et al. (2022, *Scientific Reports*) used male tafazzin knockdown (TazKD) mice (4–6 months; WT n=9, TazKD untreated n=9, TazKD+SS-31 n=4) to show SS-31 significantly restored succinate-dependent state 3 and state 4 respiration, promoted respiratory supercomplex formation (SC2 and SC4), and improved oxidative phosphorylation efficiency — without altering the pathological cardiolipin-to-monolysocardiolipin ratio [8, animal]. PMID: 36400945. This separates functional rescue from lipid remodeling and mirrors Allen et al.'s cardiac I/R finding [3].

### C.7 Dose-response: what the preclinical data show

Across rodent studies, effective doses ranged from 0.5 to 5 mg/kg/day SC (renal models) to 3 µg/g/day (cardiac aging). Clinical doses have been 4–40 mg flat-dose SC or 0.05 mg/kg/h IV. No published preclinical study formally reports a U-shaped or inverted dose-response curve for SS-31 in cardiovascular or renal models. Context-dependent effects are evident in a different dimension: in the HFpEF obese ZSF1 rat model (female rats, 12 weeks elamipretide), a 2026 IJMS study found modest increases in complex I/II-linked mitochondrial respiration without translating into diastolic performance improvement — indicating that in a fibrosis- and hypertrophy-dominated phenotype, mitochondrial rescue is necessary but not sufficient for functional benefit [9, animal] (single MDPI-published source; lower-trust — not independently corroborated).

## The Translation Gap

### C.8 Clinical trial outcomes — the negative record

The preclinical program produced positive results across at least five independent indications and three species (rat, mouse, swine). The Phase 2/3 clinical record is largely negative:

**EMBRACE-STEMI (2016, *Eur Heart J*, PMID: 26586786):** 300 STEMI patients randomized (152 active / 148 placebo; 243 completers — 120 active, 123 placebo; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01572909), IV elamipretide 0.05 mg/kg/h for 1 hour. Primary endpoint (CK-MB AUC~0–72h~) showed no significant reduction (placebo: 5785 ± 426 vs. elamipretide: 5570 ± 486 ng·h/mL; P=NS). No improvement on pre-specified MRI, angiographic, ECG, or clinical secondary endpoints. Drug was safe and tolerated. This directly contradicts the cardiac I/R animal data [11, rct]. [population: 300 randomized STEMI patients, adult, mixed sex, acute MI setting]

**PROGRESS-HF Phase 2 (2020, *J Card Fail*, PMID: 32068002):** 71 HFrEF patients (LVEF ≤40%), randomized to placebo, 4 mg, or 40 mg elamipretide SC for 28 days [10, rct]. Change in LVESV from baseline showed no significant differences (4 mg vs. placebo: −0.3 mL, 95% CI −4.6 to 4.0; P=0.90; 40 mg vs. placebo: +2.3 mL, 95% CI −1.9 to 6.5; P=0.28). No significant LVEF improvement.

**TAZPOWER Phase 2/3 (2021, *Genet Med*, PMID from Genetics in Medicine vol. 23: 471–478):** 12 Barth syndrome patients in randomized crossover (40 mg/day, 12 weeks). Neither primary endpoint (6MWT, BTHS Symptom Assessment) was met in the randomized phase. Open-label extension showed some improvement, and longer-term 168-week data suggested progressive functional gains — the strongest clinical signal in any elamipretide program.

**MMPOWER-3 (2023, *Neurology*):** 218 patients with primary mitochondrial myopathy, randomized to elamipretide 40 mg SC vs. placebo for 24 weeks. Neither primary endpoint was met: 6MWT difference was −3.2 meters (P=0.69); PMMSA Total Fatigue Score difference was −0.07 (P=0.37). A post-hoc analysis found significant 6MWT improvement in the nuclear DNA mutation subgroup (particularly mtDNA replisome defects: POLG, TWNK), suggesting the "basket" design combining mtDNA and nDNA patients obscured a genotype-restricted signal [12, rct].

### C.9 The gap and its proposed explanations

The discrepancy between robust, replicated preclinical benefit and predominantly null Phase 2/3 clinical trials constitutes the central tension of the elamipretide story. Several explanations have been proposed in peer-reviewed commentaries:

1. **Trial duration mismatch.** Preclinical aging studies required 8 weeks to 10 months of treatment; clinical trials used 4–28 weeks. Mitochondrial remodeling may require longer exposure in human tissue.

2. **Endpoint and population heterogeneity.** The basket trial design in MMPOWER-3 enrolled a genetically heterogeneous population. The post-hoc nDNA subgroup finding suggests that mtDNA heteroplasmy and the nature of the mitochondrial defect critically determine response — information unavailable at enrollment. Animal models are genetically uniform and the target mechanism is the singular insult.

3. **Guideline-directed therapy masking.** Human HF patients receive multiple background therapies (beta-blockers, ACEi/ARBs, SGLT2i) that may already modulate mitochondrial stress pathways, compressing the therapeutic window that SS-31 occupies. Animal models typically do not simulate this pharmacological milieu.

4. **Bioenergetic context-dependency.** The 2026 IJMS HFpEF rat study [9] found that mitochondrial bioenergetic improvement did not translate into functional benefit when fibrosis and hypertrophy were co-dominant — suggesting the preclinical cardiac models (simple I/R) may not recapitulate the complexity of human HF phenotypes.

5. **Biomarker gap.** The FDA has requested validation of plasma cardiolipin as a companion diagnostic. Without confirmed mitochondrial dysfunction biomarkers at enrollment, clinical trials may include patients whose cardiac/skeletal dysfunction is not driven by the cardiolipin pathway SS-31 targets.

The Barth syndrome (TAZPOWER) program stands as the partial exception: here, the target mechanism (tafazzin mutation → primary cardiolipin remodeling deficiency) is confirmed at enrollment, and open-label extension data are more encouraging — consistent with the hypothesis that mechanistic target-engagement confirmation is the missing link.
