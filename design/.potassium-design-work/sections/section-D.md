# Section D: Determinants & Clinical Significance

Serum potassium occupies a narrow physiological window (3.5–5.0 mmol/L) despite total-body stores of roughly 3,500 mmol, almost all intracellular. Because potassium balance depends on both transcellular distribution and net renal excretion, many common drugs and diseases can push serum concentration to lethal extremes. The clinical danger is bidirectional — both hyperkalemia and hypokalemia generate life-threatening arrhythmias — and a large body of cohort and meta-analytic evidence establishes a U-shaped mortality curve at both ends of the distribution [1, cohort; 2, cohort; 3, meta_analysis].

---

## Hyperkalemia (>5.0–5.5 mmol/L)

### Decreased Renal Excretion (Dominant Mechanism)

The kidney normally excretes 90–95% of absorbed potassium under aldosterone control. Any process that impairs glomerular filtration or tubular secretion raises serum potassium. Acute and chronic kidney disease are the single most common substrate for clinically significant hyperkalemia; as eGFR falls below 30 mL/min/1.73 m², the kidneys lose reserve excretory capacity and dietary potassium accumulates.

Drugs that interrupt the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) are the second major cause. ACE inhibitors and ARBs blunt angiotensin II–driven aldosterone synthesis, reducing distal tubular potassium secretion. In patients without CKD or other risk factors, the risk is modest (≤2%). In the presence of heart failure, CKD, or diabetes, hyperkalemia rates rise to 5–10% with monotherapy and higher with combination blockade [4, mechanism_review]. Mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonists (MRAs) — spironolactone, eplerenone, finerenone — directly block aldosterone's effect on principal cells and carry a clinically meaningful hyperkalemia burden: in EMPHASIS-HF, serious hyperkalemia (≥6 mmol/L) occurred more frequently in the eplerenone arm than placebo, though it was generally manageable by dose reduction. Potassium-sparing diuretics (amiloride, triamterene) block the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) in the collecting duct, impairing the electrochemical gradient that drives potassium secretion. NSAIDs suppress prostaglandin-mediated renin release, lower aldosterone, and reduce glomerular filtration — a triple hit. Calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine, tacrolimus) cause hyperkalemia through direct suppression of Na/K-ATPase and blunted aldosterone responsiveness. Trimethoprim blocks ENaC similarly to amiloride and is a significant and underappreciated cause of drug-induced hyperkalemia. Heparin and low-molecular-weight heparin suppress aldosterone synthesis directly, sometimes producing clinically relevant rises in potassium during prolonged use.

Hypoaldosteronism from any cause — Addison's disease, bilateral adrenalectomy, or the hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronemic form known as Type 4 renal tubular acidosis (RTA) — abolishes the main drive for distal potassium excretion. Type 4 RTA is particularly prevalent in elderly diabetics with mild-to-moderate CKD and frequently co-exists with RAAS-inhibitor use, creating additive risk.

### Transcellular Shift Out of Cells

Metabolic acidosis drives potassium out of cells via H⁺/K⁺ exchange across cell membranes; each 0.1-unit fall in arterial pH raises serum potassium approximately 0.4–0.6 mmol/L, though inorganic acidoses (HCl, lactic) produce larger shifts than organic (ketoacidosis). Insulin deficiency in diabetic ketoacidosis causes profound transcellular release: serum potassium is often elevated at presentation despite total-body depletion, masking the degree of deficiency that emerges as insulin is replaced.

Massive tissue breakdown floods the extracellular space with intracellular potassium. Rhabdomyolysis (crush injury, severe exertion, statins + drug interactions), tumor lysis syndrome (rapid lysis of lymphoma or leukemia cells), large burns, and intravascular hemolysis are the classical scenarios. In tumor lysis, the speed of cell breakdown and degree of accompanying oliguric renal failure determine whether hyperkalemia is mild or acutely lethal.

Non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol) impair the beta-2-adrenergic stimulation of Na/K-ATPase that normally drives potassium into cells, raising serum potassium modestly. Digoxin toxicity poisons Na/K-ATPase globally, producing severe transcellular release that is particularly dangerous because the same toxicity sensitizes the myocardium to arrhythmia. Succinylcholine, used for neuromuscular blockade, depolarizes the motor endplate and causes potassium to exit all muscle cells simultaneously; in patients with denervation injury, burns, or immobilization, the potassium efflux can be massive and rapidly fatal. Hyperkalemic periodic paralysis is a rare channelopathy (SCN4A mutations) producing episodic attacks.

### Pseudohyperkalemia — Must Be Excluded First

Before treating, pseudohyperkalemia must be excluded. Potassium is released from platelets during clotting (serum > plasma by 0.1–0.4 mmol/L normally; far more with thrombocytosis), from leukocytes in extreme leukocytosis, and from red cells with hemolysis during phlebotomy (tight tourniquet, small-gauge needle, fist-clenching, delayed processing). A repeat measurement in plasma (or promptly processed whole blood) resolves most cases. Pseudohyperkalemia carries no arrhythmia risk; treating it risks iatrogenic hypokalemia.

## Hypokalemia (<3.5 mmol/L)

### Renal and Gastrointestinal Losses

Loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide) and thiazides are the leading pharmacological causes of hypokalemia in ambulatory patients. Both increase distal tubular flow and sodium delivery, augmenting aldosterone-driven potassium secretion. Patients on chronic diuretic therapy for heart failure or hypertension routinely develop potassium depletion, and supplementation is often required [6, mechanism_review].

Vomiting causes hypokalemia primarily through renal loss (metabolic alkalosis raises aldosterone and bicotanuria drives potassium excretion), not gastric fluid loss per se. Diarrhea and laxative abuse cause direct GI potassium loss. Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome) produces classic hypokalemia with hypertension; secondary hyperaldosteronism (heart failure, cirrhosis, renal artery stenosis) causes hypokalemia via the same aldosterone mechanism. Cushing's syndrome and exogenous glucocorticoids produce mineralocorticoid-like effects. Bartter syndrome (loop-of-Henle transporters) and Gitelman syndrome (distal convoluted tubule NCC transporter) are hereditary salt-wasting conditions that present with hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, and, in Gitelman's, concurrent hypomagnesemia. Renal tubular acidosis types 1 (distal) and 2 (proximal) both impair acid excretion and drive renal potassium wasting. Amphotericin B damages tubular membranes, creating an ionic leak.

### Transcellular Shift Into Cells

Insulin drives potassium into cells via Na/K-ATPase; exogenous insulin administration and the endogenous insulin surge of glucose loading are clinically important. Beta-2 agonists (salbutamol/albuterol, used in asthma and for acute hyperkalemia treatment) stimulate the same ATPase. Metabolic alkalosis drives potassium intracellularly via H⁺/K⁺ exchange. Refeeding syndrome after prolonged starvation produces acute hypokalemia (and hypophosphatemia, hypomagnesemia) as anabolism restarts and cells re-uptake electrolytes. Thyrotoxic periodic paralysis is a dramatic form occurring disproportionately in East Asian males; thyroid hormone up-regulates beta-adrenergic sensitivity of Na/K-ATPase.

### Hypomagnesemia → Refractory Hypokalemia

A clinically critical association: hypomagnesemia causes renal potassium wasting that renders hypokalemia refractory to potassium replacement alone [5, mechanism_review]. The mechanism is molecular: intracellular magnesium tonically blocks the apical ROMK channel (renal outer medullary K⁺ channel) in the distal nephron. When intracellular Mg²⁺ falls — as in alcoholism, diuretic use, proton-pump-inhibitor use, or diarrheal illness — ROMK channel pore inhibition is lifted, driving unrestrained potassium secretion into the tubular lumen. The kidney cannot retain potassium regardless of how much is administered. The clinical rule: in any patient with refractory hypokalemia, measure and replace magnesium before concluding the potassium deficit cannot be corrected.

## Clinical Significance

### Cardiac Arrhythmia and Sudden Death

At both extremes of the potassium range, the resting membrane potential and action potential duration of cardiac myocytes are disturbed in ways that lower the threshold for malignant arrhythmia. Hyperkalemia depolarizes the resting membrane, initially producing peaked T-waves, then PR prolongation, QRS widening, and — at severe levels — a sinusoidal pattern preceding ventricular fibrillation or asystole. Hypokalemia prolongs cardiac repolarization (QT interval, U-wave emergence) and promotes torsades de pointes, ventricular fibrillation, and potentiates digoxin toxicity by competing with the drug's binding site on Na/K-ATPase. The arrhythmia risk of hypokalemia is well-established in acute MI populations [1, cohort].

### U-Shaped Mortality Across Populations

The mortality risk of potassium dysregulation is not limited to extremes; even modest deviations from the 4.0–4.5 mmol/L optimum carry measurable excess mortality. Three complementary bodies of evidence establish this:

**In Acute MI:** Goyal et al. analyzed 38,689 patients hospitalized with biomarker-confirmed AMI at 67 US hospitals (2000–2008). In multivariable analysis, potassium ≥5.5 mmol/L carried an adjusted OR for in-hospital mortality of 6.44 (95% CI 4.27–9.70) versus the 3.5–4.0 mmol/L reference; even the 4.5–5.0 mmol/L range carried OR 1.99 (95% CI 1.68–2.36), demonstrating that mortality risk rises steeply with potassium at both ends of the distribution [1, cohort].

**In Heart Failure:** Núñez et al. tracked 2,164 patients with chronic heart failure over a median follow-up of 2.8 years (16,116 potassium observations; 1,090 deaths). On a continuous scale, the association between potassium and mortality was U-shaped. Adjusted hazard ratios versus normokalemia: hypokalemia HR 2.35 (95% CI 1.40–3.93); hyperkalemia HR 1.55 (95% CI 1.11–2.16). Dynamic changes in potassium also predicted mortality; normalization was independently associated with lower risk [2, cohort].

**Across the Full Kidney-Function Spectrum:** The CKD Prognosis Consortium meta-analysis (Kovesdy, Matsushita, Grams et al.) pooled 27 international cohorts including 1,217,986 participants from general population, high-cardiovascular-risk, and CKD cohorts, followed a mean of 6.9 years. The risk relationship was U-shaped, lowest at 4–4.5 mmol/L. Compared to a reference of 4.2 mmol/L, adjusted HR for all-cause mortality was 1.22 (95% CI 1.15–1.29) at 5.5 mmol/L and 1.49 (95% CI 1.26–1.76) at 3.0 mmol/L. Findings were consistent across eGFR strata, albuminuria categories, and RAAS-inhibitor use — indicating the mortality signal is not confounded by drug use [3, meta_analysis].

A broad comorbidity analysis (Collins et al., n = 911,698) further demonstrated that all-cause mortality over 18 months was U-shaped in every disease subgroup: optimal range 4.0–5.0 mmol/L in controls; mortality rose sharply with both low and high potassium in heart failure (22%), CKD (17%), diabetes (7%), and combined disease (30%) populations [7, cohort].

### The RAAS-Inhibitor–Hyperkalemia Dilemma

The drugs that most powerfully reduce cardiorenal mortality — ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and MRAs — are exactly the drugs most likely to cause hyperkalemia, especially in patients already at risk (CKD, diabetes, older age). This tension is the central management dilemma in nephrology and cardiology. Palmer's NEJM review quantified the risk gradient: hyperkalemia rates are ≤2% in uncomplicated hypertension on RAAS monotherapy but rise to 5–10% with CKD or heart failure, and higher still with combinations [4, mechanism_review]. The practical consequence is that many high-risk patients have RAAS inhibitors inadequately dosed or prematurely discontinued when hyperkalemia appears — forfeiting mortality benefit.

Two novel potassium binders have changed the management equation. Patiromer (a polymer cation exchanger) was evaluated in 243 patients with CKD on at least one RAAS inhibitor and hyperkalemia: potassium fell by 1.01 mmol/L at 4 weeks and 76% achieved target normokalemia; in the withdrawal phase, patiromer-treated patients had significantly lower recurrence of hyperkalemia than placebo (p<0.001) [8, rct]. Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (ZS-9), a highly selective inorganic microporous zirconium silicate, achieved normokalemia in 80–94% of hyperkalemic patients at 48 hours across 5 g and 10 g doses in a phase-3 double-blind trial (n=753) [9, rct]. Both agents enable continued or up-titrated RAAS-inhibitor therapy without persistent hyperkalemia — a therapeutic strategy directly addressing the U-shaped mortality curve by allowing the drugs that prevent cardiorenal events to remain in place.

## Limitations

Serum potassium is a concentration, not a measure of total-body stores. Because roughly 98% of total potassium is intracellular, large transcellular shifts can dramatically change serum potassium without altering whole-body content — and vice versa, large depletions may occur with a seemingly near-normal serum level. Pseudohyperkalemia (hemolysis, extreme thrombocytosis, extreme leukocytosis, tourniquet artefact) must always be excluded before treatment. Potassium results must be interpreted in context: renal function (eGFR), acid-base status, magnesium level, the full medication list, ECG changes, and clinical signs integrate the picture in a way the serum value alone cannot.
