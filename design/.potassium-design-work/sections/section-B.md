# Section B: Reference Ranges, Units & Interpretation

## Units and the Serum–Plasma Distinction

Serum potassium (K⁺) is reported in millimoles per litre (mmol/L), which is numerically identical to milliequivalents per litre (mEq/L) because potassium is monovalent (charge +1). No conversion factor is needed between the two unit systems.

The canonical adult reference interval for **serum** K⁺ is **3.5–5.0 mmol/L**; some laboratories report the upper bound as 5.2 mmol/L [1, regulatory]. The intracellular K⁺ concentration (~150 mmol/L) vastly exceeds the extracellular compartment, a gradient maintained by Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and exploited by excitable tissues for action-potential generation [2, mechanism_review].

**Serum versus plasma:** Serum K⁺ is systematically ~0.2–0.5 mmol/L higher than plasma K⁺ because potassium is released from platelets during the clotting process that generates serum [6, mechanism_review]. In ordinary healthy individuals this gap is ~0.2–0.5 mmol/L. In thrombocytosis, the serum–plasma K⁺ difference (Dk) widens substantially and correlates with platelet count, though the relationship is non-linear: Sevastos et al. reported Dk ranging from 0.40 to 2.61 mmol/L across patients with thrombocytosis, and introduced the Dk100 index (Dk × 100,000 ÷ platelet count per mm³) precisely because measured Dk was disproportionately lower than expected at very high platelet counts [6, mechanism_review]. This is why a **plasma** sample drawn into a heparinised tube is the preferred specimen whenever pseudohyperkalemia from thrombocytosis is clinically suspected.

## Severity Classification

### Hyperkalemia (serum K⁺ > 5.0–5.5 mmol/L)

The diagnostic threshold for hyperkalemia varies by institution and guideline, but a pragmatic expert-consensus classification used in emergency settings divides severity as follows [5, regulatory]:

| Grade | K⁺ (mmol/L) | Clinical urgency |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|
| Mild | 5.0–6.0 | Monitor; identify and treat cause |
| Moderate | 6.1–6.5 | Urgent ECG; pharmacological treatment |
| Severe | > 6.5 | Immediate intervention; cardiac monitoring mandatory |

### Hypokalemia (serum K⁺ < 3.5 mmol/L)

| Grade | K⁺ (mmol/L) |
|-------|-------------|
| Mild | 3.0–3.4 |
| Moderate | 2.5–2.9 |
| Severe | < 2.5 |

Severe hypokalemia (< 2.5 mmol/L) carries substantial risk of life-threatening arrhythmia and respiratory muscle weakness [4, mechanism_review]. Life-threatening hypokalemia (< 2.0 mmol/L) may occur in untreated distal renal tubular acidosis and similar states of extreme potassium wasting [4, mechanism_review].

## The First Interpretive Question: Real or Spurious?

Before any clinical action is taken on an elevated K⁺ result, **pseudohyperkalemia must be excluded**. Common causes [6, mechanism_review]:

- **Hemolysis** during venipuncture or sample transport (K⁺ leaks from red cells; the most frequent cause)
- **Prolonged sample transport or delayed centrifugation** at room temperature (K⁺ leaks from red and white cells)
- **Fist-clenching** during blood draw (local muscle activity releases K⁺)
- **Marked leukocytosis or thrombocytosis** (K⁺ released from fragile or activated cells during clotting)
- **Familial pseudohyperkalemia** (rare; temperature-dependent K⁺ leakage from red cells)

A serum–plasma K⁺ discrepancy > 0.4 mmol/L on simultaneously drawn samples is the diagnostic threshold for pseudohyperkalemia [6, mechanism_review]. When genuine pseudohyperkalemia is confirmed, clinical decisions should be based on plasma (or whole-blood) K⁺, not serum.

Once a true K⁺ elevation is confirmed, the second interpretive step is to classify its mechanism: **transcellular shift** (redistribution without change in total body K⁺ — e.g., acidosis, insulin deficiency, beta-blockade, cell lysis) versus **true total-body excess** (renal retention, excessive intake) versus **impaired renal handling** (hypoaldosteronism, CKD, medications such as ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim).

## ECG Correlation: Why Potassium Is the Most Acutely Dangerous Electrolyte

The cardiac action potential depends critically on transmembrane K⁺ gradients. Both hyperkalemia and hypokalemia alter resting membrane potential and ion-channel kinetics in predictable but imperfectly correlated ways [2, mechanism_review].

### Hyperkalemia: Progressive ECG Changes

As K⁺ rises above 5.5–6.0 mmol/L, a classic sequence of ECG abnormalities unfolds [2, mechanism_review; 3, cohort]:

1. **Peaked (tented) T waves** — the earliest and most recognised sign; symmetrical, narrow, high-amplitude T waves in the precordial leads reflect global shortening of action-potential duration as inward-rectifier K⁺ current (IK1) is enhanced [2, mechanism_review].
2. **PR interval prolongation** — slowed atrioventricular conduction.
3. **P-wave flattening and eventual loss** — atrial muscle is more sensitive to depolarisation block than ventricular tissue; P waves broaden, flatten, and disappear as K⁺ approaches 6.5–7.0 mmol/L.
4. **QRS widening** — reduced conduction velocity in the His-Purkinje system and ventricular myocardium; QRS > 120 ms indicates severe toxicity.
5. **Sine-wave pattern** — progressive widening and merging of the QRS and T wave; the ECG resembles a sinusoidal wave.
6. **Ventricular fibrillation or asystole** — terminal event.

Two critical caveats from the literature: (a) **ECG changes are poorly sensitive** — in a retrospective series of 90 hyperkalemic patients with K⁺ ≥ 6.0 mmol/L, sensitivity across ECG criteria ranged from 18% (strict: new peaked and symmetric T waves) to 52% (any ECG change), and the authors concluded that no reliable diagnostic threshold could be established [3, cohort]; when physician readers were tested using ECGs from patients with K⁺ > 6.5 mmol/L, sensitivity reached only 55–62%, meaning 38–45% of severe cases had ECGs that readers did not recognise as abnormal [3, cohort]. (b) **The rate of rise matters as much as the absolute level.** Chronic hyperkalemia at 6.5 mmol/L may produce minimal ECG changes because compensatory mechanisms have had time to adapt; the same concentration rising acutely can be immediately life-threatening [2, mechanism_review]. The ECG therefore guides urgency but cannot rule out dangerous hyperkalemia when normal.

### Hypokalemia: ECG Changes and Arrhythmia Risk

Hypokalemia reduces outward K⁺ currents (IK1, IKr, IKs, Ito), prolonging action-potential duration and increasing the risk of triggered arrhythmias [2, mechanism_review]:

- **Decreased T-wave amplitude / T-wave flattening or inversion** — earliest change
- **Prominent U waves** — positive deflections following the T wave, best seen in V2–V4; arise from delayed repolarisation of the Purkinje system
- **Apparent QT/QU prolongation** — often reflects T-U wave fusion rather than true QT lengthening; creates the functional equivalent of a long-QT state
- **ST-segment depression**
- **Increased ventricular ectopy** — premature ventricular contractions, polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (torsades de pointes), ventricular fibrillation at severe levels (K⁺ < 2.5 mmol/L) [4, mechanism_review]

ECG changes from hypokalemia are typically absent until K⁺ falls below about 2.7–3.0 mmol/L; they become reliable markers only in moderate-to-severe hypokalemia [4, mechanism_review].

**Digoxin toxicity amplification:** Hypokalemia dramatically potentiates digoxin toxicity. Digoxin inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase; low extracellular K⁺ impairs reactivation of that pump independently. The combination produces intracellular Ca²⁺ overload and ventricular arrhythmias at digoxin levels that would be non-toxic in a normokalemic patient [4, mechanism_review]. This interaction is a primary reason hypokalemia should be urgently corrected in any patient receiving a cardiac glycoside. Similarly, concomitant QT-prolonging drugs (antiarrhythmics, certain antipsychotics, macrolides) compound the arrhythmia risk.

## The Hypokalemia–Hypomagnesemia Link

A critical and frequently under-recognised relationship: **hypomagnesemia causes refractory hypokalemia** that does not correct with potassium repletion alone [7, mechanism_review]. The mechanism involves magnesium-dependent inhibition of ROMK (renal outer medullary potassium) channels in the distal nephron collecting duct. When intracellular Mg²⁺ falls, this inhibitory block is released, leading to unrestrained renal K⁺ secretion regardless of serum K⁺ status [7, mechanism_review]. In any patient with persistent hypokalemia despite adequate K⁺ supplementation, serum magnesium should be measured and, if low, corrected first. Magnesium repletion is often the key that unlocks potassium retention [7, mechanism_review].
