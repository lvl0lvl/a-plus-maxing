# Section C: Measurement & Standardization

## Ion-Selective Electrode: The Standard Method

Serum and plasma potassium is measured universally by **ion-selective electrode (ISE)**, a potentiometric method that generates a voltage proportional to the activity of K⁺ ions in solution. Two configurations exist, and the distinction matters:

- **Indirect ISE** (the dominant mode on main laboratory chemistry analysers): the sample is diluted 1:20 to 1:34 with an ionic-strength buffer before measurement. This reports potassium *concentration* in plasma or serum (mmol/L), calibrated against aqueous standards. It is susceptible to the electrolyte-exclusion effect when large macromolecules displace plasma water (relevant for sodium in extreme dyslipidaemia or hyperproteinaemia; less consequential for K⁺ in normal plasma).
- **Direct ISE** (blood-gas analysers and point-of-care [POC] devices): the undiluted whole-blood or plasma sample contacts the electrode membrane directly. It measures ion *activity* in plasma water (mmol/kg H₂O). Because no dilution step intervenes, direct ISE is free of the electrolyte-exclusion artefact and is the method of choice when a rapid, matrix-faithful reading is required — notably for confirming a spuriously elevated result from a routine-lab sample.

Both modes use liquid-junction or solid polymer membranes selective for K⁺ (typically valinomycin-based). Results from direct and indirect ISE agree closely in normal samples; discordance is itself diagnostically informative.

## Pseudohyperkalemia: The Dominant Source of Error

Potassium is the electrolyte most vulnerable to pre-analytic artefact, because K⁺ is overwhelmingly intracellular: erythrocyte [K⁺] is approximately 100–115 mmol/L (≈25–40× the plasma concentration of ~4 mmol/L). Any event that ruptures cells or impairs the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase *after* blood draw will release K⁺ into the surrounding plasma or serum, producing a falsely elevated result. This artefact — **pseudohyperkalemia** — is the single most common cause of an apparent hyperkalemia referred to the laboratory, and recognising its five forms prevents unnecessary and potentially harmful treatment [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review].

### 1. Hemolysis — the leading cause

In-vitro hemolysis is responsible for more spurious K⁺ elevations than all other causes combined. Even mild hemolysis — from a difficult draw, a small-gauge needle, excessive negative pressure (vacuum tube), tube underfill, or vigorous mixing — liberates K⁺ from ruptured erythrocytes. The intracellular-to-plasma concentration ratio of approximately 25–40 means that lysis of a small fraction of the red cell mass produces a disproportionately large rise in measured K⁺.

Quantitatively, the relationship is near-linear: each **1 g/L of free hemoglobin (HI = 100 mg/dL)** raises measured potassium by approximately **0.23–0.33 mmol/L** — roughly 0.26–0.29 mmol/L in the DiToro ISE cohort [3, cohort], ~0.23–0.25 mmol/L at the threshold levels studied by Balasubramanian et al. on a blood-gas analyser [4, cohort], and ~0.33 mmol/L averaged over the 0–4.5 g/L range in the Koseoglu interference study [5, cohort]. A moderately hemolysed specimen (free Hb 1–4.5 g/L) can elevate K⁺ by 1.5 mmol/L or more, pushing a true 4.5 mmol/L into apparent danger-range hyperkalemia [5, cohort]. The **hemolysis index (HI)**, a spectrophotometric estimate of free hemoglobin concentration reported by most modern chemistry analysers, is the first-line flag; laboratories apply specimen-specific HI rejection thresholds (often 20–100 mg/dL) calibrated to a clinically significant ΔK of 0.1–0.3 mmol/L [3, cohort; 4, cohort].

### 2. Fist-clenching and prolonged tourniquet — the classic outpatient artefact

Repeated forearm-muscle contraction (fist-pumping) during phlebotomy elevates local K⁺ through two mechanisms: depolarisation-driven efflux from muscle cells and local venous stasis (tourniquet longer than ~60 seconds compounds the effect by reducing pH and impairing the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase). The rise is real at the sampling site even though the patient is not systemically hyperkalemic. The magnitude is typically 0.2–2.0 mmol/L but can exceed 3 mmol/L in prolonged or forceful contractions [1, mechanism_review; 6, cohort]. This is an under-recognised outpatient artefact: fasting patients asked to "pump their fist" to raise a vein are the prototypical scenario. The fix is a single hand-closure to raise the vein, then complete relaxation before needle insertion, with tourniquet time minimised [1, mechanism_review].

### 3. Thrombocytosis and extreme leukocytosis — the serum-specific artefact

Platelets are rich in intracellular K⁺ and release it during the clotting process that converts blood to serum. At normal platelet counts (~150–400 × 10⁹/L) this contributes the physiological serum-plasma offset of 0.2–0.4 mmol/L [7, cohort]. When platelet counts are markedly elevated — primary thrombocythaemia, polycythaemia vera, post-splenectomy states — the released load becomes clinically significant. The serum-plasma K⁺ difference rises proportionally with platelet count and can exceed 2 mmol/L when platelets exceed 1 000 × 10⁹/L [2, mechanism_review; 8, cohort]. A case report illustrates the extremes vividly: a patient with essential thrombocythaemia (platelets 1 305 × 10⁹/L) had a serum K⁺ of 6.3 mmol/L, lithium-heparin plasma K⁺ of 4.6 mmol/L, and whole-blood direct-ISE K⁺ of 3.4 mmol/L — a 2.9 mmol/L gap attributable entirely to clotting artefact [8, cohort].

Extreme leukocytosis (WBC > 100 × 10⁹/L, as in acute leukaemia) similarly causes K⁺ release if the white cells lyse during centrifugation, though occasionally the reverse phenomenon occurs (see below). The practical corrective: whenever thrombocytosis or marked leukocytosis is present, collect a **lithium-heparin (plasma) tube and centrifuge immediately** rather than waiting for a serum tube to clot. Serum K⁺ values in these patients are unreliable by design.

### 4. Delayed analysis, cold storage, and pneumatic-tube transport

The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase that actively maintains K⁺ inside erythrocytes is temperature-sensitive. When blood sits uncentrifuged at room temperature for more than ~60–90 minutes, or is refrigerated (which strongly inhibits the pump), K⁺ leaks out of cells continuously. In winter, specimens transported over long distances to a central laboratory can show pseudohyperkalemia purely from cold-induced pump inhibition — a seasonal pattern documented in rural and remote settings [1, mechanism_review]. Pneumatic-tube transport can also mechanically haemolyse fragile leukaemic cells, creating a pseudohyperkalemia that would not appear in hand-carried specimens [9, cohort]. The pre-analytic standard is centrifugation within 30–60 minutes of collection and analysis of separated plasma/serum within 4 hours, with no refrigeration of whole blood prior to centrifugation.

### 5. Hereditary pseudohyperkalemia

A rare autosomal-dominant condition (linked to mutations in *PIEZO1*, *KCNN4*, or *ABCB6* in some pedigrees) in which the red-cell membrane shows a temperature-dependent passive K⁺ leak. At 37 °C the in-vivo plasma K⁺ is normal; after the blood cools to room temperature during transport and storage, K⁺ exits red cells progressively. Affected individuals are non-anaemic, have no true hyperkalemia, and are identified by the diagnostic hallmark of serum K⁺ normalising when blood is processed at 37 °C. This is a diagnosis of exclusion; it should be considered when all extrinsic pre-analytic causes have been ruled out and the family history is positive [1, mechanism_review].

## Confirming a True Value

When pseudohyperkalemia is suspected, the confirmation hierarchy is:

1. **Whole-blood direct ISE on a blood-gas or POC analyser**, collected into a heparinised syringe and measured immediately: bypasses every post-collection artefact. This is the fastest and most reliable confirmatory test.
2. **Parallel serum vs. lithium-heparin plasma comparison**: a serum-plasma K⁺ difference > 0.5 mmol/L in the absence of visible hemolysis is the classical definition of pseudohyperkalemia [2, mechanism_review; 7, cohort].
3. **Hemolysis index**: if elevated, quantify expected ΔK and judge whether the measured excess is explained.
4. **Repeat draw** with meticulous technique — no fist-clenching, tourniquet ≤ 60 s, large-gauge needle, immediate gentle inversion, rapid transport, prompt centrifugation.

**Reverse pseudohyperkalemia** — plasma K⁺ exceeding serum K⁺ — is a rare artefact seen in haematological malignancies (typically chronic lymphocytic leukaemia) where fragile leukaemic cells survive intact in the fibrin clot of a serum tube but lyse during centrifugation of the heparinised plasma tube. Recognition requires comparing both specimen types [9, cohort].

## Standardization, Traceability, and the Serum–Plasma Offset

ISE calibration is traceable to NIST Standard Reference Material **SRM 956d** (Electrolytes in Frozen Human Serum), which provides certified K⁺ concentrations for accuracy verification. IFCC/CLSI guidelines specify calibration frequency, electrode maintenance intervals, and quality-control acceptance criteria.

The **serum-plasma offset** (serum K⁺ typically 0.2–0.4 mmol/L higher than lithium-heparin plasma) is a reproducible, method-dependent phenomenon arising from platelet K⁺ release during clotting [7, cohort]. Despite this, most laboratories apply identical reference intervals to both specimen types — a practice that leads to systematic miscategorisation of borderline results [7, cohort]. The correct approach is specimen-type-specific reference intervals; at minimum, clinicians should know which matrix their laboratory reports.

Reference intervals for serum K⁺ (typically 3.5–5.1 mmol/L in adults) are established by indirect ISE on fasting, correctly collected, promptly processed samples. Pre-analytic quality is not a footnote: the literature consistently identifies pre-analytic factors — hemolysis, delayed centrifugation, fist-clenching, cold transport — as the dominant source of erroneous potassium results in routine practice, dwarfing analytical imprecision from the ISE itself [1, mechanism_review; 3, cohort; 5, cohort].
