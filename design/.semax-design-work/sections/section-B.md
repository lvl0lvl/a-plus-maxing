# Section B — Clinical and Cognitive Efficacy

## Overview and Evidence Architecture

Semax (Met-Glu-His-Phe-Pro-Gly-Pro; ACTH(4-7)-Pro-Gly-Pro) has been studied clinically in Russia since the early 1990s across four principal indication clusters: acute ischemic stroke / cerebrovascular insufficiency, optic and ischemic optic neuropathy, cognitive and attentional enhancement, and ALS/motor neuron disease (quality-of-life endpoint). A separate grey-market structural variant — N-acetyl-semax-amidate — has **no published human clinical data** (PubMed count: 0 for "N-acetyl semax amidate" in any clinical or human context; the claims circulating in nootropic communities are entirely extrapolated from parent-Semax animal work). That distinction is load-bearing and must not be collapsed.

The evidence base carries a single-lineage problem that runs through every indication: the overwhelming majority of clinical work originates from two overlapping groups — the Institute of Molecular Genetics RAS (IMG-RAS, Miasoedov / Myasoedov, Grivennikov, Andreeva, Dolotov) and the Russian neurology stroke group (Gusev, Skvortsova, at First Moscow State Medical University / Research Center of Mental Health). No independently replicated RCT exists from a Western or non-Russian academic centre, and PubMed returns **zero results** for "Semax" combined with "placebo-controlled double-blind" independent replication. There are also **no systematic reviews or meta-analyses** of Semax's clinical efficacy in PubMed. These are not minor methodological caveats — they define the epistemic ceiling.

## 1. Acute Ischemic Stroke — The Primary Registration Indication

Semax is registered in Russia for acute ischemic stroke, and most of the human clinical evidence is concentrated here.

**PMID 11517472 (Gusev et al., 1997)** — The earliest published clinical comparison enrolled 30 patients with acute hemispheric ischemic stroke who received Semax in addition to conventional therapy, compared against 80 concurrent controls on conventional therapy alone [1, open_label]. Group allocation was not described as randomized; the 30:80 ratio suggests non-randomized assignment (likely clinician or institution of care). Rating scales were used but not named in the abstract. EEG mapping and somatosensory evoked potentials were co-monitored. Semax "had some influence on the rate of restoration of the damaged neurological functions," particularly motor disorders; effective doses were 12 mg/day for 5 days (moderate severity) and 18 mg/day for 10 days (severe). The outcome language ("some influence") reflects modest effect magnitude reported in summary form only.

**PMID 10358912 (Miasoedova et al., 1999)** — A mechanistic clinical-electrophysiological study in acute stroke patients investigated Semax's immunobiochemical effects [2, open_label]. Immunoassays showed Semax shifted the neuroinflammatory balance toward anti-inflammatory mediators (interleukin-10, TNF-α) versus pro-inflammatory interleukin-8 and C-reactive protein. This paper frames the neuroprotective mechanism rather than testing clinical outcomes directly. Authors are affiliated with the Skvortsova stroke group (First Moscow State Medical University) and Myasoedov (IMG-RAS).

**PMID 15792140 (Gusev, Skvortsova, Chukanova, 2005)** — The largest single open-label clinical study identified: n=187 patients with cerebrovascular insufficiency (earlier-stage disease, not acute stroke), assessing Semax tolerability and disease stabilisation [3, open_label]. The study reports "significant clinical improvement, stabilization of the disease progress, and reduced risk of stroke and TIA," but provides no quantitative outcome data, no named rating scales, no control group, and no randomization detail. Tolerability was rated good ("minor percent of side-effects"). Without a comparator arm or quantified endpoints, this study contributes qualitative signal only.

**PMID 29798983 (Gusev et al., 2018)** — The most recent and methodologically informative stroke study: n=110 post-stroke patients in an observational four-group design (early vs. late rehabilitation × Semax vs. no Semax) [4, open_label]. The primary novelty is the first human plasma BDNF measurement in a Semax-treated cohort: "administration of semax increased BDNF plasma levels, which remained high during the whole study period," regardless of rehabilitation timing. Motor performance (British Medical Research Council scale) and functional recovery (Barthel Index) were co-measured; combined early rehabilitation + Semax produced the best Barthel index trajectories. This study is not randomized — patients were not blindly or randomly assigned to the four arms — and the Myasoedov group (IMG-RAS) is among the co-authors.

**Evidence summary for stroke:** Four human studies, all open-label, all Russian, all involving the same two interlocking research groups, n range 30–187. No blinded controlled trial. Effect claims are directionally consistent (faster neurological recovery, BDNF elevation, better functional outcomes) but cannot be disentangled from standard-care effects, placebo, and reporting bias in the absence of blinding or independent replication. The regulatory approval in Russia predated modern RCT standards.

## 2. Optic and Ischemic Optic Neuropathy

Semax received clinical attention in Russian ophthalmology from approximately 1999–2004, with intranasal drops and endonasal electrophoresis as the delivery routes.

**PMID 10741256 (Polunin et al., 2000)** — Three-group comparison (intranasal drops vs. endonasal electrophoresis vs. control) across mixed optic nerve pathologies (vascular, toxic-allergic, inflammatory, partial atrophy) [5, open_label]. Sample sizes per group not reported in the abstract. Visual acuity, visual field extent, optic nerve electrical sensitivity and conductivity, and color vision all improved in Semax-treated groups relative to control, with the electrophoresis route showing slightly broader effects. "Particularly during acute disease stages" is where benefits were most apparent. The control condition is not described as placebo-controlled.

**PMID 11569188 (Kurysheva et al., 2001)** — Patients with glaucomatous optic neuropathy and normalised intraocular pressure received Semax as part of a neuroprotective complex [6, open_label]. Electrophysiological and computer-based examination demonstrated "advantages of new therapy over traditional neuroprotective treatment." The study cannot isolate Semax's contribution from co-administered neuroprotective agents, and n is not specified in the abstract.

**PMID 15678666 (Sheremet et al., 2004)** — Predominantly a pharmacokinetic/experimental substantiation study in rats using tritium-marked Semax, confirming that intranasally applied Semax "actively penetrates into the brain and eyes" and retains stability in the electrophoretic fields used for endonasal delivery [7, animal]. Cited here because it is the pharmacological rationale underpinning the clinical optic neuropathy applications.

**PMID 36083821 (Dragon et al., 2022)** — A 60-patient (120-eye) study of complex physiotherapeutic neurostimulation for vascular optic neuropathy, in which endonasal electrophoresis with 0.1% Semax was one of four components alongside transcranial magnetic stimulation, hyperbaric oxygen, and acupuncture [8, open_label]. The multi-modal bundle produced 94% efficacy at 1 week vs. 30–42% in vitamin-therapy controls. Semax cannot be isolated as causal — this paper evidences the bundled intervention, not Semax alone.

**Evidence summary for optic neuropathy:** Consistent directional signal across three clinical studies (visual function improvement vs. standard care), but all are small, open-label, Russian, without named comparator agents or blinding, and the most recent study makes Semax unextractable from a multi-modal bundle. No independent replication outside Russia.

## 3. ADHD and Paediatric Attention Impairment

There are **no published human clinical trials** of Semax in ADHD or paediatric attentional disorders. The ADHD claim traces to a single hypothesis paper: **PMID 16996699 (Tsai SJ, 2007, *Medical Hypotheses*)** [9, mechanism_review]. Tsai proposes that Semax may benefit ADHD and Rett syndrome based on (a) animal data showing Semax can augment psychostimulant-induced dopamine release and (b) BDNF stimulation in rodent models. *Medical Hypotheses* is a conjecture-only journal with no peer-review gate for empirical validity — this paper is a published hypothesis/opinion piece, not a trial or systematic analysis, and must not ground an efficacy claim. No trial has been registered or published. **PMID 14552554 (Maslova et al., 2003)** reported that ante- and postnatal hypoxia in rats caused CNS alterations correctable with peptide hormones including Semax, but this is a rodent developmental model with no translation to ADHD [10, animal]. The ADHD indication is extrapolation without human data.

## 4. Neurasthenia and Cognitive Impairment

There are **no PubMed-indexed human clinical trials** specifically testing Semax against neurasthenia or age-related cognitive impairment in a controlled design (search "Semax neurasthenia OR cognitive impairment human": count = 0). Semax is used clinically in Russia for these presentations — Russian drug labelling includes cognitive-impairment and neurasthenic indications — but the published evidence is absorbed into the broader cerebrovascular insufficiency cohort (PMID 15792140, n=187, open-label) rather than standing as a distinct cognitive-impairment trial.

**PMID 24738258 (Umnov et al., 2013)** — A Russian review article comparing polypeptide complexes (cortexin, cerebrolysin) and short peptides (Semax, kortagen, pinealon) as neuroprotectors in elderly patients, characterising their cellular mechanisms [11, mechanism_review]. Not a primary clinical trial; classified here to represent the geriatric neuroprotection literature context. Authors are from Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology RAS.

## 5. Human BDNF and Neurotrophin Data

Two categories of human data exist:

**Plasma BDNF in stroke rehabilitation (PMID 29798983):** The only published measurement of BDNF changes in humans receiving Semax. As described above (section 1), plasma BDNF increased in all Semax-treated post-stroke groups and remained elevated throughout follow-up. This is an observational finding without randomisation or blinding — confounders (rehabilitation intensity, diet, physical activity) are not controlled [4, open_label].

**Resting-state fMRI in healthy volunteers — two studies:**

*PMID 30225715 (Lebedeva et al., 2018)* — n=24 healthy volunteers (14 Semax, 10 placebo); intranasal 1% Semax; fMRI at baseline, 5 min, and 20 min post-administration [12, open_label]. The Semax group showed greater volume of the default mode network's rostral (medial frontal cortex) subcomponent vs. placebo. Authors: Research Center of Mental Health, Moscow (including Myasoedov NF). This study includes a placebo arm but randomization is not documented — it is placebo-referenced but not a randomized controlled trial — and n is small with no cognitive performance endpoints.

*PMID 32342318 (Panikratova et al., 2020)* — n=52 healthy participants; three-arm design (Semax, Selank, placebo); resting-state fMRI at baseline and post-administration [13, open_label]. Semax and Selank produced distinct and overlapping functional connectivity changes between the right amygdala and temporal-parahippocampal regions. Authors: Research Center of Mental Health (same Moscow group).

Both fMRI studies demonstrate acute brain network modulation by Semax in humans, but neither links imaging changes to cognitive performance outcomes in validated assessments — they establish biological signal, not efficacy on any clinical endpoint.

**Rodent BDNF mechanistic context (not human):** PMID 16996037 and PMID 16635254 (both Dolotov et al., 2006, Brain Research and J Neurochemistry) established in rats that intranasal Semax produces a 1.4-fold BDNF protein increase in hippocampus and a rapid BDNF rise in basal forebrain, with associated upregulation of BDNF exon III mRNA and TrkB mRNA [not separately cited — subsumed under the mechanistic literature]. These are preclinical foundations; the 2018 Gusev study is the only human extrapolation.

## 6. Motor Neuron Disease (ALS)

**PMID 18379501 (Serdiuk et al., 2007)** — Open-label study, n=27 ALS patients; intranasal Semax 12 mg/day in two 10-day courses [not assigned a new citation number — subsumed; see bibliography]. Semax did not alter the progression of chronic partial denervation on needle EMG or modify ALS functional rating scales. It did significantly improve quality-of-life scores (ALSAQ-40) via "improvement of emotional state and motivation," maximal at day 10. This is a negative efficacy result for the primary neurological endpoint and a positive signal on a subjective QoL endpoint in an unblinded study.

## 7. The Load-Bearing Tensions: Evidence Quality and Single-Lineage Problem

**Single lineage:** Virtually the entire human clinical literature on Semax connects to two overlapping Russian institutional networks — IMG-RAS (Myasoedov/Grivennikov group, responsible for synthesis and mechanistic biology) and the Moscow neurology cluster (Gusev/Skvortsova, responsible for most clinical applications). Myasoedov NF appears as co-author on clinical, mechanistic, fMRI, and pharmacological papers spanning 1997 to 2025. The inventor co-authorship pattern (Miasoedov / Myasoedov is the principal co-inventor of Semax alongside Ashmarin) means that the evidence base has never undergone external adversarial scrutiny in an independent trial.

**Design quality:** Every human trial identifiable in PubMed is open-label or has design features inconsistent with contemporary RCT standards (non-randomised group assignment, unnamed rating scales, no placebo arm, small n). The most rigorous human studies are the two fMRI connectome papers (n=14–52, placebo-referenced) — and these measure neural connectivity, not clinical outcomes.

**Missing study types:** Zero systematic reviews. Zero meta-analyses. Zero Western-jurisdiction trials. Zero registered trials on ClinicalTrials.gov under "Semax" as of the knowledge boundary. Zero independent non-Russian replication of any clinical claim.

**Western-paradigm non-acceptance:** The "regulatory peptide" pharmacological framework undergirding Semax (the Ashmarin school: endogenous regulatory peptides modulate CNS via receptor-nonselective pleiotropic effects) has not been accepted or tested by Western regulatory agencies. Semax is not approved by the FDA, EMA, or any Western regulator. It has no IND/CTA designations in the published literature.

**The analogue problem:** N-acetyl-semax-amidate is a structural modification (N-terminal acetylation + C-terminal amidation) sold in grey-market peptide markets with claims of enhanced potency and duration. There is **no PubMed record** of any human or controlled animal study of this specific modification ("N-acetyl semax amidate" OR "NAC semax": count = 0). Claims for it are wholly derived by analogy from parent Semax, itself a compound with limited blinded human data. The epistemic gap is compounded, not merely continued.

**What the evidence does support:** There is directionally consistent biological plausibility for neuroprotection and BDNF upregulation across the animal literature and one human plasma-BDNF observation. In stroke rehabilitation, the available (unblinded) human data trend toward faster neurological recovery when Semax is added to standard care. In optic neuropathy, visual function endpoints improved vs. comparators. These signals are real in the sense that they appear repeatedly — but they have not been tested with the controls needed to distinguish true effects from regression to the mean, non-specific attention, or publication bias in a single-lineage literature.
