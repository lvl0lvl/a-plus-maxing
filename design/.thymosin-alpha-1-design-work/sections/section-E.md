## Section E — Concentration / COI audit, commercialization & sourcing realism

### E.1 Research Concentration Audit

The Thymosin alpha-1 (Tα1) literature represents one of the broadest independent evidence bases among peptide therapeutics in this class — a genuine multi-decade, multi-country, multi-sponsor body of work.

**Primary research groups identified across the primary literature:**

1. **Goldstein lab / George Washington University (USA)** — Original discovery group. Allan L. Goldstein, Department of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology, GWU Medical Center, isolated Tα1 as the principal immunoactive fraction of thymosin fraction-5 (TF5) in 1977, building on work begun at the Albert Einstein College of Medicine in the early 1960s and later at the University of Texas Medical Branch [1, mechanism_review]. This group's 1975 New England Journal of Medicine paper (with Ammann and Wara) established TF5's immunorestorative properties. This is the founding academic group; no commercial sponsorship of the original discovery work is disclosed.

2. **Garaci / Tor Vergata Rome group (Italy)** — Enrico Garaci's Department of Experimental Medicine and Surgery at the University of Rome "Tor Vergata" (and later IRCCS San Raffaele, Rome) produced extensive preclinical and phase-II oncology work: melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), hepatocellular carcinoma. A 1996 Anticancer Research phase-II trial by Salvati, Rasi, Portalone, Antilli, and Garaci combined Tα1 with low-dose IFN-α after ifosfamide in NSCLC [2, open_label]. This Italian institutional group operated independently of SciClone's registrational program and funded through Italian research foundations and ERC grants [3, mechanism_review].

3. **Andreone / University of Bologna group (Italy)** — Conducted independent hepatitis B and C RCTs through the 1990s. An Andreone et al. randomized controlled trial compared Tα1 versus IFN-α in anti-HBe, HBV-DNA-positive chronic hepatitis B [4, rct]. A separate Andreone double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot trial evaluated Tα1 in chronic hepatitis C (published Liver, 1996) [5, rct]. These Italian academic trials ran independent of SciClone's registrational track.

4. **Chan / Chinese University of Hong Kong group (Hong Kong)** — H.L. Chan, J.L. Tang, W. Tam, J.J. Sung (Department of Medicine and Therapeutics, Faculty of Medicine, CUHK) conducted a 5-trial meta-analysis covering 353 patients (PMID 11736720), finding a significant delayed virological response at 12 months post-treatment (OR 2.67, 95% CI 1.25–5.68) [6, meta_analysis]. This independent academic group is distinct from SciClone's clinical program.

5. **Mutchnick / Wayne State University group (USA)** — Division of Gastroenterology, Wayne State University School of Medicine, Detroit, ran the Phase III US multicenter RCT (97 patients; double-blind, placebo-controlled; PMID 10607256), with a 14% vs 4% complete response rate favoring Tα1 [7, rct]. Funding was listed as "Non-U.S. Gov't Research Support"; specific funder not disclosed in the record. This group is the US academic anchor for HBV efficacy data.

6. **Lim / National University Hospital Singapore group (Singapore)** — Seng Gee Lim et al. (Department of Gastroenterology and Hepatology, National University Hospital) conducted a randomized, placebo-controlled trial of Tα1 plus lymphoblastoid IFN in 98 HBeAg-positive HBV patients (Antiviral Therapy, 2006, PMID 16640105) [8, rct]. Australian and Southeast Asian co-investigators (Scott Bowden, Steven Locarnini) contributed. An independent Asian academic group, not SciClone-funded per disclosed records.

7. **Wu / Sun Yat-sen University Critical Care group (China) — Sepsis program** — The ETASS (multicenter single-blind RCT, n=361, six Guangdong hospitals, 2008–2010; PMID 23327199) and the TESTS trial (Phase 3 double-blind RCT, n=1,106, 22 Chinese centers + University of Birmingham collaboration, 2016–2020; PMID 39814420) were led by Jianfeng Wu and Xiangdong Guan, First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University [9, rct]. ETASS was funded by the Guangdong Natural Science Foundation and Guangdong Medical Scientific Research Foundation — no commercial sponsor disclosed. **TESTS was co-funded by SciClone Pharmaceuticals**, with multiple senior authors receiving consultancy fees from SciClone; this is the primary instance of commercial sponsor concentration in the Tα1 sepsis literature [10, rct].

8. **COVID-19 cohort groups (China + France)** — Liu, Shen, Wen et al. (Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University; Zhejiang Hospital; Huadong Hospital/Fudan; multiple Hubei hospitals; with French collaborators at Hôpital Bicêtre AP-HP and Hôpital Raymond Poincaré) conducted a 5-hospital retrospective cohort study in 2,282 patients (Frontiers in Immunology, 2021; PMC8366398) [11, cohort]. Funded by China's National Natural Science Foundation; no COI declared. A separate 8-center retrospective cohort in critically ill COVID-19 patients also ran independently (PMC7409727).

**Concentration calculation:**

Counting distinct primary-research groups and their sponsor footprint across the efficacy literature:

- Confirmed independent academic groups (no disclosed SciClone funding): Goldstein/GWU; Garaci/Tor Vergata Rome; Andreone/Bologna; Chan/CUHK; Mutchnick/Wayne State; Lim/NUH Singapore; Wu group ETASS; multiple COVID-19 cohort groups (Liu/Ruijin, others) = **≥8 distinct independent academic groups**
- SciClone-co-sponsored evidence: TESTS trial (the largest sepsis RCT, n=1,106) — **1 co-sponsored major RCT**
- SciClone-sponsored registrational program: HBV and HCV approvals in China, Singapore, and other markets were based substantially on independent academic trials plus SciClone-organized registration packages; no single SciClone-sponsored study dominates the HBV evidence base.

**Estimated sponsor concentration:** SciClone's direct sponsorship touches ~1 of the ~12 distinct primary trial clusters identified in the literature. Even conservatively, no single sponsor or research group accounts for more than 15–20% of the independent primary evidence base. This is well below the 70% concentration flag. The literature is genuinely diverse across geography (USA, Italy, Hong Kong, Singapore, mainland China, France), decade (1975–2024), and indication (HBV, HCV, sepsis, cancer, COVID-19).

**Verdict: BELOW CONCENTRATION FLAG. Genuine breadth.** This contrasts sharply with single-group compounds (e.g., Khavinson peptides or the Cohen BPC-157 program). The Tα1 literature is one of the more independently replicated in the immunomodulatory peptide space.

### E.2 COI and Commercialization Landscape

**Allan Goldstein (academic founder):** Professor Emeritus, Department of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology, The George Washington University Medical Center. Goldstein's group conducted the original isolation and early clinical work in the 1960s–1970s within the academic NCI/NIH funding sphere. Goldstein co-founded Alpha 1 Biomedicals (the original commercialization vehicle) to develop thymosin peptides therapeutically — a standard academic-to-commercial translation path, not a deceptive one [1, mechanism_review].

**Alpha 1 Biomedicals:** The company originally patented and developed Tα1 for HBV. In 1990, Alpha 1 licensed North American and other territory rights to SciClone, and in 1994, this became an exclusive worldwide license. In December 1997, Alpha 1 sold its royalty rights to SciClone outright for $1.93M (cash + SciClone stock), completing the IP transfer [12, anecdote_aggregate].

**SciClone Pharmaceuticals:** A US-listed specialty pharma company (NASDAQ: SCLN) that in-licensed Tα1, branded it as Zadaxin (thymalfasin), and built its China commercial franchise on the drug. By 2004, Zadaxin revenues totaled ~$22.8M with 91% from China; by 2011, ~$100M with 97% of sales from China; by Q1 2017, ~$39.5M quarterly. The China market's high HBV burden (5–10% chronic infection prevalence in East/Southeast Asia) was the commercial foundation [12, anecdote_aggregate]. SciClone's FDA NDA for HBV was not completed despite Phase III trials meeting primary endpoints — a commercial rather than scientific withdrawal. SciClone held approvals in 35+ countries including China, India, Singapore, and throughout Southeast Asia, Latin America, and the Middle East.

**Acquisition:** In October 2017, a consortium led by GL Capital Management GP, Bank of China Group Investment (BOCGI), CDH Investments, Ascendent Capital Partners, and Boying Investments acquired SciClone for $11.18/share (reported at ~$605M total; primary SEC filing not fetched — figure sourced from corporate wire release) [12, anecdote_aggregate]. The acquisition was China-oriented capital taking the company private; Zadaxin remained the flagship product. SciClone continues to operate as a private company with the China commercialization business intact.

**COI in specific trials:** The TESTS Phase 3 sepsis trial (PMID 39814420) disclosed that multiple senior authors received grants or consultancy fees from SciClone Pharmaceuticals. The ETASS earlier sepsis RCT (PMID 23327199) declared no competing interests and listed academic Chinese government funding only [9, rct]. The HBV meta-analysis by Chan et al. [6, meta_analysis] and the hepatitis trials by Andreone, Lim, and Mutchnick groups disclose no SciClone COI.

**Generics landscape:** Following patent expiration, thymalfasin generics entered the Chinese market. Multiple Chinese manufacturers now produce generic thymalfasin injections. The Chinese generic market is a legitimate pharmaceutical tier (approved, manufactured under Chinese GMP standards), distinct from the grey-market research chemical category below.

### E.3 Sourcing Realism: Dual Reality

**Tier A — Legitimate approved pharmaceutical (Zadaxin/thymalfasin):**
Zadaxin is a 1.6 mg lyophilized powder in single-use vials, administered by subcutaneous injection. It is a prescription product regulated under the pharmaceutical approval frameworks of 35+ countries. In approved markets (China, India, Singapore, Philippines, most of Southeast Asia, parts of Latin America and Middle East), obtaining Zadaxin is a standard Rx process. In these markets, purity, potency, and sterility are regulated. Chinese generic thymalfasin follows the same regulatory track. The compound's identity, sequence, and biological activity are precisely characterized (synthetic 28-mer, identical to the endogenous peptide).

**Tier B — Grey-market "thymosin alpha-1" research chemicals (US and non-approved markets):**
In the United States, Tα1 is not FDA-approved and, as of FDA communications (including a warning letter and the 2024 PCAC proceedings), the FDA has stated it cannot be legally compounded under 503A or 503B frameworks. As of April 2026, removal from Category 2 (which formerly allowed compounding) took effect, with formal PCAC review scheduled for July 2026. A telehealth compounding pathway may exist in some formulations in practice, but legal access is contested. This creates conditions for grey-market procurement [13, regulatory].

Grey-market "research-grade" Tα1 sold by peptide vendors commonly presents two documented problems: (a) **purity shortfalls** — independent analyses of grey-market peptides consistently find actual purity 6–15% below claimed levels, with truncated sequences common due to synthesis errors in cost-optimized manufacturing; (b) **identity confusion** — some vendors mislabel products as thymosin alpha-1 that are in fact Thymosin Beta-4 (TB-500/TB4Frag), a cheaper-to-synthesize, completely unrelated peptide. These are structurally and functionally distinct: Tα1 is a 28-amino-acid immune modulator with TLR2/TLR9 agonism; TB-500 is a fragment of the 43-amino-acid thymosin beta-4, which regulates actin polymerization and promotes tissue repair. The "thymosin" label shared across both compounds is a source of consumer confusion that some vendors may exploit [13, regulatory].

**The Tα1-vs-Tβ4 (TB-500) distinction is critical:** These are not related forms of the same compound. They have different sequences, different mechanisms, different evidence bases, and different clinical programs. Research on one does not transfer to the other.
