# Section D: Determinants & Clinical Significance

## Hypogonadism Classification: Localizing the Defect

When total testosterone is low, the first clinical task is to localize the defect using the gonadotropins LH and FSH. Two anatomically distinct failure modes produce the same low-T result but carry different diagnoses, prognoses, and treatment implications.

**Primary hypogonadism** (hypergonadotropic): the testes fail to produce testosterone despite adequate stimulation. The pituitary, sensing low androgen feedback, increases LH and FSH output — so primary hypogonadism presents with low T and HIGH LH/FSH. Common causes include Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), orchitis (viral or autoimmune), chemotherapy or radiation injury, surgical orchiectomy, and anorchia. The elevated gonadotropins are the diagnostic fingerprint [1, mechanism_review].

**Secondary (central/hypogonadotropic) hypogonadism**: the fault lies upstream — the hypothalamus fails to produce adequate GnRH, or the pituitary fails to release sufficient LH/FSH. Because the signal never reaches the testes, T is low AND LH/FSH are low or inappropriately normal (they should be elevated if T is truly low). Causes include pituitary adenoma (especially prolactinoma), hyperprolactinemia from any cause, Kallmann syndrome, opioid use, glucocorticoid excess, and severe systemic illness [1, mechanism_review].

**Functional/age-related hypogonadism**: a third category in which neither organ is irreversibly damaged. Obesity, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes (T2D), obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and intercurrent illness suppress the HPG axis through multiple overlapping mechanisms — elevated estradiol from peripheral aromatization in adipose tissue, hyperleptinemia, inflammatory cytokines, and hyperinsulinemia all reduce GnRH pulsatility. The result is low T with low-to-normal LH/FSH, phenotypically indistinguishable from central hypogonadism. Weight loss can normalize testosterone without any exogenous hormone [1, mechanism_review].

## Symptoms and the Diagnostic Threshold Controversy

The hallmark symptoms of testosterone deficiency — reduced libido, erectile dysfunction (particularly loss of morning erections), fatigue, decreased muscle mass, and reduced bone mineral density — are nonspecific and highly prevalent in aging men independent of testosterone status [2, cohort].

The **300 ng/dL (10.4 nmol/L) cutoff** popularized by the American Urological Association as a diagnostic threshold has been contested. The Endocrine Society's 2018 Clinical Practice Guideline (Bhasin et al.) sets the lower limit of normal at **264 ng/dL (9.2 nmol/L)**, derived from a harmonized reference range in healthy, nonobese young men calibrated to the CDC LC-MS/MS standard [3, regulatory]. The guideline requires both criteria: symptoms AND "unequivocally and consistently low" testosterone, confirmed on two separate fasting morning samples. A single low value is insufficient.

The European Male Aging Study (EMAS) provided the strongest population-level evidence on the symptom-threshold relationship. In 3,369 men aged 40–79, Wu et al. (2010) found that only three sexual symptoms — poor morning erection, erectile dysfunction, and low sexual desire — showed a specific, graded association with testosterone concentrations. Late-onset hypogonadism was best identified by the combination of at least three of these sexual symptoms PLUS total T < 11 nmol/L (< 317 ng/dL) AND free T < 220 pmol/L (< 64 pg/mL) [2, cohort]. This work shifted the field toward requiring both symptom and biochemical criteria rather than a single absolute number.

## Determinants That Lower Total Testosterone

**Aging** is the most prevalent driver of declining testosterone at the population level. Feldman et al. (2002) in the Massachusetts Male Aging Study (MMAS) quantified, from longitudinal data in 1,156 men followed 7–10 years, a within-subject decline of **1.6%/year for total T and 2–3%/year for bioavailable T** — rates steeper than cross-sectional estimates because men who developed comorbidities contributed disproportionately to the longitudinal slope [4, cohort]. A more recent analysis of 625 men in the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (Carto et al., 2023) further dissected this: after adjusting for comorbidities (diabetes, heart failure, obesity, anemia), age itself did not independently predict testosterone decline — the bulk of the apparent age effect was mediated by accumulating disease burden [5, cohort].

**Obesity and metabolic syndrome / T2D** suppress testosterone through two parallel pathways: (a) SHBG is lowered by hyperinsulinemia and fatty liver, reducing measured total T even when free T is relatively preserved; (b) adipose-derived estradiol, leptin, and inflammatory cytokines directly suppress GnRH pulsatility, producing a secondary (central) pattern with low LH/FSH [1, mechanism_review]. This two-punch mechanism — reduced SHBG AND suppressed central drive — makes obesity one of the most potent non-pharmacological suppressors of total testosterone.

**Opioids** are a major and underdiagnosed pharmacological cause of central hypogonadism. Kafel et al. (2025) reviewed the prevalence of opioid-induced androgen deficiency (OPIAD) in men: **20–80% of men on chronic opioids develop biochemically confirmed hypogonadism**, with the range reflecting dose, formulation, and duration. The mechanism is primarily central — opioids inhibit hypothalamic GnRH synthesis and secretion via ε-receptors, yielding the low-T + low/inappropriately-normal-LH/FSH signature of secondary hypogonadism [6, mechanism_review].

**Glucocorticoids** suppress testosterone through dual central and gonadal mechanisms. MacAdams et al. (1986) documented in a controlled study that testosterone fell in 14 of 16 men on chronic glucocorticoids, to a mean of **211 ± 93 ng/dL vs. 449 ± 111 ng/dL in matched controls** (p < 0.001), with a significant inverse dose-response (r = –0.78) [7, cohort]. Both hypothalamic GnRH suppression and direct Leydig cell inhibition of steroidogenic enzymes contribute.

**Sleep deprivation and OSA** associate with lower testosterone. Kim and Jeon (2019) reviewed OSA cohort data finding consistent associations between OSA in middle-aged men and reduced testosterone secretion, compounded by obesity and aging [8, mechanism_review]. The directionality is partly bidirectional: low T may worsen sleep architecture, while sleep fragmentation disrupts the nocturnal pulsatile T surge that accounts for the physiologic morning peak.

**Anabolic-androgenic steroid (AAS)-induced hypogonadism (ASIH)**: exogenous androgens suppress the HPG axis via negative feedback, reducing GnRH, LH, and FSH, leading to testicular atrophy and near-complete cessation of endogenous testosterone synthesis. Rahnema et al. (2014) reviewed the clinical management of ASIH and noted that biochemical recovery of testosterone typically occurs over months to 1–2 years after cessation — but some individuals, particularly those with long or high-dose exposure, may not fully recover [9, mechanism_review]. This represents a potentially persistent secondary hypogonadism pattern.

Additional suppressors include: **heavy alcohol** (both acute — suppresses LH pulse amplitude — and chronic — direct Leydig cell toxicity); **marijuana** (cannabinoids inhibit GnRH/LH pulsatility); **hyperprolactinemia** (prolactin directly suppresses GnRH, a central mechanism); and **chronic systemic illness** (inflammatory cytokines and the associated catabolic state reduce gonadotropin drive).

## Determinants That Raise Total Testosterone

In men: **testosterone replacement therapy (TRT)** and other exogenous androgens are the most potent pharmacological elevators. **hCG** (human chorionic gonadotropin) mimics LH and directly stimulates Leydig cell testosterone production — relevant both in hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and in fertility-preserving alternatives to TRT. **Clomiphene citrate and other SERMs** block hypothalamic/pituitary estrogen receptors, reducing negative feedback and increasing endogenous LH/FSH and thereby T — often used in secondary hypogonadism when fertility preservation matters. **Weight loss in obese men** raises testosterone through the reverse of the obesity-suppression pathway: SHBG rises and central HPG suppression remits.

In **women**, elevated total testosterone signals androgen excess. The most common cause is **polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)**, which involves both ovarian and adrenal androgen excess. Other causes include androgen-secreting ovarian or adrenal tumors (often producing markedly elevated levels with rapid virilization), **congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH)** (particularly 21-hydroxylase deficiency, causing diversion of steroid precursors to androgens), and exogenous androgen administration.

## Associations and the CV-Safety Question

Low testosterone associates with metabolic syndrome, T2D, and all-cause mortality in multiple observational cohorts. The critical interpretive caveat is that much of this signal reflects **reverse causation and confounding by illness/obesity rather than T deficiency as an independent cause** [1, mechanism_review]. Chronic disease and adiposity independently suppress testosterone — so men who are sicker have lower T, but this does not establish that low T caused the adverse outcomes. Prospective observational data cannot resolve this, which is precisely why the TRAVERSE trial was designed.

The **TRAVERSE trial** (Lincoff et al., 2023, NEJM) was a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, noninferiority trial enrolling **5,246 men aged 45–80** with symptomatic hypogonadism (testosterone < 300 ng/dL on two fasting samples) and pre-existing cardiovascular disease or multiple CV risk factors. Men were randomized to transdermal testosterone (1.62% gel, titrated to 350–750 ng/dL) or placebo [10, rct]. **Primary result: MACE (cardiovascular death, nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke) occurred in 7.0% of the testosterone group vs. 7.3% of placebo (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.78–1.17), meeting the noninferiority criterion (p < 0.001)**. TRT did not increase major adverse cardiac events in this high-risk population.

**However, TRAVERSE revealed secondary safety signals**: the testosterone group had higher incidences of atrial fibrillation, pulmonary embolism, and acute kidney injury compared with placebo. These findings do not negate the MACE noninferiority result but underscore that TRT is not categorically safe in all domains and that arrhythmia and thromboembolic risk require consideration in individual patients [10, rct].

## SHBG and the Obligation to Interpret Contextually

Because ~44% of circulating testosterone is tightly bound to SHBG (and ~54% loosely bound to albumin), and because only the unbound free fraction (~2%) plus the loosely albumin-bound fraction (bioavailable fraction) is thought to be biologically active, **total testosterone is an unreliable surrogate when SHBG is abnormal** [3, regulatory].

Conditions that **raise SHBG** (thus elevating total T while free T may be normal or low): aging, hyperthyroidism, liver disease, HIV, certain anticonvulsants, and anorexia. Conditions that **lower SHBG** (depressing total T while free T may be relatively preserved): obesity, hypothyroidism, hyperinsulinemia, nephrotic syndrome, and exogenous androgens. The Endocrine Society (Bhasin et al., 2018) recommends measuring free testosterone — either by equilibrium dialysis (gold standard) or by calculation from total T + SHBG + albumin — whenever SHBG abnormality is suspected [3, regulatory].

## Measurement Limitations

Several analytical and biological factors limit the reliability of a single total testosterone result:

- **Diurnal variation**: total T peaks in the early morning (07:00–09:00 h) and may be 20–35% lower by afternoon; the Endocrine Society mandates two fasting morning samples before diagnosing hypogonadism [3, regulatory].
- **Biological variability**: intra-individual day-to-day CV is approximately 10–15%, meaning borderline values fluctuate substantially.
- **Assay dependence**: immunoassay-based platforms in widespread clinical use show poor accuracy at low concentrations compared with LC-MS/MS (liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry), which is the gold standard; harmonization across labs is incomplete despite CDC efforts.
- **Acute illness, fasting, and exercise** all acutely suppress testosterone and can produce artifactually low readings.
- **SHBG confounding** (see above): a single total T number is uninterpretable without knowing SHBG status.
