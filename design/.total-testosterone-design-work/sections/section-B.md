# Section B: Reference Ranges, Units & Thresholds

## The Harmonized Reference Range

For decades, clinical laboratories reported total testosterone using their own internally derived reference intervals, calibrated against different standards with different immunoassay platforms. The result was substantial interlaboratory disagreement: one 2007 demonstration showed 1,133 laboratories measuring the same hypogonadal sample produced values ranging from 45 to 365 ng/dL — a nearly eightfold spread [5, regulatory]. This fragmentation made cross-study comparisons unreliable and left clinicians without a defensible normal range.

The 2017 Travison harmonization study resolved this [1, cohort]. Using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) at the CDC's reference laboratory as the calibration anchor, researchers re-measured archived samples from four major population cohorts — the Framingham Heart Study (US), the European Male Aging Study (8 European centers), the Osteoporotic Fractures in Men Study (US), and the Sibling Study of Osteoporosis (Belgium) — totaling 9,054 community-dwelling men. Passing-Bablok regression translated each cohort's local assay values to the CDC standard, then pooled percentile curves were derived via constrained quantile regression.

**The harmonized reference range for healthy, non-obese men aged 19–39 years is 264–916 ng/dL (9.2–31.8 nmol/L).** This represents the 2.5th–97.5th percentile interval. The 5th percentile (often used as a more conservative lower cutoff) is 303 ng/dL (10.5 nmol/L); the median is 531 ng/dL (18.4 nmol/L) [1, cohort]. These figures replace the lab-to-lab idiosyncratic ranges that preceded them and are now the reference standard endorsed by the Endocrine Society.

## Units and Conversion

Total testosterone is reported in two unit systems. In the United States, ng/dL (nanograms per deciliter) predominates clinically. International literature uses nmol/L (nanomoles per liter). The conversion factor is:

> **1 ng/dL = 0.0347 nmol/L** (or equivalently, 1 nmol/L = 28.8 ng/dL)

| ng/dL | nmol/L |
|-------|--------|
| 264 | 9.2 |
| 300 | 10.4 |
| 531 | 18.4 (median) |
| 600 | 20.8 |
| 916 | 31.8 |

When reading a lab report, confirm which unit the reference range is expressed in — an unchecked unit swap is a common source of misinterpretation.

## The Hypogonadism Threshold: 300 ng/dL Is a Landmark, Not a Bright Line

A value of approximately 300 ng/dL (10.4 nmol/L) circulates widely in clinical practice as the lower diagnostic threshold for male hypogonadism. This is a reasonable clinical landmark, sitting close to the harmonized 2.5th percentile, but the 2018 Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline makes the diagnostic framework explicit [2, regulatory]:

**Diagnosis requires two conditions simultaneously:**

1. **Consistently low testosterone confirmed on ≥2 separate early-morning (AM) samples.** The guideline specifies fasting, morning specimens because of testosterone's pronounced diurnal rhythm: levels peak between 06:00–08:00 and fall approximately 30–35% by afternoon in younger men [4, cohort]. A single sub-threshold afternoon draw does not establish hypogonadism.

2. **Clinical symptoms and signs consistent with testosterone deficiency.** These include reduced libido, erectile dysfunction, decreased energy, depressed mood, reduced muscle mass, increased body fat, and reduced bone density. An isolated low laboratory value in an asymptomatic man does not, by itself, warrant a diagnosis or treatment.

The Endocrine Society guideline specifically notes that roughly 30% of men who show a hypogonadal-range value on a first measurement will be within the normal range on a repeat sample. This biological variability — driven by pulsatile luteinizing hormone secretion, stress, illness, sleep disruption, and assay imprecision — is why serial morning sampling is mandatory, not optional. Testosterone exists on a biological continuum; there is no sharp metabolic threshold at which tissue responsiveness abruptly switches. The 300 ng/dL convention encodes probability of deficiency, not biochemical certainty.

## Female Reference Range

Total testosterone in women is substantially lower than in men, reflecting ovarian and adrenal contributions that are far smaller than testicular output. Reference ranges in healthy premenopausal adult women are approximately **15–70 ng/dL (0.5–2.4 nmol/L)** by LC-MS/MS-based assays, though published ranges vary by age group, menstrual cycle phase, and measurement method [3, regulatory].

The International Society for the Study of Women's Sexual Health (ISSWSH) notes that establishing a single universal female reference range is not currently possible, given that conventional immunoassays lack the sensitivity and specificity to reliably measure testosterone at female physiological concentrations (generally below 100 ng/dL) [3, regulatory]. LC-MS/MS is the preferred analytical method for female samples. Premenopausal concentrations decline with age even before menopause; values in women in their 40s are roughly half those of women in their 20s [3, regulatory].

## Age-Related Decline and Age-Adjusted Ranges

Total testosterone declines with age in men. The Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging, which followed 702 men with up to 10 serial measurements each, quantified this as approximately 1.6% per year across all age groups from the 30s onward — roughly 16% per decade [6, cohort]. By age 70, a substantial proportion of men who had normal levels at 40 will have drifted below the young-adult reference range.

This raises a clinically contested question: should age-adjusted reference intervals be used, making a 70-year-old man's 250 ng/dL "normal for his age"? The Endocrine Society guideline declines to endorse age-specific lower limits, citing two concerns [2, regulatory]:

- The cross-sectional data underlying age-specific ranges conflate aging with accumulating comorbidities (obesity, sleep apnea, metabolic syndrome) that suppress testosterone independently of age.
- Age-adjusted ranges risk normalizing levels at which older men still experience symptoms and adverse outcomes.

The practical consensus is that the harmonized 264 ng/dL lower bound remains the reference for all adult men, with age as clinical context rather than a numerical correction applied to the threshold.

## Assay Variation and Why a Single Low Value Is Not Diagnostic

Immunoassays — the workhorses of high-throughput clinical laboratories — have well-documented limitations at testosterone concentrations below 200–300 ng/dL: cross-reactivity with other steroids, variable calibration, and manufacturer-specific differences in antibody specificity [5, regulatory]. Even when two laboratories run the same platform, their reported values on the same sample can differ by 20–30%. The Endocrine Society position statement on testosterone measurement catalogues these limitations and calls for LC-MS/MS as the gold standard whenever diagnostic precision is required [5, regulatory].

Taken together, three forces explain why a single low testosterone value cannot establish hypogonadism: (1) diurnal rhythm — a single specimen drawn outside the reference collection window will be spuriously low; (2) day-to-day biological variability of 10–15% within an individual; and (3) assay imprecision that adds measurement error on top of biological variance. Only a pattern of low values on properly timed, replicated draws, paired with symptoms, supports the diagnosis.
