# Section A: Physiology & What Total Testosterone Measures

## Testosterone Biosynthesis and the HPG Axis

Testosterone is the primary circulating androgen in men and is synthesized predominantly (approximately 95%) in the **Leydig cells** of the testicular interstitium. The signal cascade begins in the hypothalamus, where specialized neurons release **gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH)** in discrete pulses — typically every 90–120 minutes in adult men. GnRH travels via the hypothalamic-pituitary portal blood to the anterior pituitary, where it binds to gonadotroph receptors and triggers the pulsatile release of **luteinizing hormone (LH)** and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) [1, mechanism_review].

LH is the primary driver of Leydig cell steroidogenesis. Upon binding to LH receptors on Leydig cells, cyclic AMP (cAMP) is generated as a second messenger, rapidly upregulating the steroidogenic acute regulatory (StAR) protein. StAR facilitates translocation of cholesterol from the outer to the inner mitochondrial membrane — the rate-limiting step in steroid hormone synthesis [2, mechanism_review]. Cholesterol is then converted to pregnenolone by the CYP11A1 (P450scc) enzyme at the inner mitochondrial membrane. Subsequent enzymatic steps involving HSD3B2, CYP17A1, and HSD17B convert pregnenolone through a series of intermediates to yield **testosterone**, which is secreted into the testicular venous blood and lymphatics [2, mechanism_review].

The HPG axis operates under a classic **long-loop negative feedback** mechanism. Rising serum testosterone suppresses both hypothalamic GnRH pulse frequency and pituitary sensitivity to GnRH, dampening further LH release. Estradiol — produced by peripheral aromatization of testosterone — provides additional and potent negative feedback, acting at both the hypothalamus and the anterior pituitary to reduce gonadotropin secretion [1, 3, mechanism_review]. FSH acts primarily on Sertoli cells to support spermatogenesis, while inhibin B, secreted by Sertoli cells, selectively suppresses FSH in a short feedback loop. This architecture creates a homeostatic set-point that keeps serum testosterone within the adult male reference range of approximately **300–1,000 ng/dL (10.4–34.7 nmol/L)** under normal conditions.

## What "Total Testosterone" Measures: The Three Circulating Pools

A serum "total testosterone" assay measures the **sum of all three molecular pools** in which testosterone circulates, and understanding these pools is essential for interpreting any clinical result [4, mechanism_review]:

1. **SHBG-bound (~44%, tightly bound, NOT bioavailable):** The majority of circulating testosterone is bound with high affinity to sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), a hepatically synthesized glycoprotein. This fraction is biologically inert for most practical purposes — SHBG-bound testosterone cannot diffuse freely into target cells, and its tight association constant means it remains essentially sequestered during capillary transit [4, mechanism_review].

2. **Albumin-bound (~50%, loosely bound, bioavailable):** Roughly half of circulating testosterone associates weakly with albumin. Due to albumin's low binding affinity and the relatively short capillary transit time, this fraction dissociates readily and is accessible to tissues. Albumin-bound testosterone is therefore considered **bioavailable** [4, mechanism_review].

3. **Free (~2%, immediately active):** A small but physiologically critical fraction circulates unbound. Free testosterone can diffuse directly across cell membranes to bind intracellular androgen receptors and drive gene transcription. It represents the most immediately bioactive form [4, mechanism_review].

The **free-hormone hypothesis** — formalized through decades of endocrinology research — holds that free plus albumin-bound testosterone constitutes the "bioavailable" fraction (approximately 52% of total). This bioavailable pool correlates more closely with androgen-sensitive clinical endpoints (bone mineral density, muscle mass, libido) than total testosterone alone in many studies, though total testosterone remains the recommended first-line diagnostic test due to assay standardization and cost [4, 5, mechanism_review]. Clinicians should note that SHBG levels are influenced by many factors (obesity, insulin, thyroid disease, aging, medications), which can cause total testosterone to diverge substantially from the bioavailable fraction — particularly in older men and in women.

## Diurnal Rhythm and the Need for AM Sampling

Testosterone secretion follows a pronounced **circadian rhythm** driven by pulsatile LH release. In healthy young to middle-aged men, serum total testosterone peaks between **7:00 and 7:30 AM**, with values declining by at least 43% from peak to nadir by early evening [5, mechanism_review]. Free and bioavailable testosterone follow the same morning acrophase pattern. SHBG, by contrast, reaches its daily high in the mid-afternoon — partially buffering the circulating total, though the decline in free and bioavailable fractions through the day is still clinically meaningful.

This rhythm is the reason clinical guidelines (including the Endocrine Society) recommend drawing testosterone between **8:00 and 10:00 AM** for diagnostic evaluation. Samples drawn in the afternoon may yield values 20–35% lower than morning levels in the same individual, leading to false-positive diagnoses of hypogonadism. The diurnal amplitude is preserved in fit, healthy men into the seventh decade, though it may be partially attenuated in older or metabolically unwell individuals [5, mechanism_review].

## Peripheral Conversions: DHT and Estradiol

Testosterone is not merely a terminal effector — it serves as a **prohormone** for two distinct active metabolites produced in peripheral tissues:

- **Dihydrotestosterone (DHT):** The enzyme **5α-reductase** (types 1, 2, and 3) converts testosterone to DHT in androgen-sensitive tissues including the prostate, seminal vesicles, skin, hair follicles, and skeletal muscle. DHT binds the androgen receptor with approximately three times the affinity of testosterone itself, making it the dominant intratissue androgen in these organs [6, mechanism_review]. DHT cannot be aromatized to estradiol, so it remains a pure androgen signal. In men, DHT mediates virilization of the external genitalia, prostate growth, and scalp hair loss; 5α-reductase inhibitors (finasteride, dutasteride) reduce circulating DHT substantially while leaving total testosterone relatively intact.

- **Estradiol (E2):** The enzyme **aromatase** (CYP19A1), expressed in adipose tissue, liver, bone, brain, and gonads, converts testosterone to estradiol. In men, estradiol plays essential roles in regulating libido, bone mineral density, and hypothalamic feedback; loss of estradiol signaling (in men with aromatase mutations or CYP19A1 deficiency) produces osteoporosis and metabolic derangements despite normal testosterone levels. Rising adiposity increases aromatase activity, shifting the testosterone-to-estradiol ratio and contributing to secondary hypogonadism in obese men [3, mechanism_review].

## Testosterone in Women

Women produce testosterone at levels approximately 10- to 20-fold lower than men. Sources are roughly distributed as: **one quarter from the ovaries**, **one quarter from the adrenal glands**, and **approximately one half from peripheral conversion** of precursors (androstenedione, DHEA, DHEA-sulfate) in adipose and other tissues [7, mechanism_review]. Median serum total testosterone in reproductive-age women is approximately **0.34 nmol/L (approximately 10 ng/dL)**, with a wide reference interval of 0.04–1.01 nmol/L [7, mechanism_review]. Levels fluctuate across the menstrual cycle, peaking modestly at midcycle. In women, testosterone contributes to libido, bone density, muscle maintenance, and mood, and its assay interpretation is complicated by the fact that most immunoassays lack sufficient sensitivity at female concentration ranges.

## Age-Related Decline in Men

After approximately age 30–40, serum total testosterone in men declines at a rate of roughly **1% per year** [8, mechanism_review]. The decline reflects a dual deficit: reduced Leydig cell capacity to synthesize testosterone in response to LH stimulation, and age-related attenuation of hypothalamic GnRH pulse amplitude and frequency [8, mechanism_review]. Bioavailable and free testosterone decline more steeply — at approximately 2–3% annually — because SHBG concentrations rise with age, sequestering a larger fraction of the declining total pool. By the eighth decade, approximately 30% of men have total testosterone values in the hypogonadal range, and up to 50% have low free testosterone values. This progressive androgen decline underpins the clinical syndrome of late-onset hypogonadism (LOH) but should be distinguished from pathological primary or secondary hypogonadism, which may present at any age.
