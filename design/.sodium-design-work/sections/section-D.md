# Section D: Determinants & Clinical Significance

Serum sodium ([Na⁺]) is a **concentration**, not a measure of total-body sodium — it reflects the ratio of sodium to total body water. Interpreting a result in isolation is therefore insufficient; volume status, serum osmolality, blood glucose, and the clinical context must all be integrated [1, regulatory]. Two directions of derangement dominate clinical practice: hyponatremia (<135 mmol/L) and hypernatremia (>145 mmol/L). Both carry independent prognostic weight that extends well beyond their immediate biochemical danger.

---

## Hyponatremia (<135 mmol/L): The Most Common Electrolyte Disorder

Hyponatremia affects approximately 14–30% of hospitalised adults and is the most frequent electrolyte abnormality encountered in clinical practice [6, cohort]. The European clinical practice guideline published by Spasovski et al. in 2014, developed jointly by three European medical societies (ESICM, ESE, ERA-EDTA), defines the threshold at <135 mmol/L and classifies severity as mild (130–134), moderate (125–129), and profound (<125) [1, regulatory]. Understanding the *mechanism* requires classifying by volume status, because treatment differs fundamentally across subtypes.

### Hypovolemic Hyponatremia

The patient has lost both sodium and water, but has replaced more of the water than the sodium — often through thirst and hypotonic fluid intake. Causes include:

- **Thiazide diuretics** (particularly thiazides, not loop diuretics, because thiazides blunt dilution in the distal tubule while loop diuretics spare this mechanism)
- Vomiting and diarrhea (sodium-rich GI fluid loss)
- Third-spacing (pancreatitis, burns, peritonitis)
- **Adrenal insufficiency** (aldosterone deficiency → renal salt wasting)
- Salt-wasting nephropathy (tubulointerstitial disease, post-obstructive diuresis)
- **Cerebral salt wasting** (natriuretic peptide–driven sodium loss from CNS injury — distinguished from SIADH by *volume depletion*, not euvolemia; treatment is volume and sodium replacement, not fluid restriction)

### Euvolemic Hyponatremia

Clinically, the patient appears euvolemic; there is water retention without overt oedema. The dominant mechanism is non-osmotic ADH (vasopressin) secretion driving excessive renal water retention.

**SIADH (Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion)** is the most common cause of euvolemic hyponatremia. Diagnostic criteria require hypotonic hyponatremia with an inappropriately concentrated urine (>100 mOsm/kg), elevated urine [Na⁺] (typically >40 mmol/L), clinical euvolemia, and exclusion of hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, and diuretic use. Major precipitants include [1, regulatory]:

- **Drugs**: SSRIs, carbamazepine, antipsychotics (particularly haloperidol), vincristine, cyclophosphamide, NSAIDs
- **CNS disease**: stroke, subarachnoid haemorrhage, traumatic brain injury, meningitis, encephalitis
- **Pulmonary disease**: pneumonia, TB, ARDS, positive-pressure ventilation
- **Malignancy**: small-cell lung cancer (the classic culprit — ectopic ADH production), lymphoma, pancreatic carcinoma
- **Post-operative/procedural**: pain and nausea are potent non-osmotic ADH stimuli; a major source of hospital-acquired hyponatremia
- **Primary polydipsia and beer potomania/low-solute intake**: excessive water ingestion overwhelms renal diluting capacity (primary polydipsia) or insufficient solute substrate limits the kidney's ability to excrete free water even without elevated ADH (beer potomania)

Other euvolemic causes include **hypothyroidism** (reduced GFR and ADH release) and **glucocorticoid deficiency** (cortisol deficiency stimulates ADH and impairs cardiac output, reducing free-water excretion).

### Hypervolemic Hyponatremia

Oedematous states in which effective arterial blood volume (EABV) is perceived as low by baroreceptors, triggering non-osmotic ADH release and avid renal sodium retention — yet total-body water and sodium are both expanded, with the dilutional effect on sodium predominating:

- **Heart failure**: reduced cardiac output → neurohormonal activation → ADH + aldosterone + sympathetic nervous system → free-water and sodium retention
- **Cirrhosis**: splanchnic vasodilation → reduced EABV → same neurohormonal cascade; hyponatremia is so prognostically relevant that serum sodium was incorporated into the MELD-Na score [3, cohort]
- **Nephrotic syndrome**: oncotic pressure loss → oedema with reduced effective intravascular volume
- **Advanced renal failure**: diminished capacity to excrete free water

### Exercise-Associated Hyponatremia (EAH)

EAH is a distinct and potentially fatal variant relevant to endurance athletes. The primary mechanism is *over-drinking hypotonic fluid* in excess of free-water losses, often compounded by non-osmotic ADH release (pain, nausea, exertion). Almond et al. (NEJM, 2005) prospectively studied 488 Boston Marathon runners at finish-line blood draw and found 13% had hyponatremia (≤135 mmol/L), with 0.6% reaching critical hyponatremia (≤120 mmol/L) [2, cohort]. On multivariate analysis, weight gain during the race (OR 4.2, 95% CI 2.2–8.2) and race time >4 hours (OR 7.4 vs. <3:30 hours, 95% CI 2.9–23.1) were the strongest independent predictors [2, cohort]. Severe EAH has caused deaths from cerebral herniation; the prevention strategy is drinking to thirst — not to a schedule — and avoiding hypotonic fluids.

## Hypernatremia (>145 mmol/L): A Free-Water Deficit

Hypernatremia almost always represents a deficit of water relative to sodium — pure free-water loss or insufficient intake, occasionally combined with hypertonic sodium gain. Because the thirst mechanism is a powerful defence, sustained hypernatremia usually requires *impaired access to water* or *impaired thirst perception*, so it disproportionately affects elderly patients, infants, the unconscious, and those with neurological disease.

Principal causes include:

- **Inadequate water intake**: impaired consciousness, physical disability, dementia, extreme age, iatrogenic fluid restriction
- **Central diabetes insipidus**: ADH deficiency from posterior pituitary or hypothalamic damage (neurosurgery, traumatic brain injury, tumour, infiltrative disease, ischaemia) — characterised by dilute urine with persistent hypernatremia; responds to desmopressin
- **Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus**: renal ADH resistance — most commonly acquired from **lithium** (downregulation of aquaporin-2 channels), **hypercalcaemia** (impairs ADH signalling), chronic hypokalaemia; also hereditary (AVPR2 or AQP2 mutations)
- **Osmotic diuresis**: hyperglycaemia (the osmotic load drives obligatory water loss exceeding sodium loss); mannitol therapy; urea; post-contrast nephropathy
- **Gastrointestinal and skin losses**: severe watery diarrhoea (rotavirus, cholera), profuse sweating, burns — particularly when free-water replacement is inadequate

Arzhan et al. (Kidney360, 2022) analysed 1.9 million hospitalised patients and found 3% had hypernatremia (Na >145 mEq/L) at admission, with in-hospital mortality of 12% versus 2% in normonatraemic patients; severe hypernatremia (Na >155 mEq/L) carried an adjusted in-hospital mortality OR of 34.41 (95% CI 30.59–38.71) compared with normonatraemia [8, cohort]. The magnitude of this association reflects extreme physiological derangement rather than a correctable single lesion.

## Prognostic Significance: Both Poles Predict Mortality

### Hyponatremia and Mortality

The prognostic weight of hyponatremia is well-established across multiple disease contexts, with the consistent caveat that it largely acts as a **severity or illness marker** — it indexes the neurohormonal activation and haemodynamic compromise of underlying disease — rather than as the direct cause of death in most chronic cases.

**Heart failure**: Gheorghiade et al. (Eur Heart J, 2007) analysed 48,612 patients from the OPTIMIZE-HF registry across 259 US hospitals [4, cohort]. For every 3 mmol/L decrement in admission sodium below 140 mmol/L, in-hospital mortality risk increased by 19.5% and follow-up mortality risk increased by 10%. A complementary meta-analysis of eight cohort studies (Wang et al., Heart Fail Rev, 2019; N = 5,714 acute decompensated HF patients) demonstrated that hyponatremia *improvement* during hospitalisation reduced all-cause mortality risk (RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.53–0.80, P<0.001), with short-term mortality reduction most marked (RR 0.54) [5, meta_analysis].

**Cirrhosis**: Kim et al. (NEJM, 2008) studied 6,769 adult candidates for liver transplantation registered with the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) in 2005–2006 [3, cohort]. Each 1 unit decrease in serum sodium between 125–140 mmol/L was associated with a hazard ratio of 1.05 for 90-day waitlist mortality (P<0.001). Incorporating serum sodium into MELD (to create MELDNa) was estimated to have prevented 7% of 90-day waitlist deaths in the 2006 validation cohort. This finding was influential enough to drive adoption of MELDNa into organ allocation policy.

**General hospitalised patients**: Waikar, Mount, and Curhan (Am J Med, 2009) conducted a prospective cohort study of 98,411 hospitalised adults at two Boston teaching hospitals (2000–2003) and found hyponatremia in 14.5% on initial measurement [6, cohort]. After multivariable adjustment, hyponatremia was associated with increased in-hospital mortality (OR 1.47, 95% CI 1.33–1.62), 1-year mortality (HR 1.38, 95% CI 1.32–1.46), and 5-year mortality (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.21–1.30). Even mild hyponatremia (130–134 mEq/L) carried an in-hospital mortality OR of 1.37 (95% CI 1.23–1.52). Critically, resolution of hyponatremia during hospitalisation attenuated this risk — consistent with the marker-vs.-cause framing [6, cohort].

**Community-acquired pneumonia**: Krüger et al. (Respir Med, 2014) in the multicentre prospective CAPNETZ cohort (N = 2,138) found a U-shaped association between serum sodium and 28-day mortality, with both hyponatremia (31.8% prevalence) and hypernatremia independently predicting 28-day mortality in multivariate Cox analysis [7, cohort].

### Hypernatremia and Mortality

Hypernatremia carries even higher crude mortality than hyponatremia, though again much of this reflects severity of background illness [8, cohort]. In the large Arzhan et al. population-based analysis, an admission sodium >145 mEq/L was associated with an unadjusted in-hospital mortality rate six times that of normonatraemic patients (12% vs. 2%) [8, cohort].

## Symptoms and the Correction-Rate Danger

### Acute Hyponatremia

The brain adapts to hypo-osmolality via solute extrusion over 48–72 hours (the adaptation window). When sodium falls *acutely* (within <48 hours), this adaptation has not yet occurred, and the brain takes on water — producing cerebral oedema with a characteristic clinical progression: headache → nausea → confusion → seizures → herniation. This is the setting where acute symptomatic hyponatremia is *directly dangerous* rather than merely a severity marker, and where urgent correction with hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) is warranted.

Even chronic "asymptomatic" hyponatremia is not truly benign. Renneboog et al. (Am J Med, 2006) documented in a case-control study of 122 hyponatraemic patients (mean Na 126 mEq/L, mean age 72 years) that 21.3% were admitted for falls versus 5.3% of controls (adjusted OR 67, P<.001), with objective gait instability (centre-of-pressure displacement 1,336 vs. 1,047 mm, P=.003) and attention deficits (58 ms increase in reaction time, P<.001) [9, cohort].

### Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome

Over-rapid correction of *chronic* hyponatremia is the signature iatrogenic danger. Once the brain has adapted (extruded osmolytes), a sudden osmotic gradient causes rapid water egress from brain cells — oligodendrocytes are particularly vulnerable — producing **osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)**, historically called central pontine myelinolysis. Sterns, Riggs, and Schochet (NEJM, 1986) identified the critical threshold in 60 patients with serum sodium below 116 mmol/L: all neurological sequelae occurred when sodium was raised by >12 mmol/L per day [10, cohort]. Current consensus guidelines cap correction more conservatively at ≤6–8 mmol/L per 24 hours (the 2014 European guideline uses 8 mmol/L/24h as the ceiling) [1, regulatory], reflecting subsequent evidence that even 12 mmol/L/day can be harmful in high-risk patients (chronic alcoholism, malnutrition, hypokalaemia, liver disease). When accidental overcorrection occurs, re-lowering serum sodium with hypotonic fluids ± DDAVP is the rescue strategy.

### Hypernatremia Correction

Over-rapid correction of hypernatremia carries the symmetric danger: the chronically dehydrated brain (having accumulated idiogenic osmoles to prevent shrinkage) is now at risk of cerebral oedema if sodium is corrected too fast. Correction should not exceed 10–12 mmol/L per 24 hours.

## Interpretive Limitations

Serum [Na⁺] is a concentration — it is the ratio of sodium to water in the sampled fluid, not a measure of total-body sodium stores. Two critical artefacts must be excluded:

1. **Pseudohyponatremia**: Extreme hyperlipidaemia or hyperproteinaemia (e.g., multiple myeloma, severe hypertriglyceridaemia) displaces plasma water when sodium is measured by indirect flame photometry — yielding a falsely low sodium despite normal plasma osmolality. Modern ion-selective electrode methods largely eliminate this artefact, but it remains clinically relevant when lipids are grossly elevated.

2. **Translocational hyponatremia**: Osmotically active solutes that cannot freely cross cell membranes — most importantly severe hyperglycaemia, but also mannitol — draw water from the intracellular compartment into plasma, diluting [Na⁺] without a true excess of free water. The corrected sodium rises approximately 1.6 mmol/L for every 5.6 mmol/L increment in glucose above the normal range. This is not hypotonic hyponatremia and requires glucose correction, not sodium supplementation.

The obligatory framework for interpretation therefore layers serum osmolality over the measured sodium, then integrates volume status (oedema, skin turgor, mucous membranes, orthostatic vitals, urine output, urine sodium and osmolality) to classify hypo-, eu-, or hypervolaemic aetiology before any treatment is initiated.
