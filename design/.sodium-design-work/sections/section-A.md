# Section A: Physiology & What Serum Sodium Measures

## The Principal Extracellular Cation

Sodium (Na⁺) is the principal cation of extracellular fluid (ECF). Serum sodium concentration in healthy adults is maintained within a narrow range of approximately 135–145 mmol/L (equivalent to mEq/L in clinical use) [1, mechanism_review]. Within cells, however, sodium concentration is only ~10–15 mmol/L — a steep inward electrochemical gradient that is actively sustained by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump [6, mechanism_review]. This enzyme, present in virtually every mammalian cell membrane, continuously hydrolyzes one intracellular ATP molecule to export three Na⁺ ions out of the cell while importing two K⁺ ions, consuming roughly 20–40% of resting cellular energy expenditure to maintain these gradients [6, mechanism_review]. The resulting 10:1 (or greater) ratio of extracellular to intracellular sodium is not incidental: it drives the secondary transport of nutrients, neurotransmitters, and amino acids; sets the resting membrane potential essential for nerve conduction and muscle contraction; and underpins the entire architecture of epithelial transport throughout the gut, kidney, and brain [6, mechanism_review].

## Sodium as the Dominant Determinant of Plasma Osmolality

Because Na⁺ — together with its balancing anions (principally chloride and bicarbonate) — constitutes ~90% of ECF solute, it is the principal determinant of plasma osmolality [8, mechanism_review]. Plasma osmolality can be estimated at the bedside with the Edelman-derived formula:

> **Plasma osmolality ≈ 2 × [Na⁺] + glucose (mg/dL)/18 + BUN (mg/dL)/2.8**   (conventional units)

where the coefficient of 2 accounts for the paired anions, and the glucose and BUN terms correct for other measurable osmoles [7, mechanism_review]. In healthy individuals, plasma osmolality is normally 280–295 mOsm/kg H₂O; sodium alone accounts for ~270 mOsm/kg of that total. Because sodium and its anions dominate the numerator, any clinically meaningful change in serum [Na⁺] directly shifts plasma tonicity — the effective osmolality that drives water movement across cell membranes [1, mechanism_review].

## The Load-Bearing Concept: Serum Sodium Reflects Water Balance, Not Sodium Intake

The most important conceptual point about serum [Na⁺] is frequently misunderstood: **serum sodium concentration is a measure of water balance, not of total-body sodium content or dietary salt intake** [1, mechanism_review]. Formally, serum [Na⁺] approximates the ratio of total-body exchangeable sodium (plus potassium) to total-body water:

> [Na⁺]serum ≈ Na⁺exchangeable + K⁺exchangeable / Total Body Water

This identity, established by Edelman and colleagues, means that the serum sodium can fall even when total-body sodium is elevated — the defining paradox of heart failure, hepatic cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome — because free water retention by the kidney outpaces any sodium accumulation, diluting the extracellular compartment [1, mechanism_review]. Conversely, hypernatremia most commonly reflects a net water deficit rather than sodium loading [2, mechanism_review]. A patient can therefore be simultaneously hypervolemic (expanded ECF volume, pitting edema, elevated total-body sodium) and hyponatremic (diluted serum sodium due to proportionally greater water retention). This dissociation between volume status and sodium concentration is the conceptual foundation of dysnatremia evaluation.

## Osmoreceptor–Vasopressin–Aquaporin-2 Axis: Regulating Tonicity

The body defends serum osmolality — and therefore [Na⁺] — with remarkable precision (±1–2%) via an integrated hypothalamic–renal axis [1, mechanism_review]. Specialized osmoreceptor neurons in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus detect changes in effective plasma osmolality; threshold sensitivity is exquisite, with even a 1% rise in osmolality triggering measurable arginine vasopressin (AVP, also called antidiuretic hormone, ADH) release from the posterior pituitary [5, mechanism_review]. AVP then binds to V2 receptors on principal cells of the renal collecting duct, activating a cAMP–protein kinase A cascade that phosphorylates aquaporin-2 (AQP2) water channels and drives their rapid insertion into the apical membrane within minutes [4, mechanism_review]. Water crosses the tubular lumen through AQP2 and exits the cell basally via AQP3 and AQP4, producing concentrated urine and retaining free water in the circulation — restoring osmolality toward set point [5, mechanism_review]. With sustained AVP stimulation (hours to days), AQP2 gene transcription is upregulated, amplifying overall water-reabsorptive capacity [5, mechanism_review]. Thirst operates in parallel: the same hypothalamic osmoreceptors that trigger AVP release also stimulate thirst above ~290 mOsm/kg, providing the behavioral limb of water retention. Together, AVP secretion and thirst constrain osmolality fluctuations to within approximately 2–3 mOsm/kg of the individual's set point under physiological conditions.

## Volume Regulation: A Separate but Interacting Axis

Critically, the regulation of ECF **volume** is governed by a distinct axis that is not primarily concerned with [Na⁺] at all. Volume and pressure sensors — baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch, as well as renal juxtaglomerular cells detecting renal perfusion pressure — activate the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) when effective circulating volume falls [8, mechanism_review]. Aldosterone acts on the collecting duct to increase apical sodium influx via epithelial sodium channels (ENaCs), enhancing Na⁺ reabsorption and expanding ECF volume. This RAAS/aldosterone axis regulates how much **total-body sodium** is retained or excreted, not the serum concentration. The two axes can conflict: in severe volume depletion, baroreceptors override osmoreceptors and drive non-osmotic AVP release, causing water retention even at subnormal osmolality — a clinically important scenario that produces hypovolemic hyponatremia [3, mechanism_review].

## Tonicity, Cell Volume, and Neurological Consequences

Because the blood–brain barrier is freely permeable to water but relatively impermeable to sodium, acute changes in plasma tonicity drive rapid water shifts into or out of brain cells. Acute hyponatremia — a fall in [Na⁺] producing hypotonic plasma — causes water influx into neurons, with brain cell swelling and raised intracranial pressure; this is the mechanism of hyponatremic encephalopathy, manifesting as headache, confusion, seizures, and, in severe cases, herniation and death [3, mechanism_review]. Acute hypernatremia — a rise in [Na⁺] producing hypertonic plasma — extracts water from neurons, causing brain shrinkage, stretching of bridging veins, and risk of subdural hemorrhage [2, mechanism_review]. The brain's adaptive response — accumulation of intracellular idiogenic osmoles (organic osmolytes) over hours to days — partially restores cell volume during sustained hypernatremia, but this same adaptation creates the danger of over-rapid correction: correcting hypernatremia too quickly reverses the osmolar gradient, floods previously adapted cells, and can cause cerebral edema [2, mechanism_review]. Analogously, over-rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia strips neurons of osmolytes faster than they can be resynthesized, risking osmotic demyelination syndrome (formerly central pontine myelinolysis) [1, mechanism_review]. The kinetics of adaptation thus dictate the rate of safe correction for both disorders.

## Clinical Summary

Serum [Na⁺] (normal 135–145 mmol/L) is maintained primarily by the hypothalamic osmoreceptor–AVP–AQP2 axis, which tunes renal water excretion to buffer plasma tonicity. It is a read-out of the ratio of exchangeable solutes to total body water, not of dietary salt. Hyponatremia is a relative water excess; hypernatremia is a relative water deficit. Changes in [Na⁺] alter brain cell volume and underlie the neurological complications of both dysnatremias, as well as the danger of over-rapid correction.
