# Section B — Reference Ranges, Units & Interpretation

## Reference Interval and Units

Serum sodium is reported in millimoles per litre (mmol/L), which is numerically identical to milliequivalents per litre (mEq/L) because sodium is monovalent — no conversion factor is needed between the two unit expressions.

The adult reference interval is approximately **135–145 mmol/L** [1, regulatory]. Dysnatraemia is classified at both poles:

| Condition | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Mild hyponatraemia | 130–134 mmol/L |
| Moderate hyponatraemia | 125–129 mmol/L |
| Severe / profound hyponatraemia | < 125 mmol/L |
| Hypernatraemia | > 145 mmol/L |
| Severe hypernatraemia | > 155 mmol/L |

These thresholds are adopted from the 2014 European Society of Intensive Care Medicine / European Society of Endocrinology clinical practice guideline [1, regulatory] and corroborated by the North American expert panel consensus [2, regulatory].

## The Hyponatraemia Interpretation Framework

Because hyponatraemia (serum Na < 135 mmol/L) arises from mechanistically distinct processes, a stepwise algorithm is required before any clinical or nutritional interpretation is made [1, regulatory; 2, regulatory; 3, mechanism_review].

### Step 1 — Measure Serum Osmolality / Tonicity

The first question is whether the low sodium reflects a genuinely hypotonic state.

- **Normal or elevated serum osmolality (≥ 275 mOsm/kg)** in the setting of a low measured sodium indicates a non-hypotonic cause — either pseudohyponatraemia (see below) or a translocational / hyperosmolar state (see glucose correction, below). Treatment directed at water restriction would be inappropriate and potentially harmful.
- **Low serum osmolality (< 275 mOsm/kg)** confirms true hypotonic hyponatraemia and initiates Step 2.

### Step 2 — Assess Volume Status

Clinical assessment divides hypotonic hyponatraemia into three haemodynamic categories:

1. **Hypovolaemic hyponatraemia** — reduced extracellular fluid volume (ECF); causes include gastrointestinal losses, excessive diuresis, third-spacing. Urine sodium < 20–30 mmol/L (unless the kidney is the primary sodium-losing organ).

2. **Euvolaemic hyponatraemia** — normal ECF; the dominant cause is the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuresis (SIAD/SIADH). Characterised by urine osmolality persistently > 100 mOsm/kg (typically > 300 mOsm/kg) and urine sodium ≥ 20–30 mmol/L despite hyponatraemia. Other euvolaemic causes: hypothyroidism, glucocorticoid deficiency, certain drugs.

3. **Hypervolaemic hyponatraemia** — expanded ECF (oedema states: heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome). Urine sodium typically < 20 mmol/L due to avid renal sodium retention; effective circulating volume is perceived as low despite total body sodium excess.

### Step 3 — Urine Sodium and Urine Osmolality as Decision Branches

| Urine Na | Urine Osm | Likely aetiology |
|---|---|---|
| < 20–30 mmol/L | variable | Hypovolaemia (extrarenal loss) or hypervolaemic states (HF, cirrhosis) |
| ≥ 20–30 mmol/L | > 100 mOsm/kg (often > 300) | SIADH; also diuretic use, renal salt-wasting |
| ≥ 20–30 mmol/L | < 100 mOsm/kg | Primary polydipsia; reset osmostat |

This three-step framework is embedded in both the European guideline [1, regulatory] and the Hoorn & Zietse compilation [3, mechanism_review].

## Pseudohyponatraemia: A Laboratory Artifact

Pseudohyponatraemia is a **measurement artifact**, not a physiological state, and must be excluded before treating an apparently low sodium result.

**Mechanism.** Plasma is normally ~93% water and ~7% solids (proteins + lipids). When lipid or protein concentrations are severely elevated, the aqueous fraction falls below 93%. Indirect ion-selective electrodes (indirect ISE) — the dominant platform on most centralised chemistry analysers — dilute the sample before measurement and assume 93% water content. When this assumption is violated, the reported sodium is falsely depressed even though the sodium concentration *within the aqueous phase* is normal [4, mechanism_review].

Conditions causing pseudohyponatraemia: severe hypertriglyceridaemia (triglycerides typically > 10–15 mmol/L / > 1000 mg/dL), severe hyperproteinaemia (e.g., paraproteinaemia in myeloma), extreme hypercholesterolaemia.

**How to recognise it.** The key distinguishing feature is a **normal or elevated measured serum osmolality** (≥ 275–285 mOsm/kg) in the presence of low indirect-ISE sodium. In genuine hypotonic hyponatraemia, osmolality is always low. A direct ISE measurement (e.g., from an arterial blood gas analyser, which does not dilute the sample) returns a normal sodium and confirms the artifact [4, mechanism_review].

## Translocational / Hyperosmolar Hyponatraemia: The Glucose Correction

**Mechanism.** When plasma glucose is markedly elevated — as in diabetic ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic states — osmotic water shifts from cells into the extracellular compartment dilute sodium. Serum osmolality is elevated (not low), indicating the hyponatraemia is dilutional and secondary, not reflective of true sodium deficit.

**Glucose correction formula.** The measured sodium must be adjusted before any interpretation of sodium homeostasis. Two correction factors are in clinical use:

- **Katz (1973) factor: + 1.6 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL glucose above 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L).** This is the historically taught value, derived from in-vitro calculations.
- **Hillier (1999) factor: + 2.4 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL glucose above 100 mg/dL.** Hillier et al. measured actual serum sodium in diabetic patients across a range of glucose concentrations and found the mean correction required was 2.4 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL increment — 50% larger than the Katz estimate, particularly at glucose levels > 400 mg/dL where the dilutional effect is nonlinear [5, cohort].

Corrected Na (Hillier) = Measured Na + 2.4 × [(glucose in mg/dL − 100) ÷ 100]

The Hillier factor is now generally preferred in clinical practice; both factors are often quoted together [2, regulatory; 5, cohort]. The same dilutional logic applies to mannitol infusions.

## Over-Rapid Correction Danger

### Chronic Hyponatraemia → Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome (ODS)

When hyponatraemia has been present for more than 24–48 hours, brain cells adapt by exporting organic osmolytes (taurine, myoinositol, glutamate) to protect against cerebral oedema. Rapid sodium correction reverses this adaptation abruptly, causing osmotic shrinkage of myelin-bearing cells and demyelination — classically of the pons (central pontine myelinolysis) but also extrapontine structures.

**Guideline correction rate limits [1, regulatory; 3, mechanism_review]:**
- **General recommendation:** do not increase serum sodium by more than **10–12 mmol/L in the first 24 hours** and **18 mmol/L in the first 48 hours**.
- **High-risk patients** (serum Na ≤ 105 mmol/L, hypokalaemia, alcoholism, malnutrition, chronic liver disease, or pre-menopausal women): limit correction to **4–6 mmol/L per 24 hours; not to exceed 8 mmol/L per 24 hours** [6, mechanism_review].

A 2023 systematic review (96 documented ODS cases) found demyelination occurred in 10.4% of cases even with correction < 10 mmol/L per 24 hours, reinforcing the stricter 6–8 mmol/L target for high-risk groups [6, mechanism_review].

If overcorrection occurs (sodium rising too fast), desmopressin (dDAVP) plus electrolyte-free water can be used to re-lower sodium — so-called "rescue" or "rescue re-lowering."

### Hypernatraemia → Cerebral Oedema

The converse danger exists in chronic hypernatraemia: brain cells accumulate osmolytes (idiogenic osmoles) to defend against cell shrinkage. Correcting hypernatraemia faster than approximately **10–12 mmol/L per 24 hours** risks cerebral oedema, seizures, and brainstem herniation.

## Hypernatraemia (Na > 145 mmol/L): Free-Water Deficit

Hypernatraemia almost always reflects a free-water deficit relative to total body solute — either inadequate intake, excessive insensible loss (fever, burns), diabetes insipidus, or, rarely, sodium excess. The free-water deficit is estimated as [1, regulatory; 2, regulatory]:

**FWD (L) = TBW × [(current Na / target Na) − 1]**

Where TBW ≈ 0.6 × body weight in kg (young males), 0.5 × weight (young females / elderly males), or 0.45 × weight (elderly females).

Example: 70 kg male, measured Na = 158 mmol/L, target = 145 mmol/L:
TBW = 42 L; FWD = 42 × [(158/145) − 1] = 42 × 0.090 = **3.8 L**

This estimate covers the deficit only; ongoing losses (urine, insensible) must be replaced in addition. Free water is replaced orally (water, dilute feeds) or intravenously (5% dextrose in water).
