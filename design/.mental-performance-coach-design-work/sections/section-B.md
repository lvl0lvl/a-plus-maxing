# Section B — Nootropic / Cognitive-Enhancer Compound Landscape (Evidence-Graded)

Goal-agnostic LIBRARY survey of the cognitive-enhancer compound landscape, type-tagged per claim and deliberately hype-resistant. This domain's marketing runs far ahead of its evidence; the section's job is to be an over-claim circuit-breaker, not a prescribing manual. Boundary fact encoded throughout: a `mental-performance-coach` READS the cognitive `compounds` class but does NOT author compound entries — that authoring escalates to the `supplement-specialist`. Prescription cognitive enhancers in healthy adults are framed as a PRESCRIPTIVE_DIRECTIVE routing to a prescriber, never a coach-directed protocol.

## Findings

### Caffeine

**1. Acute caffeine produces small but real improvements in attention (reaction time and accuracy) in healthy adults, with a dose-response that is linear for speed and inverted-U for accuracy.** A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 31 trials (1,455 participants) found Hedges' g = 0.28 for reaction time and g = 0.27 for accuracy favoring caffeine [1, meta_analysis]. Doses ≥200 mg produced larger gains; the reaction-time relationship was linear while accuracy followed a quadratic (inverted-U) pattern, declining beyond an optimal dose [1, meta_analysis]. Effects were *not* moderated by habitual consumption, task complexity, or attention network [1, meta_analysis]. **Certainty: moderate** for the small acute effect. **Heterogeneity:** present but the moderators above were null. **Agent relevance:** the effect size is small (g ≈ 0.27–0.28), not the transformative boost marketing implies.

**2. A substantial fraction of caffeine's apparent benefit in habitual users is withdrawal-reversal, not net enhancement.** The most influential statement of this view concluded that effects on performance and mood are "almost wholly attributable to reversal of adverse withdrawal effects" of short abstinence, with minimal net gain in sustained users [2, meta_analysis]. This is a methodological critique, not a settled fact — the 2025 attention meta-analysis [1, meta_analysis] found effects *independent* of habitual consumption, which argues against pure withdrawal-reversal. **Certainty: the truth is mixed/contested.** **Agent relevance:** the honest framing is "caffeine reliably restores a withdrawn baseline and produces a small genuine acute attention effect on top." Do NOT present caffeine as a large net enhancer.

**3. Tolerance to caffeine's performance benefit develops with habitual use, and abrupt cessation produces a clinically recognized withdrawal syndrome.** Withdrawal headache, fatigue, reduced alertness, and impaired concentration begin after overnight abstinence and peak roughly 20–51 h post-cessation [8, mechanism_review]. Caffeine Withdrawal is a DSM-5 diagnosis; Caffeine Use Disorder remains a "condition for further study," not a formal diagnosis [9, mechanism_review]. **Agent relevance:** tolerance means escalating dose yields diminishing return; the dependence framing matters for any taper guidance, which is a supplement-specialist / prescriber concern, not coach-authored.

**4. Up to 400 mg/day caffeine is not associated with adverse effects in healthy adults; concentrated forms are acutely dangerous.** A systematic review across acute toxicity, cardiovascular, bone/calcium, behavioral, and reproductive endpoints affirmed 400 mg/day as the no-adverse-effect threshold in healthy adults [10, meta_analysis]. The FDA cites the same 400 mg/day figure and warns that powdered/liquid caffeine reaches toxic and lethal concentrations (≈1 tsp powder ≈ 28 cups of coffee) [11, regulatory]. Toxicity is generally cited at ~10 mg/kg and potential lethality near 150 mg/kg [8, mechanism_review]. **AE/safety:** wide interindividual variation in sensitivity and clearance; pre-existing cardiac/anxiety conditions lower the safe ceiling. **Agent relevance:** 400 mg/day is the population reference; individual sensitivity overrides it.

### L-theanine and caffeine + L-theanine

**5. The caffeine + L-theanine combination produces small-to-moderate, short-window improvements in attention-switching and reaction time; benefits are larger than L-theanine alone but modest overall.** The 2025 Payne meta-analysis (50 RCTs identified; 16 trials across 15 references meta-analyzed) reported, for theanine + caffeine within ~2 h: attention-switching accuracy SMD 0.33 (95% CI 0.13–0.54), digit-vigilance accuracy SMD 0.20 (0.02–0.38), and simple reaction time SMD −0.71 (−0.92 to −0.50) [3, meta_analysis]. The authors flag large prediction intervals and I² values spanning near-zero to >75% [3, meta_analysis]. **Certainty: low-to-moderate** given heterogeneity. **Agent relevance:** the combination's headline use case is acute attention/focus in a short post-dose window — not durable cognitive gain.

**6. The mechanistic rationale (L-theanine attenuating caffeine's jitter while preserving alertness) is supported by a small foundational RCT but the literature is dominated by short crossover trials.** Owen et al. (2008), a placebo-controlled crossover in 27 participants using 50 mg caffeine ± 100 mg L-theanine, found the combination improved both speed and accuracy on attention-switching and reduced distractibility at 60 and 90 min [4, rct]. **Certainty: low** for any single small trial; the meta-analytic signal [3, meta_analysis] is the better anchor. **AE/safety:** L-theanine is well tolerated at studied doses (typically 100–200 mg); the AE profile of the combination is driven by its caffeine content (finding 4). **Agent relevance:** small-trial-dominated field; cite the meta-analysis for any numeric claim, not individual crossovers.

### Creatine for cognition

**7. Creatine's cognitive evidence is far weaker than its physical-performance evidence; the clearest signals are acute, high-dose, and concentrated in stressed states (sleep deprivation) and likely low-baseline subgroups (vegetarians).** A single oral 0.35 g/kg dose during 21 h sleep deprivation improved word memory (~10%) and processing speed (16–29%) and shifted cerebral high-energy phosphates (↑PCr/Pi) in a double-blind crossover (n=15) [5, rct]. A 2024 meta-analysis (16 RCTs, 492 participants, all creatine monohydrate) found significant but modest effects on memory (SMD 0.31, 95% CI 0.18–0.44) and processing speed (SMD −0.51, −1.01 to −0.01), with **no** significant effect on overall cognition or executive function [6, meta_analysis]. **Caveat (concentration audit):** this meta-analysis [6] later drew a published commentary alleging double-counting of non-independent within-study tests, and a 2025 corrigendum was issued — treat its pooled estimates as inflated and low-confidence. **Certainty: moderate for memory, low/very-low elsewhere; downgraded further by the double-counting critique.** The cognitive effect appears at ~20 g/day or single high doses, not at maintenance 3–5 g/day [12, regulatory]. **Agent relevance:** the much-promoted "creatine for brain" claim rests on stressed-state and low-baseline subgroups, not well-rested healthy adults at standard doses. (Vegetarian subgroup: mechanistically plausible due to lower dietary creatine baseline, but direct subgroup RCT evidence remains thin in these syntheses — flag as a mechanism-driven hypothesis, not a demonstrated effect.)

**8. The European regulator concluded there is NO established cause-and-effect relationship between creatine and improved cognitive function.** EFSA's 2024 evaluation of an Article 13(5) health claim reviewed 21 human studies and concluded a cause-and-effect relationship "has not been established," noting acute effects appeared only at 20 g/day and not at lower or continuous 5 g/day dosing [12, regulatory]. **Agent relevance:** this regulatory rejection is the single strongest hype-counterweight in the creatine-for-cognition space and should anchor any coach-facing framing.

### Omega-3 / DHA

**9. Omega-3 / DHA shows dose-related cognitive benefit at the population level, but effects in healthy younger adults are weak/absent — benefit concentrates in older adults, mild memory complaints, and low-baseline-intake individuals.** A 2025 dose-response meta-analysis (58 RCTs) reported per-2000 mg/day improvements in attention (SMD 0.98, GRADE low), processing speed (SMD 0.50, GRADE moderate), and memory (non-linear), concluding "modest improvement" overall [7, meta_analysis]. The Yurko-Mauro DHA-and-adult-memory meta-analysis (15 RCTs) found episodic-memory benefit in adults with mild memory complaints and at >1 g/day combined DHA/EPA, but **no significant between-group effect in healthy young adults** [13, meta_analysis]. **Certainty: low-to-moderate**, with wide confidence intervals and population-dependent effects. **AE/safety:** generally well tolerated; high doses can affect bleeding time. **Agent relevance:** DHA is a long-horizon, population/diet-dependent intervention, not an acute focus aid — its benefit is greatest where dietary baseline is low.

### Adaptogens / herbals

**10. Bacopa monnieri improves speed of attention with chronic (≥12-week) dosing, but the effect is narrow and dosing is slow-onset.** Kongkeaw et al. (2014) meta-analysis (9 RCTs, 437 analyzed) found shortened Trail B (−17.9 ms, 95% CI −24.6 to −11.2) and reduced choice reaction time (−10.6 ms, −12.1 to −9.2), concluding benefit on "speed of attention" specifically [14, meta_analysis]. Standardized extracts at 300–450 mg/day for ≥12 weeks were the studied regimen [14, meta_analysis]. **AE/safety:** dose-dependent gastrointestinal effects (nausea, cramping, increased stool) are the common AEs [14, meta_analysis]. **Certainty: moderate for the narrow attention-speed outcome.** **Agent relevance:** Bacopa is NOT an acute aid — it requires weeks of consistent dosing, and the effect is confined to attention speed, not broad cognition.

**11. Rhodiola rosea (fatigue) and Panax ginseng (cognition) rest on contradictory, high-risk-of-bias evidence; neither supports a confident efficacy claim.** For Rhodiola, a systematic review (11 trials) found effectiveness in only 2/6 physical-fatigue and 3/5 mental-fatigue RCTs and concluded ALL included studies had high risk of bias or reporting flaws, with "contradictory" overall evidence [15, mechanism_review]. For ginseng, the Cochrane review (9 RCTs) found "a lack of convincing evidence to show a cognitive enhancing effect of Panax ginseng in healthy participants and no high quality evidence" in dementia, with pooling precluded by heterogeneity [16, meta_analysis]. **AE/safety:** no serious AEs flagged in the ginseng Cochrane review; adaptogen trials are small and heterogeneous. **Certainty: low / very-low.** **Agent relevance:** these are the canonical "small-trial / open-label / heterogeneous" adaptogen problem cases — present as unproven, not as supported.

**12. L-tyrosine has a weak, conditional recommendation: it mitigates cognitive decrements specifically under acute stressors (sleep loss, cold, noise, multitasking), not as a baseline enhancer.** A rapid evidence assessment (14 trials: 10 RCTs + 4 CCTs) made a *weak* recommendation favoring tyrosine for cognitive performance under stress, with no recommendation for physical performance, and judged evidence insufficient for confident claims [17, mechanism_review]. **Certainty: low.** **Agent relevance:** L-tyrosine's evidence is acute-stressor-bound — it is not shown to enhance cognition in unstressed, rested individuals.

### Prescription cognitive enhancers off-label in healthy adults

**13. In healthy (non-sleep-deprived) adults, modafinil and methylphenidate produce only small, domain-selective effects and D-amphetamine shows none — the cognitive-enhancement reputation outruns the evidence.** A meta-analytic series found: modafinil overall SMD 0.12 (memory-updating 0.28); methylphenidate overall SMD 0.21 (recall 0.43, sustained attention 0.42, inhibitory control 0.27); D-amphetamine no significant effect — with the authors concluding the data are "far from positive" and the enhancement claim "not supported by the evidence so far" [18, meta_analysis]. A modafinil systematic review found effects highly contingent on assessment complexity and noted impaired divergent/creative thinking in some studies [19, mechanism_review]. **Certainty: low-to-moderate; effects are small and inconsistent.** **Agent relevance:** in healthy non-clinical adults the benefit is small and domain-specific, and creativity may be impaired — the popular "smart drug" framing is not evidence-supported.

**14. Prescription stimulants and modafinil are controlled prescription drugs carrying dependence, cardiovascular, and diversion/legal liabilities — use in healthy adults is off-label and MUST route to a prescriber (PRESCRIPTIVE_DIRECTIVE), never coach-directed.** Methylphenidate and amphetamines are US Schedule II controlled substances and carry black-box warnings for abuse/dependence and serious cardiovascular events; nonmedical use associates with increased risk of transient ischemic attack and sudden death/ventricular arrhythmia, and cardiovascular risk is materially elevated in those with pre-existing conditions [20, mechanism_review]. National nonmedical-use prevalence among adults was ~1.9% (2020), with documented diversion [20, mechanism_review]. **Agent relevance — the load-bearing boundary:** a mental-performance-coach has no authority to direct, dose, or source these. Any user query about off-label cognitive use of modafinil/methylphenidate/amphetamine is a hard PRESCRIPTIVE_DIRECTIVE escalation to a licensed prescriber. The coach documents the landscape; it does not prescribe, and it does not author the underlying `compounds` entries (that is supplement-specialist scope, and for scheduled drugs, prescriber scope).

## Concentration / sponsorship / population notes

- **Creatine cognition [6]:** the headline 2024 meta-analysis attracted a published commentary alleging double-counting of non-independent tests and required a 2025 corrigendum — pooled effects are likely inflated; the regulator (EFSA [12]) found no established effect. Effects concentrate in *stressed states* (sleep deprivation [5]) and plausibly *low-baseline* subgroups (vegetarians, mechanistic only). Not demonstrated in rested healthy adults at maintenance doses.
- **Caffeine [1,2]:** the field splits between a "small genuine acute effect" camp [1] and a "withdrawal-reversal explains it" camp [2]; honest framing acknowledges both. No single-lab dominance evident.
- **L-theanine + caffeine [3,4]:** small-trial-dominated, short crossover designs, high heterogeneity (I² up to >75%); some industry interest exists (tea/supplement sponsors) — the meta-analysis [3] is the safest anchor and itself flags wide prediction intervals.
- **Omega-3 / DHA [7,13]:** population-dependent — benefit concentrates in older adults, mild-memory-complaint, and low-dietary-intake groups; near-null in healthy young adults [13].
- **Adaptogens (Rhodiola [15], ginseng [16], L-tyrosine [17]):** the canonical open-label / small-trial / high-risk-of-bias / heterogeneous problem space. Tyrosine's effect is acute-stressor-bound only.
- **No population-mismatch flags were needed:** all numeric/efficacy claims above are sourced to human RCTs or meta-analyses of human RCTs. Mechanistic phosphate-shift detail [5] is from the same human crossover, not extrapolated from animal data. Vegetarian-subgroup creatine benefit is explicitly flagged as a mechanism-driven hypothesis, not a demonstrated human-subgroup effect.
