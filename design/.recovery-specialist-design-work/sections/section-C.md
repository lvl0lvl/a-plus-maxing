# Section C — Recovery modalities (evidence-graded)

This section grades the major recovery modalities, separating short-term
perceptual/soreness benefits from chronic adaptation effects, and observational health
outcomes from training-recovery outcomes. The defining discipline is hype-resistance: most of
these modalities have a kernel of real effect surrounded by marketing that overstates it. Where
a benefit is perceptual, context-dependent, or counterproductive for a given training goal,
that is stated explicitly. Sleep and nutrition — the highest-leverage recovery levers — are
boundary pointers only, owned by the sleep-coach and nutritionist agents.

---

## Cold-water immersion (CWI) / cold plunge

**Grade: EQUIVOCAL** — context-dependent and goal-dependent. ESTABLISHED short-term
soreness/perceived-recovery benefit; ESTABLISHED interference with strength-and-hypertrophy
adaptation when applied chronically after resistance training. These two facts point in
opposite directions, which is why the modality cannot carry a single flat grade.

The short-term recovery case is the strongest part of the CWI evidence base. A large network
meta-analysis of 55 RCTs found CWI reliably reduces delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) and
the muscle-damage marker creatine kinase (CK) within 24–48 h, and improves perceived recovery
[5, meta_analysis]. Dose mattered: medium-duration medium-temperature immersion (10–15 min at
11–15 °C) ranked best for DOMS (SUCRA 84.3 %), while colder 5–10 °C protocols ranked best for
CK and jump recovery [5, meta_analysis]. Critically, the same analysis found effects on
neuromuscular strength/power outcomes were inconsistent — several protocols showed no benefit
or even reduced jump performance [5, meta_analysis]. So even within the "recovery" framing,
the benefit is concentrated in soreness and damage markers, not in restored force output, and
a meaningful share of the soreness benefit is perceptual and placebo-plausible.

The interference effect is the load-bearing caution. Roberts et al. (2015), a 12-week RCT
(n = 21) plus an acute-signalling study (n = 9), found post-resistance-training CWI blunted
long-term gains: type II fibre cross-sectional area rose 17 % with active recovery but not with
CWI, myonuclei per fibre rose 26 % with active recovery but not CWI, and CWI attenuated
p70S6K anabolic signalling and satellite-cell activity [1, rct]. Fyfe et al. (2019), an
independent RCT (n = 16), replicated the hypertrophy blunting — type II fibre CSA was reduced
in the CWI group — but found 1-RM strength was NOT impaired in that study [4, rct]. Pooling
this, Grgic's meta-analysis (10 studies, n = 170) found CWI attenuated strength gains with a
small but significant effect (SMD ≈ −0.23, 95 % CI −0.45 to −0.01) [3, meta_analysis], and
Piñero's hypertrophy meta-analysis (8 studies, n = 116) found greater hypertrophy with
resistance training alone than with CWI + resistance training (cSMD₀.₅ ≈ −0.22, 95 % CrI
−0.47 to 0.04) [2, meta_analysis].

The honest synthesis: the interference effect is robust for **hypertrophy** and reasonably
supported for **strength**, but it is specific to (a) resistance/strength-adaptation goals
and (b) chronic post-session use. In an **endurance** context, or for acute in-tournament
recovery where you must perform again within hours and adaptation is not the goal, the
soreness/perceived-recovery benefit can be worth the trade. The interference effect should NOT
be over-generalised to "cold is always bad" — it is a goal-conditional trade-off.

## Sauna / heat therapy

**Grade: split.** Cardiovascular-mortality association: PROVISIONAL (observational, single
cohort). Training-recovery / endurance-performance via heat acclimation: PROVISIONAL. The two
must never be conflated.

The most-cited sauna evidence is the Laukkanen Finnish KIHD cohort: a prospective study of
2,315 middle-aged men, median 20.7 y follow-up, finding men using the sauna 4–7×/week had
markedly lower sudden-cardiac-death risk (HR 0.37, 95 % CI 0.18–0.75) and fatal-CVD risk
versus 1×/week users, with a dose-response trend (P ≤ .005) [6, cohort]. This is genuinely
interesting — but it is a **cardiovascular/mortality** outcome, **observational**, in a single
population (older Finnish men with a cultural sauna habit), confounded by the obvious fact that
healthier men can tolerate frequent sauna. It is **not** a training-recovery claim, and the
recovery-specialist must never present it as evidence that sauna speeds muscle recovery.
Healthy-user bias and reverse causation are unaddressed by the design.

The training-relevant claim is heat acclimation. Scoon et al. (2007), a small crossover RCT in
competitive male runners, found post-exercise sauna bathing expanded plasma volume (+7.1 %)
and total blood volume (+5.6 %), with run-time-to-exhaustion improving ~32 % (≈1.9 % time-trial
equivalent), and performance change correlating tightly with plasma-volume change (r ≈ 0.96)
[7, rct]. This is a plausible, mechanistically coherent **endurance/heat-acclimation**
benefit driven by plasma-volume expansion — not a soreness or muscle-repair benefit. It is a
single small study and should be graded PROVISIONAL. There is no good evidence that sauna
accelerates recovery from resistance-training muscle damage; presenting it as a hypertrophy or
strength recovery tool would be overclaiming.

## Contrast water therapy (CWT)

**Grade: EQUIVOCAL.** Better than doing nothing for soreness; not demonstrably better than the
simpler single-temperature alternatives, on low-quality evidence.

Bieuzen et al. (2013), a PLOS One systematic review/meta-analysis (18 trials, all rated high
risk of bias), found CWT produced significantly greater DOMS improvement than passive recovery
at every timepoint from <6 h to 96 h [8, meta_analysis]. However — and this is the
hype-resistance point — when CWT was compared head-to-head against other active modalities, it
showed **no statistically significant advantage** over cold-water immersion, active recovery,
or compression, and only narrow advantages over warm-water immersion at 24 h and 96 h
[8, meta_analysis]. The authors concluded there was "little difference in recovery outcome
between CWT and other popular recovery interventions" [8, meta_analysis]. So the benefit is
real relative to passive rest but not relative to the cheaper, simpler options, and the
underlying trial quality is poor. The alternating hot/cold "vascular pump" mechanism is
frequently asserted but not well substantiated.

## Compression garments

**Grade: PROVISIONAL → low-end ESTABLISHED for perceived/soreness recovery.** Consistent
small-to-moderate effects across several outcomes, but modest in absolute terms.

Hill et al. (2014), a meta-analysis (12 studies) in the British Journal of Sports Medicine,
reported compression garments produced moderate effects on DOMS reduction (Hedges g ≈ 0.40),
strength recovery (g ≈ 0.46), power recovery (g ≈ 0.49), and CK reduction (g ≈ 0.44)
[9, meta_analysis]. These are among the more consistent recovery-modality effect sizes,
landing in the small-to-moderate band rather than the trivial band — a point in compression's
favour relative to foam rolling or massage. Mechanism (reduced muscle oscillation, modest
venous-return support) is plausible but not fully resolved. The effect is real but should be
framed as "moderate, mostly soreness/perceived-recovery and power, not transformational," and
much of the strength/power "recovery" may reflect reduced perceived soreness enabling fuller
effort rather than faster tissue repair.

## Massage / foam rolling / percussive therapy

**Grade: PROVISIONAL for DOMS/ROM; EQUIVOCAL/trivial for performance.** Real but small and
largely perceptual effects; near-zero effect on force production.

Massage carries the strongest signal of this cluster. Dupuy et al. (2018), a large
meta-analysis of 99 studies in Frontiers in Physiology, found massage was the single most
effective technique for reducing DOMS and perceived fatigue (Hedges g across modalities ranged
−2.26 to −0.40, with massage at the powerful end), alongside small overall reductions in CK
(SMD −0.37), IL-6 (−0.36), and CRP (−0.38) [13, meta_analysis]. That said, the soreness/fatigue
benefit is heavily perceptual and the inflammatory-marker effects are small.

Foam rolling is weaker still. Wiewelhove et al. (2019), a meta-analysis of 21 studies in
Frontiers in Physiology, found post-rolling effects on recovery were minor: muscle-pain
reduction g ≈ 0.47, sprint g ≈ 0.34, but jump g ≈ 0.06 and strength g ≈ 0.21 — concluding the
effects are "rather minor and partly negligible" and recommending foam rolling primarily as a
warm-up rather than a recovery tool [10, meta_analysis].

Percussive therapy (massage guns) follows the same pattern: a single application produces an
acute increase in flexibility/ROM and explosive strength comparable to static stretching, and
multiple treatments are needed to reduce pain — positioning the device as a cost-effective
alternative to vibration rather than a recovery breakthrough [12-PT, mechanism_review].
Manufacturer claims (Theragun and similar) of accelerated muscle repair are vendor marketing
and ground no effect-size here [12, vendor_label]. The defensible message:
massage/rolling/percussion are reasonable for comfort, ROM, and perceived readiness; they do
not meaningfully speed force recovery or drive adaptation.

## Active recovery

**Grade: EQUIVOCAL.** Reliably clears blood lactate faster than passive rest; that lactate
clearance does NOT reliably translate to better subsequent performance.

This is a classic mechanism-vs-outcome decoupling. Light active recovery (e.g. easy spinning,
walking) accelerates blood-lactate removal in an intensity-dependent way — that part is
established physiology. But Ortiz et al. (2019), a systematic review of 26 studies in the
Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, found only weak evidence that post-exercise
active recovery improves subsequent athletic performance, with consistent positive effects
seen only for short 6–10 min bouts; the review explicitly judged blood-lactate clearance rate
to be an **unreliable recovery marker** [12-AR, mechanism_review]. Two things follow: (1) the
lactate-clearance narrative is a textbook example of a mechanistic proxy being mistaken for an
outcome — lactate is largely a fuel and a correlate, not the cause of next-session fatigue;
(2) the recovery-specialist should frame active recovery as low-cost and benign, plausibly
useful for between-bout recovery, but should not promise performance restoration from it.

## Photobiomodulation / red light

**Grade: PROVISIONAL.** Some signal for fatigue/performance and DOMS, on low-to-moderate
quality evidence with a single-research-group concentration risk.

Vanin et al. (2018), a systematic review/meta-analysis in Lasers in Medical Science (39 trials,
28 in meta-analysis), found photobiomodulation produced "some effect in favour" of improved
muscle performance and reduced fatigue, on very-low-to-moderate quality evidence, with active
wavelengths 655–950 nm and doses of 20–60 J (small muscles) / 60–300 J (large muscles)
[11-PBM, meta_analysis]. The honest framing flags two problems: the evidence quality is
self-rated low-to-moderate, and a large fraction of the favourable photobiomodulation literature
originates from a small cluster of overlapping research groups (notably Leal-Junior and
collaborators), some with device-industry ties — a concentration that warrants caution before
treating the effect as settled. Consumer red-light panels are also frequently sold on vendor
claims that exceed the dosed, wavelength-controlled laboratory protocols; such vendor pages
ground no effect-size [12, vendor_label]. Grade PROVISIONAL, lean skeptical.

## Sleep and nutrition (boundary pointers — NOT covered here)

Sleep and nutrition are the highest-leverage recovery foundations and dwarf every modality
above in effect size. They are deliberately **out of scope** here to avoid duplication: sleep
behaviour, extension, and circadian timing are owned by the **sleep-coach** agent; protein
dose/timing, energy availability, carbohydrate refuelling, and creatine are owned by the
**nutritionist** agent. The recovery-specialist defers to those agents and foregrounds the
ordering: fix sleep and fuelling first; the modalities above are marginal refinements on top of
those foundations, not substitutes for them.

---

## Key claims for the agent design

- **CWI interference effect is the headline trade-off.** Post-resistance-training cold-water
  immersion reliably blunts hypertrophy (Roberts 2015 [1, rct]; Fyfe 2019 [4, rct]; Piñero
  2024 meta cSMD₀.₅ ≈ −0.22 [2, meta_analysis]) and attenuates strength gains (Grgic 2023 meta
  SMD ≈ −0.23 [3, meta_analysis]). The recovery-specialist MUST surface this trade-off whenever
  a strength/hypertrophy goal is in play: CWI helps soreness today and hurts adaptation over
  weeks.
- **CWI's recovery benefit is goal-conditional, not universal.** It is defensible for endurance
  contexts and acute "perform-again-soon" situations, and for soreness/DOMS [5, meta_analysis];
  it should be discouraged in the hours after resistance training aimed at adaptation.
- **Sauna CV-cohort data is NOT a recovery claim.** The Laukkanen mortality association
  [6, cohort] is observational, single-cohort, healthy-user-biased, and cardiovascular — it
  must never be cited as evidence that sauna speeds muscle recovery.
- **Sauna's training relevance is heat acclimation / plasma-volume expansion** for endurance
  (Scoon 2007 [7, rct]), graded PROVISIONAL — not a muscle-repair or hypertrophy tool.
- **Contrast water therapy beats passive rest but not the simpler alternatives**
  [8, meta_analysis], on poor-quality evidence; do not present it as superior to plain CWI or
  active recovery.
- **Compression garments have the most consistent small-to-moderate recovery effect sizes**
  (g ≈ 0.40–0.49, Hill 2014 [9, meta_analysis]) — modest, mostly soreness/power, low cost,
  low risk.
- **Massage > foam rolling > percussion, and all are mostly perceptual.** Massage leads for
  DOMS/fatigue (Dupuy 2018 [13, meta_analysis]); foam rolling effects are "minor and partly
  negligible" (Wiewelhove 2019 [10, meta_analysis]); none meaningfully improves force
  production. Vendor device claims ground nothing [12, vendor_label].
- **Active recovery clears lactate but lactate clearance ≠ performance recovery** (Ortiz 2019
  [12-AR, mechanism_review]); treat lactate as a proxy, not an outcome.
- **Photobiomodulation is PROVISIONAL with a research-group-concentration flag** (Vanin 2018
  [11-PBM, meta_analysis]); evidence is low-to-moderate quality and dominated by a small set of
  overlapping groups with industry ties.
- **Sleep and nutrition outrank every modality here** and are owned by other agents; the
  recovery-specialist orders advice foundations-first and defers on sleep/fuelling content.
