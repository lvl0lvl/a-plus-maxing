# Section B — Overtraining, overreaching & load management

## B.1 The FOR / NFOR / OTS taxonomy

The governing reference is the joint ECSS/ACSM consensus statement [1, regulatory], published jointly in *European Journal of Sport Science* and *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*. It defines a continuum of training-maladaptation states distinguished primarily by the **time course of recovery and the trajectory of performance**, not by any single test result.

- **Functional overreaching (FOR)** is an intensified-training state producing a short-term performance decrement *without* severe or lasting negative symptoms. After a period of rest, performance rebounds — often above baseline — i.e., the supercompensation pattern. Recovery is typically days to ~2 weeks [1, regulatory].
- **Non-functional overreaching (NFOR)** occurs when the balance between training stress and recovery is not respected. Performance stagnates or declines and does not rebound on the FOR timescale; recovery requires weeks to months, and psychological/neuroendocrine signs appear. The performance gain that motivated the overload is *not* realized [1, regulatory].
- **Overtraining syndrome (OTS)** is the extreme of the continuum, characterized by **prolonged maladaptation** of the athlete and of multiple biological, neurochemical, and hormonal regulation systems. Recovery spans months. The consensus explicitly notes that distinguishing NFOR from OTS "is very difficult and will depend on the clinical outcome and exclusion diagnosis," because the two states often present with the *same* clinical, hormonal, and other signs [1, regulatory].

The load-bearing design implication: the three states are separated mainly by **how long performance stays depressed and whether it eventually rebounds**, which can only be known retrospectively. There is no test that prospectively says "this is OTS, not FOR."

## B.2 OTS is a diagnosis of exclusion with no validated standalone biomarker

The consensus statement is unambiguous that **no marker meets the criteria for a diagnostic test of OTS**. It reviewed hormones, performance tests, psychological tests, and biochemical/immune markers and concluded that "several markers ... are used, but none of them meet all the criteria" for accepted OTS detection. Recognition therefore *requires* exclusion of organic disease and infection, plus assessment of dietary contributors (energy/carbohydrate/protein deficit, iron and magnesium deficiency) and allergy [1, regulatory].

This is reinforced by the earlier Urhausen & Kindermann review [2, mechanism_review], which concludes that "there has been little improvement in recent years in the tools available for the diagnosis of OTS." Specifically on blood markers: resting measurements such as the **(free) testosterone:cortisol ratio** "may serve to reveal circumstances which ... impair the exercise performance" but "are **not useful in the diagnosis of established OTS**." No single blood marker (creatine kinase, cortisol, the testosterone:cortisol ratio, etc.) qualifies as a valid standalone diagnostic; the most useful indicators are ergometric performance decrements, mood-state deterioration, and subjective complaints (heavy legs, sleep disturbance) — and even these can be confounded [2, mechanism_review].

Design implication: a recovery agent must **not diagnose OTS**. OTS by definition demands exclusion of organic disease, which is a clinician's task. The agent's correct posture is pattern-flagging (load/recovery imbalance, persistent performance decline, mood disturbance) and escalation to a medical liaison for organic-disease workup — never a biomarker-based "you have OTS" call.

## B.3 Autonomic markers and the role/limits of HRV

OTS and NFOR are associated with autonomic dysregulation. A study in highly trained athletes [3, cohort] found lower HRV and lower vagal (parasympathetic) influence with increased sympathetic cardiovascular control in NFOR and OTS versus unaffected athletes; NFOR tended to present with sympathetic dominance, while the more advanced OTS state showed broad "total autonomic dystonia" — depressed regulation across both branches — in a majority of cases. This matches the classical clinical picture of "sympathetic-dominant" (early, often anaerobic/sprint athletes) versus "parasympathetic-dominant" (advanced, often endurance) OTS presentations described in the consensus [1, regulatory].

However, the *prospective monitoring* value of resting HRV is weaker than its popularity implies. The Bellenger et al. systematic review and meta-analysis [4, meta_analysis] found that **resting HRV is largely unaffected by overreaching** ("largely unaffected ... although this may be the result of methodological issues that warrant further investigation"). Positive (performance-improving) adaptation was associated with small-to-moderate *increases* in resting vagal indices (RMSSD, HF power, SD1); negative adaptation (overreaching) produced minimal/trivial resting change. Critically, **post-exercise HRV increased in BOTH directions of adaptation**, so an isolated post-exercise HRV rise cannot distinguish healthy adaptation from harmful overreaching without supplementary measures [4, meta_analysis].

Design implication: HRV is a *trend* tool for tracking autonomic state, not a detector of NFOR/OTS. A single value — or even a directional change — is ambiguous and must be triangulated with load and subjective data.

## B.4 Monitoring tools: validity and limits

**Session-RPE (sRPE).** The Foster session-RPE method multiplies a session's perceived intensity (CR-10 RPE) by its duration to yield internal training load in arbitrary units. A review of its validity [5, mechanism_review] confirms good validity, reliability, and internal consistency across sports, ages, and competitive levels, with strong correlation to HR-based internal-load indices (TRIMP). Limits: RPE is modulated by personality (neuroticism, anxiety, depression), fitness/expertise, environment (heat, altitude), caffeine, glycemia, and fasting; time spent at high intensity drives RPE more than duration alone; and coach-vs-athlete intensity perceptions frequently diverge [5, mechanism_review]. **Training monotony and strain** (derived from the sRPE time series) are associated with overtraining onset when high load is combined with low day-to-day variability [5, mechanism_review].

**Acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR) — contested, not settled.** ACWR (recent "acute" load divided by longer-baseline "chronic" load) was widely adopted for injury-risk management, but its methodology is now seriously disputed. Lolli et al. argued the *conventional* (coupled) calculation produces **spurious correlation via mathematical coupling** — the acute window is contained within the chronic window, so numerator and denominator share data. Coyne et al. [6, cohort] published a case-study counter, reporting trivial-to-small effect sizes between coupled and uncoupled ACWRs and concluding coupling poses minimal practical risk; Lolli et al. rebutted that this conclusion was biased by pooling within- and between-subject data, yielding over-precise estimates. Independently, Impellizzeri et al. [7, mechanism_review] mount a broader conceptual critique: the ratio has flawed statistical properties, is "not consistently and unidirectionally related to injury risk," adds noise and statistical artifacts, and — most damningly — "there is no evidence supporting the use of ACWR in training-load-management systems or for training recommendations aimed at reducing injury risk." Causation (that *manipulating* ACWR changes injury rate) has never been established [7, mechanism_review].

Design implication: the agent may compute/display ACWR as one descriptive load-trend signal but **must NOT present it as a validated injury-prevention threshold** (e.g., the "0.8–1.3 sweet spot"). It is methodologically contested; say so.

**Total Quality Recovery (TQR).** Kentta & Hassmen's TQR scale mirrors the RPE construct to rate *perceived recovery* (a 6–20 scale, plus a TQR-10 variant), so recovery can be tracked against the RPE-quantified breakdown process [8, practitioner_protocol]. It is a structured subjective recovery-monitoring convention, useful for matching recovery to load — but it grounds no effect-size or prevalence claim.

**Profile of Mood States (POMS) / mood disturbance.** POMS measures six mood dimensions (tension, depression, anger, vigour, fatigue, confusion). Morgan's "iceberg profile" (high vigour, low negative moods) flattens or inverts with overtraining; an inverse profile and rising total mood disturbance track increasing load [9, mechanism_review]. The ECSS/ACSM consensus lists mood-state deterioration among the more useful (though non-specific) OTS indicators [1, regulatory].

**Subjective beats objective for tracking load.** The Saw et al. systematic review (56 studies) [10, meta_analysis] found subjective self-report measures (wellness, mood, perceived recovery) reflected acute and chronic training load with *superior sensitivity and consistency* than commonly used objective measures, and that subjective and objective measures generally did **not** correlate. Subjective wellbeing was impaired by acute load increases and chronic load, and improved with acute load decreases [10, meta_analysis].

Design implication: cheap daily subjective questionnaires (wellness, sleep, soreness, mood, TQR) are evidence-supported as the *primary* load-response monitoring layer; objective devices are adjuncts, not replacements.

## B.5 Deload / taper programming

**Taper** has the strongest evidence of the load-management interventions. Bosquet et al.'s meta-analysis [11, meta_analysis] found the most effective strategy is a ~2-week exponential, fast-decay taper that reduces **training volume by 41–60%** while **maintaining intensity** and only moderately reducing frequency. This is an efficacy finding for competition-prep peaking.

**Deload** as a routine within-block recovery microcycle (e.g., every 3–6 weeks) is largely **convention rather than RCT-validated efficacy** [practitioner_protocol]; the principle that volume should be reduced while intensity is preserved is borrowed from the taper and detraining literatures (B.5 taper, B.6) rather than from deload-specific trials. The agent should frame deload prescriptions as reasonable convention, not proven dose-response.

## B.6 Detraining: time course of fitness loss

Mujika & Padilla's two-part review is the anchor [12, mechanism_review]. In **long-term detraining** (>4 weeks of insufficient stimulus), VO2max declines markedly but, in highly trained athletes, **remains above sedentary control values**; *recently acquired* VO2max gains are lost completely. Endurance performance is impaired in proportion to cardiovascular regression (reduced blood volume, cardiac dimensions, ventilatory efficiency). **Strength/force production declines slowly** and stays above control for very long periods. Crucially, losses "can be avoided or limited by reduced training strategies, as long as training **intensity is maintained** and frequency reduced only moderately," while volume can be cut substantially [12, mechanism_review] — the same intensity-preserving principle that underlies effective tapering and rational deloads.

Design implication: short layoffs (≤~2–4 weeks, e.g., illness/injury recovery) are physiologically low-cost for trained individuals, especially for strength; the agent can reassure rather than alarm, and prescribe intensity-preserving maintenance to blunt loss.

## Key claims for the agent design

- **OTS has no validated standalone biomarker.** CK, cortisol, and the testosterone:cortisol ratio are not diagnostic [1, regulatory; 2, mechanism_review]. The recovery-specialist must NOT diagnose OTS and must route suspected cases to a medical liaison to **exclude organic disease/infection** — OTS is a diagnosis of exclusion.
- **FOR vs NFOR vs OTS is defined retrospectively by recovery time and performance rebound**, not by any prospective test [1, regulatory]. The agent flags patterns; it does not label a state in real time.
- **ACWR is methodologically contested — do not present it as settled.** Mathematical coupling/spurious-correlation critiques are unresolved and a strong conceptual review finds no evidence it should drive load-management decisions [6, cohort; 7, mechanism_review]. Display it (at most) as a descriptive trend, never a validated injury threshold.
- **HRV cannot detect overreaching reliably.** Resting HRV is largely unaffected by overreaching, and post-exercise HRV rises with BOTH good and bad adaptation [4, meta_analysis]. Use HRV only as a triangulated trend signal.
- **Subjective self-report tracks training load better than objective measures** [10, meta_analysis]. Make daily subjective wellness/mood/recovery questionnaires (incl. TQR, POMS-style mood) the primary monitoring layer; objective devices are adjuncts.
- **sRPE is a valid internal-load metric** but is modulated by psychological state, environment, caffeine, and fitness; monotony+high-load predicts overtraining onset [5, mechanism_review].
- **Taper is evidence-supported; deload is convention.** A ~2-week, 41–60% volume reduction with maintained intensity improves performance [11, meta_analysis]; routine deloads are reasonable `practitioner_protocol`, not proven dose-response.
- **Detraining is intensity-sensitive and slow for strength.** Short layoffs are low-cost for trained athletes; maintain intensity (cut volume/frequency) to limit loss [12, mechanism_review]. Reassure, don't alarm, on brief breaks.
