## Section D — Safety, dosing, route, PK, regulatory & monitoring

### D.1 Safety: the honest picture

Dihexa (N-hexanoyl-Tyr-Ile-(6)-aminohexanoic acid amide; also called PNB-0408) has **no published human safety data of any kind**. There is no Phase 1 dose-escalation study, no human pharmacokinetic profile, no characterised adverse-effect spectrum in people, and no chronic toxicity, reproductive toxicity, genotoxicity, or carcinogenicity dataset in any species [1, anecdote_aggregate]. The compound has never been evaluated in a registered human trial; a search of ClinicalTrials.gov returns zero results for any dihexa study [2, regulatory].

The preclinical record in rodents is limited to the studies produced mainly by the originating Washington State University group (Wright, Kawas, Harding). Those studies assessed behavioural outcomes (Morris water maze, novel-object recognition) across durations of roughly 4–12 weeks; no systematic organ-level histopathology or oncogenicity evaluation was reported [3, animal]. The one study that applied dihexa in a disease model outside the originating group (a 5-week 3-nitropropionic acid Huntington's-like rat study, Wells et al. 2024) reported no efficacy and described no adverse findings, but was not designed as a toxicology study [4, animal].

#### Retraction of the mechanistic evidence base — and the earlier Expression of Concern

The integrity timeline begins **before** the 2025 retractions. In **September 2021**, the same journal (*J Pharmacol Exp Ther*) published an **Expression of Concern** (PMID 34551989) covering the primary dosing anchor, McCoy AT et al. 2013 (PMID 23055539) [3-EoC, animal] — flagging data-integrity concerns in that paper years before any retraction was issued. This means the animal dosing record that underpins all preclinical dose extrapolation was formally integrity-flagged in 2021.

The 2025 retractions then followed. The central paper establishing that Dihexa's procognitive effects are **mechanistically dependent on HGF/c-Met pathway activation** — Benoist CC et al. (2014), *J Pharmacol Exp Ther* 351:390–402, PMID 25187433 — was formally retracted in April 2025 (retraction PMID 40312093, *J Pharmacol Exp Ther* 392(4):103567) [5, animal]. A companion 2012 paper from the same group ("Development of Angiotensin IV Analogs as Hepatocyte Growth Factor/Met Modifiers," *J Pharmacol Exp Ther* 340:539–548) was retracted simultaneously (retraction PMID 40312092) [6, animal]. The WSU investigation found that figures and data in those papers contained falsified and/or fabricated elements attributable to co-author Leen H. Kawas. The retractions substantially weaken the evidentiary basis for the HGF/c-Met mechanism, though they do not in themselves resolve whether that mechanism operates.

**Chronological summary:** the 2021 EoC on the dosing paper was the first published integrity flag in this series; the 2025 Benoist/Kawas retractions followed. The dosing data was therefore integrity-flagged years before the mechanistic papers were retracted.

#### The load-bearing theoretical risk: HGF/c-Met-potentiation and oncogenesis

This is the most significant safety concern associated with Dihexa, and it must be stated prominently and precisely.

The HGF/c-Met receptor tyrosine kinase axis is one of the best-characterised oncogenic signalling pathways in cancer biology. When constitutively activated — through gene amplification, mutation, or excessive ligand — c-Met promotes tumour cell proliferation, survival, invasion, angiogenesis, and metastasis across a broad range of malignancies including non-small-cell lung cancer, breast cancer, glioblastoma, pancreatic cancer, prostate cancer, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [7, mechanism_review]. The FDA approved capmatinib (2020) and tepotinib for metastatic NSCLC harbouring MET exon 14 skipping mutations specifically because blocking this pathway suppresses tumour growth [8, regulatory]. Approved c-Met inhibitors exist precisely because chronic activation of this receptor drives cancer.

Dihexa is proposed to work not by constitutively activating c-Met but by potentiating the receptor's response to subthreshold concentrations of endogenous HGF — a mechanistic distinction from the constitutive gain-of-function seen in cancers [3, animal — **EoC Sept 2021, PMID 34551989; integrity-flagged**]. Whether potentiation of a canonically oncogenic receptor under conditions of ambient ligand exposure carries a meaningfully different long-term tumour-promotion risk is **unknown**. No carcinogenicity study for Dihexa exists. Rodent studies lasting 4–12 weeks are structurally incapable of detecting tumour promotion, which typically requires latency of months to years. The question is therefore not answered by the existing literature; it is simply unanswered. Any person or clinician weighing self-administration of a systemically bioavailable, brain-penetrant c-Met potentiator must treat this as a **theoretical but mechanistically grounded oncogenic risk with no empirical safety data to bound it**.

#### Grey-market-specific risks

Dihexa is sold exclusively through unregulated research-chemical vendors — not through any pharmacy, compounding facility, or authorised distributor [1, anecdote_aggregate]. The grey-market supply chain introduces risks entirely absent from the preclinical literature:

- **Chemical identity:** no mandatory identity verification; vendor-labelled "Dihexa" may be a structurally similar analogue, a degradation product, or an entirely unrelated compound.
- **Purity:** no pharmacopoeial standard; common contaminants include residual synthesis reagents and process impurities.
- **Sterility:** injectable forms (SC) sold through grey-market channels carry infection risk; no sterility testing is required or verified.
- **Dose accuracy:** vendor-stated concentrations are unverified.

### D.2 Dosing and route: preclinical vs grey-market

**Important framing:** the numbers below come from animal studies. No human dose-response relationship exists. Extrapolation from rodent doses to humans is speculative; allometric scaling is not validated for this compound.

#### Preclinical (animal-derived; citable)

The originating group used oral administration at approximately **1–2 mg/kg/day** in rats to reverse scopolamine-induced cognitive deficits in Morris water maze tasks [3, animal — **EoC Sept 2021, PMID 34551989; integrity-flagged**]. Intracerebroventricular (ICV) doses in the range of **0.1–1 nmol** and intraperitoneal doses of **0.05–0.5 mg/kg** appear across the rodent literature, with most cognitive-enhancement studies clustering around **0.5–2 mg/kg** by various peripheral routes [3, animal — **EoC; integrity-flagged**; 9, animal]. The 2021 APP/PS1 Alzheimer's mouse study (Sun et al., *Brain Sciences*, PMID 34827486) used oral administration without specifying the dose in the abstract, reporting BBB penetration and spatial learning improvements [9, animal]. The 2024 HD-model study (Wells et al., PMID 38489193) used SC administration over 5 weeks with no cognitive benefit observed [4, animal].

**Oral bioavailability and BBB penetration:** The Wright/Harding group designed Dihexa specifically to overcome the pharmacokinetic barrier of prior angiotensin IV analogs, engineering N- and C-terminal modifications to improve metabolic stability and lipophilicity sufficient for BBB passive diffusion. Published descriptions characterise it as "orally active, blood-brain barrier-permeable" [3, animal — **EoC Sept 2021, PMID 34551989; integrity-flagged**; 10, mechanism_review]. The absolute oral bioavailability fraction has not been formally determined in any published study, and no human pharmacokinetic data exist.

#### Grey-market conventions (anecdote-sourced; no number may be grounded here)

Community forums and vendor-adjacent commentary describe oral, topical, and subcutaneous routes. Reported cycle patterns vary widely. Because these are anecdote_aggregate-sourced conventions with no clinical grounding, no specific doses, frequencies, or cycle lengths are cited in this entry. These conventions should not be used to guide any dosing decision.

### D.3 Regulatory status

**United States (FDA):** Dihexa is not approved for any human indication. No Investigational New Drug (IND) application has been filed for Dihexa itself, and no New Drug Application (NDA) exists [1, anecdote_aggregate]. Community sources report that in April 2026 the FDA removed Dihexa acetate from its Category 2 restricted-peptide list as part of a 12-peptide reclassification action — **however, this claim is sourced only from anecdote_aggregate (Tier-5) material; no primary FDA administrative record confirming this action was located at research access (FDA 503A bulk-drug-substance pages returned 404 during verification).** This claim should not be read as a confirmed FDA administrative action. If confirmed by a primary FDA record, removal from Category 2 would not constitute approval, would not create a prescription pathway, and would not indicate the agency assessed the compound as safe for human use; it would mean only that the active safety-concern nomination is no longer maintained under that administrative category [1, anecdote_aggregate]. The substance remains distributed exclusively as a research-use-only chemical.

**503A/503B (compounding):** Dihexa is not on any FDA-approved drug list and has not been evaluated under the bulk-drug substance program that governs compounding pharmacy access (503A) or outsourcing facilities (503B). It is not a compoundable substance under current FDA framework.

**Athira Pharma / fosgonimeton (ATH-1017) — critical distinction:** Athira Pharma (formerly M3 Biotechnology) developed fosgonimeton (ATH-1017), a subcutaneously administered HGF/c-Met modulator, as a distinct, patented clinical candidate informed by the same mechanistic rationale as Dihexa but not identical to it. Fosgonimeton is structurally engineered to have superior pharmacokinetic properties compared to Dihexa [11, regulatory]. Fosgonimeton completed Phase 1 (88 participants, no serious adverse events), Phase 2 ACT-AD, Phase 2 SHAPE (Parkinson's disease dementia/DLB), and Phase 2/3 LIFT-AD (Alzheimer's disease) trials — the Phase 2/3 LIFT-AD trial failed its primary and key secondary endpoints in September 2024 [11, regulatory]. **The clinical-trial data for fosgonimeton cannot be attributed to grey-market Dihexa: they are different compounds with different chemistry, different routes, different pharmacokinetics, and only the clinical compound has human safety data.** Citing Athira's Phase 1 safety findings as evidence that Dihexa is safe in humans is a category error.

**European / other jurisdictions:** Dihexa is not approved by EMA, MHRA, TGA, or any other regulatory authority. It is not a controlled substance in most major jurisdictions but lacks any authorised therapeutic use everywhere.

**WADA status:** Dihexa is not explicitly named on the 2026 WADA Prohibited List. However, the Prohibited List's **S0 (Non-Approved Substances)** category functions as a categorical ban on "any pharmacological substance which is not addressed by any of the subsequent sections of the List and with no current approval by any governmental regulatory health authority for human therapeutic use" — including drugs under pre-clinical or clinical development, discontinued drugs, designer drugs, and veterinary drugs [12, regulatory]. As a substance not approved for human therapeutic use by any governmental regulatory authority, Dihexa would fall under S0 by this definition, making it prohibited in sport at all times (in and out of competition) for athletes subject to WADA-code-compliant organisations. **Verification caveat:** WADA has not published a formal classification ruling for Dihexa. Athletes should seek a formal Therapeutic Use Exemption inquiry or NADO guidance before drawing conclusions from this general-rule analysis.

### D.4 Contraindications, monitoring, and stopping criteria

No validated contraindication list, monitoring protocol, or stopping criteria exist for Dihexa, because the compound has never been evaluated in humans. The following reflects the mechanistic and regulatory picture, not empirical clinical guidance.

**Strongest precautionary contraindication — personal or family cancer history:**
Given the HGF/c-Met-potentiation mechanism and the complete absence of carcinogenicity data, any personal history of cancer (particularly cancers with documented c-Met involvement: lung, breast, hepatocellular, glioblastoma, gastric, pancreatic) or a first-degree family history of those cancers constitutes a serious precautionary contraindication. The theoretical risk of potentiating an oncogenic pathway in someone with a pre-existing oncological substrate or genetic predisposition is not quantifiable but is not zero. A clinician presented with a patient requesting information about Dihexa should treat this as a hard stop absent any carcinogenicity data.

**Monitoring posture (no Dihexa-specific biomarker exists):**
No biomarker validated as a Dihexa-specific safety signal exists. There is no established Dihexa "therapeutic window," no validated pharmacodynamic marker for on-target effect, and no surrogate endpoint for monitoring c-Met potentiation in a clinical context. Given the mechanistic concern, the most defensible surveillance posture for anyone who has used this compound is:

- Routine cancer surveillance appropriate to age and family history (not a Dihexa-specific interval — standard clinical guidelines)
- Heightened attention to any unexplained mass, lymphadenopathy, systemic symptoms, or laboratory changes that might prompt earlier oncological evaluation
- Hepatic function monitoring (LFTs at baseline and periodically) given c-Met's role in hepatocyte biology and the use of hepatotoxic c-Met inhibitors as a comparator class
- Avoidance of concomitant agents that further stimulate HGF/c-Met signalling
- **Drug interaction — c-Met inhibitors (capmatinib, tepotinib):** concurrent use of an approved c-Met inhibitor with Dihexa creates a direct pharmacodynamic opposition: one agent potentiates the c-Met receptor while the other suppresses it. The net effect is unknown and the combination is mechanistically irrational. Patients receiving capmatinib (Tabrecta), tepotinib (Tepmetko), or any investigational c-Met/MET inhibitor for cancer should be regarded as having an absolute contraindication to any c-Met potentiator. This is not a speculative interaction — it is a mechanistically direct agonist/antagonist opposition at the same receptor target [8, regulatory].

**Stopping criteria:**
In the absence of clinical data, the reasonable stopping criterion is any signal of unexplained proliferative, haematological, or hepatic change that emerges temporally with use, pending evaluation. The compound should not be resumed without oncological clearance.

**Immunocompromised individuals:** Theoretical additional caution applies; HGF/c-Met has roles in immune cell migration, and the net effect of potentiating that pathway in immunocompromised states is unknown.
