## Section A — Identity & molecular mechanism

> **Evidence-tier ceiling — read before interpreting this section.**
>
> The dihexa-SPECIFIC mechanistic evidence is **evidence tier D** — the lowest tier, meaning the primary mechanistic claims rest on retracted or integrity-flagged sources with zero independent replication. Four compounding factors drive this ceiling:
>
> 1. **Foundational mechanism paper retracted.** The paper establishing the HGF/c-Met mechanism (Benoist CC et al., JPET 2014, PMID 25187433 [7]) was **formally retracted in April 2025** (retraction notice PMID 40312093) after a WSU investigation found falsified or fabricated elements in figures 1B and 2A/C.
> 2. **Companion synthesis/dosing paper under Expression of Concern.** McCoy AT et al. (JPET 2013, PMID 23055539 [1]), the only source for dihexa's oral dosing in animals, received a **Notice of Concern in September 2021** (PMID 34551989) for the same image-manipulation concern and has not been retracted as of June 2026 — its integrity remains unresolved.
> 3. **"100-million-times-more-potent-than-BDNF" potency figure originates from the retracted paper.** The widely repeated "7 orders of magnitude" / "100 million times more potent than BDNF" claim appears in the body of the now-retracted Benoist 2014 paper (hippocampal slice synaptogenesis assay). It cannot be treated as established.
> 4. **Zero independent, non-WSU-originating-group replication of the HGF/c-Met mechanism.** Every primary experimental source for the HGF/c-Met → dendritic-spine → synaptogenesis chain traces to the Harding/Wright/Kawas group at WSU. No peer-reviewed paper from an independent laboratory has replicated this specific mechanism.
>
> The unretracted **IRAP/AT4 hypothesis** (Albiston et al. 2001, PMID 11707427 [5] — independent group, not WSU, no integrity concerns) represents a parallel candidate mechanism for the broader AngIV/AT4 procognitive axis, but it does not establish the synaptogenic potency claims specific to dihexa.
>
> **This ceiling is a documented evidence reality, not a section defect.** The section below handles the retracted/EoC material honestly — it does not present HGF/c-Met or the BDNF-potency figure as established — but readers should enter with the ceiling explicit.

### A.1 Identity and chemical structure

Dihexa (also designated PNB-0408) is a small-molecule peptidomimetic developed by Joseph W. Harding and John W. Wright at Washington State University as a metabolically stabilized analog of **angiotensin IV (AngIV; Ang II residues 3–8)**. Its chemical name is **N-hexanoyl-Tyr-Ile-(6)-aminohexanoic acid amide**, reflecting two deliberate terminal modifications of the parent AngIV sequence: an N-terminal hexanoyl acyl cap replacing the free amine (improving lipophilicity and protease resistance) and a C-terminal 6-aminohexanoic acid amide in place of the free carboxylate (reducing hydrogen-bond donor character). These changes were designed to confer oral bioavailability and blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability while retaining the procognitive core activity residing in the Tyr-Ile dipeptide unit [1, animal; 2, mechanism_review].

Verified structural data (PubChem CID 129010512):

- **Molecular formula:** C₂₇H₄₄N₄O₅
- **Molecular weight:** 504.7 g/mol
- **IUPAC name:** (2S,3S)-N-(6-amino-6-oxohexyl)-2-[[(2S)-2-(hexanoylamino)-3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)propanoyl]amino]-3-methylpentanamide
- **Isomeric SMILES:** CCCCCC(=O)N[C@@H](CC1=CC=C(C=C1)O)C(=O)N[C@@H]([C@@H](C)CC)C(=O)NCCCCCC(=O)N
- **InChIKey:** XEUVNVNAVKZSPT-JTJYXVOQSA-N

Dihexa belongs to a lineage of AngIV-derived compounds developed by the Harding/Wright group from the 1990s onward, including Nle¹-AngIV and earlier AT4 receptor agonists [3, animal; 4, mechanism_review]. The peptidomimetic strategy replaced the labile natural hexapeptide with a structure resistant to aminopeptidase cleavage [1, animal].

### A.2 The angiotensin IV / AT4 receptor lineage

Angiotensin IV (Val-Tyr-Ile-His-Pro-Phe) is a hexapeptide metabolite of the renin-angiotensin system generated by successive aminopeptidase cleavage of Ang II. Beginning in the mid-1990s, the Harding/Wright group at WSU characterised a distinct brain receptor subtype—the **AT4 receptor**—with selective affinity for AngIV and its analogs, distinct from the AT1 and AT2 receptors that bind Ang II [4, mechanism_review]. The AT4 receptor is heavily expressed in hippocampal CA1–CA3 fields, parahippocampal regions, and other memory-related structures. In rat spatial-learning paradigms (circular water maze), intracerebroventricular AT4 agonists facilitated acquisition while the AT4 antagonist Divalinal-AngIV impaired it, establishing the AngIV/AT4 system as a procognitive axis [3, animal].

A separate line of research identified the molecular identity of the AT4 receptor. Albiston et al. (2001), using protein purification and peptide sequencing from bovine adrenal cortex, reported that the AT4 receptor is **insulin-regulated aminopeptidase (IRAP)**, a type II transmembrane zinc metalloprotease [5, in_vitro]. Functional characterisation showed that AT4 ligands dose-dependently inhibit IRAP's catalytic activity, suggesting that prolonged IRAP inhibition extends the half-lives of neuropeptide substrates in synaptic clefts as a putative memory mechanism [5, in_vitro; 6, mechanism_review]. The IRAP identification was corroborated by Albiston et al. (2003) in a follow-up mechanistic review [6, mechanism_review]. This IRAP hypothesis remained the dominant mechanistic framework for AngIV-derived cognition effects through the mid-2000s.

The Harding/Wright group later proposed an alternative target: the **hepatocyte growth factor (HGF)/c-Met receptor system** (see A.3), creating a contested dual-target picture that the Progress in Neurobiology 2015 review explicitly flags as unresolved [2, mechanism_review].

### A.3 Proposed mechanism: HGF/c-Met potentiation and synaptogenesis

The principal mechanistic claim for dihexa — and the basis for all downstream claims about its potency — is that it functions as a **potentiator of HGF at the c-Met receptor tyrosine kinase**, acting at sub-threshold HGF concentrations to augment c-Met phosphorylation and downstream signalling (PI3K/Akt, MAPK/ERK pathways), resulting in **dendritic spine formation and de novo synaptogenesis**.

The foundational experimental paper for this claim is Benoist CC, Kawas LH, Zhu M et al. (2014), JPET 351:390–402 (PMID 25187433) [7, animal]. This study reported, in HEK-293T cells (c-Met phosphorylation Western blots), MDCK cells (scatter assay), dissociated rat hippocampal neurons (1–2 day-old Sprague-Dawley pups, ≥10 neurons per condition), and organotypic hippocampal slice cultures, that:

- Dihexa at 10⁻¹⁰–10⁻¹² M augmented HGF-dependent c-Met phosphorylation at sub-threshold HGF concentrations (1.25–2.5 ng/mL), without activating c-Met independently when applied alone.
- Dihexa increased dendritic spine density and miniature EPSC frequency in hippocampal neurons comparably to HGF itself.
- An HGF antagonist ("Hinge") and c-Met knockdown abolished both spinogenesis and the procognitive effect of orally-delivered dihexa in Morris water maze (male Sprague-Dawley rats, spatial learning task).

**Critical integrity notice:** This paper received a Notice of Concern from JPET in September 2021 (JPET 378:311) citing possible image manipulation linked to co-author Leen Kawas. Following a WSU investigation, the paper was **formally retracted in April 2025** (retraction notice PMID 40312093, DOI 10.1016/j.jpet.2025.103567). Specifically, figures 1B (HGF dimerization) and 2A/C (c-Met phosphorylation Western blots) were found to contain falsified or fabricated elements. **The HGF/c-Met mechanism of dihexa should therefore be treated as unconfirmed by independent replication in the primary literature.** The behavioral Morris water maze data (Figure 7, a separate assay independent of the implicated Western blots) was not directly implicated, but its evidentiary weight is diminished by the overall retraction.

The companion characterization paper — McCoy AT, Benoist CC, Wright JW et al. (2013), JPET 344:141–154 (PMID 23055539) [1, animal] — which described dihexa's synthesis, oral activity in rat (scopolamine-reversal, Morris water maze, 0.05–2 mg/kg oral gavage in Sprague-Dawley rats n=8–10; 2 mg/kg "completely reversed the scopolamine-dependent learning deficit"), and brain penetration (radiolabeling) — received a Notice of Concern in September 2021 (PMID 34551989, JPET 378:313) for the same image manipulation concern. As of June 2026 it has not been retracted but remains under scrutiny.

A third review paper — Wright JW, Kawas LH, Harding JW (2015), Prog Neurobiol 125:26–46 (PMID 25455861) [2, mechanism_review] — co-authored by Kawas, reviews the AngIV → AT4/IRAP vs. HGF/c-Met controversy and the dihexa data without carrying an EoC or retraction notice as of June 2026.

The broader **AngIV/AT4 procognitive axis** (pre-dihexa, pre-HGF/c-Met claims) rests on papers unaffected by the Kawas concerns — most notably Wright et al. (1999), J Neurosci 19:3952–3961 (PMID 10234025) [3, animal], and the Albiston IRAP identification papers [5, in_vitro; 6, mechanism_review].

### A.4 The "seven orders of magnitude more potent than BDNF" claim

A widely repeated claim in secondary sources and popular science holds that dihexa is **"seven orders of magnitude more potent than BDNF"** (i.e., ~10 million-fold) in promoting synaptogenesis. This claim originates from the Harding/Wright originating group and was disseminated via a WSU press release (October 2012) summarizing unpublished/preliminary organotypic hippocampal slice data; the seven-orders-of-magnitude figure does not appear in the abstract of the now-retracted Benoist 2014 paper or in the McCoy 2013 EoC paper as retrieved via PubMed. It appears in the body of the now-retracted Benoist 2014 paper in a hippocampal slice synaptogenesis assay comparing effective concentrations of dihexa vs. BDNF.

This claim requires multiple layers of qualification:

1. **In-vitro only:** The comparison is from an in-vitro organotypic slice assay, not an animal efficacy or human study.
2. **Originating-group single source:** The data were produced and reported by the lab that developed and patented dihexa; no independent replication of this specific concentration-potency comparison is documented in the peer-reviewed literature.
3. **Retracted primary source:** The paper containing this in-vitro comparison has been formally retracted (April 2025) due to figure manipulation. The potency figure cannot be treated as established.
4. **Different mechanism, not comparable endpoint:** BDNF acts via TrkB; dihexa is proposed to act via HGF/c-Met potentiation. The "potency" comparison reflects effective concentration in one assay system, not a general cognitive or clinical potency.

The claim is stated here for transparency (it dominates secondary literature) but **must not be presented as an established fact**.

### A.5 Pharmacology: oral activity and BBB penetration

The rationale for the N-hexanoyl / C-terminal amide modifications was to increase lipophilicity and protease resistance relative to the native AngIV hexapeptide, which is rapidly degraded in plasma and does not cross the BBB efficiently [1, animal; 2, mechanism_review]. The McCoy 2013 paper (under EoC) reported oral activity in young Sprague-Dawley rats at 0.05–2 mg/kg by gavage in the Morris water maze, and brain penetration via radiolabeling across multiple regions including hippocampus, exceeding blood-contamination levels. These findings are from the originating group and are subject to the same scrutiny as the broader Kawas-linked publication record. Independent pharmacokinetic characterisation of dihexa in peer-reviewed literature is absent as of June 2026.

Wright JW and Harding JW's 2019 review (J Alzheimers Dis 67:469–480, PMID 30664507) [8, mechanism_review] — a non-Kawas co-authored paper — provides broader renin-angiotensin/cognition context without independent pharmacokinetic data for dihexa specifically.

The systematic review by Ho JK and Nation DA (2018, Neurosci Biobehav Rev 92:209–225, PMID 29733881) [9, meta_analysis] found across 32 experimental studies that Ang IV and analogs (including dihexa) showed cognitive benefits in 8 of 9 models of cognitive impairment, predominantly via AT4R-mediated mechanisms. This review pre-dates the retractions and does not adjudicate the IRAP vs. HGF/c-Met dispute.

No human pharmacokinetic studies, dose-escalation studies, or clinical trials of dihexa are indexed in PubMed or ClinicalTrials.gov as of June 2026.
