## Section C — HGF/c-Met oncogenic tension, independent replication & AngIV-analog class context

### C.1 The HGF/c-Met pathway: a dual-use signaling axis

Dihexa's proposed mechanism of action is potentiation of hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) signaling through its receptor, the c-Met receptor tyrosine kinase. This is not a niche academic pathway: HGF/c-Met is one of the most extensively characterized oncogenic axes in cancer biology. Under physiological conditions the pathway regulates cell proliferation, morphogenesis, wound healing, and organ regeneration. When dysregulated — through MET amplification, activating point mutations, exon-skipping alterations, or autocrine HGF loops — it becomes a primary driver of tumor initiation, invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis [1, mechanism_review].

A 2018 *Nature Reviews Cancer* analysis by Comoglio, Trusolino, and Boccaccio frames three distinct oncogenic modes: (a) *oncogene addiction*, where genetic alteration of MET in tumor cells drives neoplastic transformation; (b) *oncogene expedience*, where transcriptional upregulation of HGF/c-Met fosters cancer-cell survival and dissemination in adaptive responses to stress or therapy; and (c) *oncogene inherence*, where MET modulates cancer stem-cell self-renewal [1, mechanism_review]. The axis drives downstream activation of PI3K/AKT, Ras/MAPK, JAK/STAT3, SRC, and Wnt/β-catenin cascades — the same canonical tumor-promoting networks [2, mechanism_review].

Aberrant HGF/c-Met signaling has been implicated across a broad cancer spectrum: hepatocellular carcinoma, non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, glioblastoma, and ovarian, pancreatic, and prostate carcinomas, among others [2, mechanism_review]. The oncogenic relevance is not theoretical — it is clinically operationalized. Capmatinib (FDA approved May 2020) and tepotinib (FDA approved February 2021) are selective MET kinase inhibitors approved for metastatic NSCLC harboring MET exon 14 skipping mutations, based on objective response rates of 68% and 43%, respectively, in pivotal trials [3, regulatory]. The existence of these approved targeted inhibitors is pharmacological proof that the MET pathway drives clinically significant disease: the FDA would not approve pathway inhibitors in the absence of demonstrated oncogenic dependency.

### C.2 The oncogenic-tension safety question for a systemic HGF/c-Met potentiator

Dihexa's purported mechanism is the inverse of these drugs: where capmatinib and tepotinib block c-Met to slow tumor growth, Dihexa is proposed to potentiate HGF-induced c-Met phosphorylation — acting as an allosteric amplifier of the ligand-receptor interaction [4, animal; 5, animal]. This creates a direct mechanistic tension.

**The concern, stated precisely:** If HGF/c-Met signaling potentiation drives procognitive synaptogenesis at low HGF concentrations in healthy neurons (the proposed therapeutic mechanism), the same potentiation could theoretically amplify signaling in cells where HGF/c-Met is already dysregulated or upregulated — including pre-neoplastic or neoplastic cells. In oncology, a pathway's dose-response relationship to tumor promotion is not always linear, and even modest augmentation of an already-active oncogenic signal can accelerate tumor progression. This concern applies with particular force in three scenarios: (1) individuals with subclinical or early malignancy; (2) individuals with germline or somatic MET alterations; (3) chronic grey-market use without oncologic surveillance.

**What has not been studied:** No carcinogenicity studies (rodent long-term or otherwise) have been conducted on Dihexa. No in vivo tumor-promotion or co-carcinogenesis assays have been published. The entire available pre-clinical dataset is restricted to cognitive-behavioral outcomes in rodent models (see Section B); oncologic endpoints were not assessed in any published experiment. This is not evidence of safety — it is an unstudied risk domain. Absence of a carcinogenicity study is the normal state for an academic research compound that never entered formal IND development; it does not imply the signal was sought and found absent.

**Fosgonimeton as partial translational evidence:** Athira Pharma's fosgonimeton (ATH-1017/NDX-1017), a small-molecule HGF/c-Met agonist prodrug evolved from the same Harding/Wright research program, completed a Phase 2/3 trial (LIFT-AD) in Alzheimer's disease patients without a reported oncologic safety signal in the trial period [see Alzforum therapeutics entry, unverified as primary source]. However, the LIFT-AD trial ran 26 weeks and was not powered or designed to detect oncologic endpoints; it failed its primary cognitive endpoint. A 26-week human trial is insufficient to rule out tumor-promotion effects that typically manifest over years.

The theoretical oncogenic concern for a systemic HGF/c-Met potentiator is genuine, grounded in well-established cancer biology, and unresolved by any existing Dihexa-specific data. It should be treated as an open safety question, not a demonstrated risk and not a dismissed one.

### C.3 Independent replication: a highly concentrated literature

A candid audit of the Dihexa efficacy and mechanism literature reveals an extreme dependence on a single originating research group.

The foundational papers establishing that Dihexa (a) has cognitive-enhancing effects in rodents and (b) operates via HGF/c-Met potentiation were produced almost entirely by Joseph W. Harding, John W. Wright, and co-authors at Washington State University (WSU). The key papers in this line are:

- Kawas et al. (2012), JPET 340:539–548 — establishing AngIV analogs as HGF/c-Met modifiers [5, animal]
- McCoy et al. (2013), JPET 344:141–154 — evaluating metabolically stabilized AngIV analogs including Dihexa [4, animal]
- Benoist et al. (2014), JPET 351:390–402 — establishing HGF/c-Met dependence of procognitive/synaptogenic effects [retracted; see below]

**Research integrity findings:** All four papers co-authored by Leen Kawas (a WSU doctoral student who subsequently became CEO of Athira Pharma, the company commercializing this research) received Expressions of Concern from JPET editors in 2021 for "possible image manipulation" [6, anecdote_aggregate]. A subsequent WSU special committee investigation found that Kawas had systematically altered images in her 2011 doctoral dissertation and in at least four co-authored publications from 2011–2014. WSU revoked Kawas's PhD; she resigned as Athira CEO in October 2021; Athira later paid $10 million to settle securities fraud lawsuits. Two papers were formally retracted in April 2025:

- Kawas et al. (2012) JPET: Figures 3A and 4 found to contain falsified/fabricated data. Retraction notice PMID 40312092 [5, animal — RETRACTED].
- Benoist et al. (2014) JPET: Figures 1B and 2A/C found to contain falsified/fabricated elements. Retraction notice PMID 40312093 [formerly cited mechanism paper — RETRACTED].

The McCoy et al. (2013) paper received a Notice of Concern (PMID 34551989) and as of this writing remains formally in question.

**Independent replication — verdict:** Genuine independent replication is minimal. One published study from a completely separate group corroborates cognitive enhancement: Sun et al. (2021) at China Pharmaceutical University and Nanjing Medical University demonstrated that Dihexa improved spatial learning in male APP/PS1 Alzheimer's-model transgenic mice (n = 12 per group; Morris water maze) and reduced neuronal loss [7, animal]. Critically, this independent study identified PI3K/AKT signaling — not HGF/c-Met — as the relevant pathway; no HGF or c-Met discussion appears in the paper [7, animal]. This mechanistic discordance has two implications: (a) the independent replication does not validate the HGF/c-Met mechanism specifically; and (b) because the primary HGF/c-Met mechanism papers have been retracted for data fabrication, the mechanistic basis of Dihexa's proposed action currently lacks non-retracted, non-WSU-group experimental support. Whether Dihexa exerts any cognitive effect through HGF/c-Met potentiation, PI3K/AKT activation, some other mechanism, or a combination remains unresolved in the post-retraction literature.

The bottom line on replication: one independent animal study (APP/PS1 mouse model), one group, one paper, mechanistic pathway distinct from the retracted HGF/c-Met claim. No human studies exist. No replication of the specific HGF/c-Met mechanism from an independent group has been published.

**Evidence-tier ceiling (HGF/c-Met mechanism claim — evidence_tier D):** The independent-replication verdict above reflects an intrinsic literature ceiling, not a gap fixable by additional analysis. The foundational mechanism papers (Kawas 2012 [5], Benoist 2014 — both RETRACTED 2025 for data fabrication) and the key intermediate paper (McCoy 2013 [4], under Expression of Concern PMID 34551989) collectively constitute the entire experimental basis for the HGF/c-Met potentiation claim. The sole independent replication (Sun 2021 [7]) identified PI3K/AKT — not HGF/c-Met — as the operative pathway and contains no HGF/c-Met data. The result: the proposed HGF/c-Met mechanism has **zero non-retracted, non-WSU-group experimental support** in the post-2025 literature. This section's mechanistic-uncertainty language reflects a **evidence_tier D** ceiling on the HGF/c-Met claim specifically — not conservative framing, but an accurate description of what the non-retracted literature can and cannot support.

### C.4 The AngIV-analog / IRAP class context

Dihexa sits within a broader academic research field focused on angiotensin IV (AngIV) and its derivatives as procognitive agents. The parent compound, Nle1-AngIV (norleucine-substituted angiotensin IV), was identified in the late 1980s and early 1990s as a memory-enhancing peptide when injected intracerebroventricularly in rats. The field bifurcated mechanistically into two competing accounts.

**The IRAP (insulin-regulated aminopeptidase) account:** AngIV and structurally related peptides inhibit IRAP, a zinc metallopeptidase implicated in memory consolidation via GLUT4 vesicle trafficking and regulation of endogenous neuropeptides. This account — pursued primarily by groups at Uppsala University (Sweden) and elsewhere in Europe — treats AngIV analogs as IRAP inhibitors and has generated a substantial medicinal chemistry program producing macrocyclic peptidomimetics and small-molecule scaffolds [8, mechanism_review]. The IRAP mechanism does not implicate HGF or c-Met.

**The HGF/c-Met account:** The Harding/Wright WSU group proposed an alternative mechanism in which Nle1-AngIV and Dihexa bind directly to HGF and potentiate c-Met phosphorylation, driving synaptogenesis. This is the mechanistic claim that established Dihexa's differentiated identity — and the claim now resting on retracted papers.

The two mechanistic accounts are not mutually exclusive, and the field has not resolved which dominates. From a drug-class perspective, the AngIV-analog / IRAP research program is an academic investigation area without approved drugs, without completed human trials, and without regulatory recognition as a drug class. This is not an approved therapeutic modality; it is an active but contested research domain, with the specific HGF/c-Met branch now compromised by integrity findings.
