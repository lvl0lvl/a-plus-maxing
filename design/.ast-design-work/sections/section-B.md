# Section B: Reference Ranges, Units, and the De Ritis Ratio

## Units and Conventional Reference Ranges

AST activity is reported in **U/L** (units per litre), which is numerically identical to IU/L and the SI-compliant kU/L × 1000. Results from different laboratories are not interchangeable without assay harmonisation because AST lacks a commutable international reference material; method- and analyser-specific ULNs therefore vary [1, regulatory].

Conventional adult reference intervals reported by major clinical laboratories cluster around **17–40 U/L for women** and **17–50 U/L for men**, though published values differ modestly across assay platforms. A large cross-sectional analysis of more than 1.3 million consecutive blood samples confirmed significant sex dependence: the 95th-percentile for women younger than 50 is approximately 40 U/L, rising to ~45 U/L by age 60, while men's 95th percentile generally sits higher throughout adulthood before declining after age 60 [2, cohort]. The 2017 ACG Clinical Guideline uses 35 U/L (women) and 50 U/L (men) as working ULNs, but emphasises that local laboratory reference intervals take precedence [1, regulatory].

Unlike ALT, for which evidence-based lower thresholds (~19–25 U/L in healthy women; ~29–33 U/L in men) have been proposed after careful exclusion of metabolic confounders, AST has received less attention as a standalone marker and the case for revising its ULN downward is less robustly studied [1, regulatory].

**Age and sex dependence are real but modest for AST.** Reference intervals are method- and population-specific; direct comparison across laboratories without standardisation is unreliable [2, cohort].

## Elevation Bands (×ULN)

| Category | AST (×ULN) | Clinical implications |
|---|---|---|
| Borderline | < 2× | Isolated finding; recheck and investigate cause if persistent |
| Mild | 2–5× | Broad differential; include MASLD, ALD, drugs, thyroid, muscle |
| Moderate | 5–15× | Acute hepatocellular injury, more urgent evaluation warranted |
| Marked | > 15× | Acute viral hepatitis, ischaemic hepatitis, toxin, Wilson's; AST may reach thousands |
| Massive | > 10,000 U/L | Ischaemic hepatitis, acute acetaminophen toxicity |

These tiers are adapted from the ACG 2017 guideline categories (borderline <2×, mild 2–5×, moderate 5–15×, severe >15×), which were defined for ALT but are applied to AST in the same framework [1, regulatory]. At the marked-to-massive end, AST often rises faster and higher than ALT because of its mitochondrial isoform contribution and greater hepatic abundance; absolute levels above 1,000–3,000 U/L point strongly toward ischaemic, toxin, or acute viral aetiology rather than chronic liver disease [1, regulatory; 3, cohort].

## The AST:ALT Ratio — The De Ritis Ratio

The **De Ritis ratio** (AST÷ALT) is not a reference-range parameter but a **pattern interpretation tool** that substantially shifts the differential diagnosis when AST is elevated. It is load-bearing: the ratio must be calculated whenever AST is abnormal.

### Ratio > 2 — Strongly Suggests Alcoholic Liver Disease

A ratio consistently above **2** is the classic fingerprint of **alcoholic liver disease (ALD)**. This pattern reflects two converging mechanisms: (1) alcohol-induced mitochondrial damage preferentially releases mitochondrial AST; (2) chronic alcohol use depletes pyridoxal-5-phosphate (vitamin B₆), a cofactor required for ALT synthesis more than for AST, depressing ALT activity disproportionately.

The landmark study by Nyblom et al. (2004) examined three patient cohorts and found that a **high AST/ALT ratio indicates advanced alcoholic liver disease rather than simply heavy drinking** — patients with severe alcoholic hepatitis or cirrhosis drove the ratio elevation, not those with harmful alcohol use without significant hepatic injury [4, cohort]. This framing is clinically important: a ratio ≥ 2 does not merely confirm alcohol exposure; it flags *established hepatic injury* from alcohol. This was corroborated by Williams and Hoofnagle (1988), who noted in a series of 177 patients with chronic hepatitis that the AST/ALT ratio exceeds 2 in the great majority of ALD cases, whereas in non-alcoholic chronic hepatitis it typically stays below 1 [3, cohort].

### Ratio < 1 — Typical of MASLD/NAFLD and Acute Viral Hepatitis

When the ratio is below **1** — that is, ALT exceeds AST — the pattern favours **metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, formerly NAFLD)** or **acute viral hepatitis**. In MASLD, preserved hepatocyte mass with cytoplasmic rather than mitochondrial predominance keeps ALT activity disproportionately high. In acute viral hepatitis, cytoplasmic AST release is high but ALT release is proportionally higher still [3, cohort].

### Ratio > 1 in Chronic Liver Disease — Suggests Advancing Fibrosis or Cirrhosis

In a patient with established chronic liver disease of *any* aetiology, a ratio that has **risen above 1** should prompt reassessment for fibrosis progression. Williams and Hoofnagle demonstrated that among chronic hepatitis B patients, the mean ratio was **0.59 without cirrhosis versus 1.02 with cirrhosis** — a statistically significant separation [3, cohort]. Nyblom and colleagues extended this principle to **primary biliary cholangitis (PBC)**: an AST/ALT ratio ≥ 1 was a "strong predictor" of liver-related death or need for transplantation (nearly fourfold higher risk), and the ratio correlated with histological cirrhosis [5, cohort]; the same group found the ratio elevated above 1 in cirrhotic patients with **primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)** as well [6, cohort]. The mechanism is straightforward: as hepatocyte mass is destroyed by progressive fibrosis, residual ALT-producing hepatocytes are preferentially lost, while AST continues to be released from surviving cells and from non-hepatic sources.

**Practical summary of De Ritis thresholds:**

| Ratio | Pattern |
|---|---|
| < 1 | MASLD, acute viral hepatitis |
| 1–2 | Non-specific; cirrhosis possible; chronic hepatitis |
| > 2 (AST usually < 300 U/L) | ALD — advanced injury; also seen in advanced cirrhosis of any cause |
| > 2 (AST > 1,000 U/L) | Re-evaluate: likely ischaemic, acute hepatitis, or toxin even if ALD suspected |

## The Essential Co-Interpretation Requirement: CK

AST is expressed in **liver, skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, kidney, and red blood cells** — it is fundamentally non-tissue-specific. An isolated AST elevation without a concurrent ALT elevation should trigger immediate measurement of **creatine kinase (CK)**:

- **Elevated AST + elevated CK, normal ALT** → skeletal or cardiac muscle source (rhabdomyolysis, myopathy, strenuous exercise, myocardial infarction)
- **Elevated AST + normal CK, elevated ALT** → hepatocellular injury
- **Elevated AST + normal CK, normal ALT** → consider macro-AST (see below), haemolysis, or thyroid disease

Dufour (1988) formalised the **CK:AST ratio** as a discriminator between cardiac and skeletal muscle sources of elevated CK: because skeletal muscle contains more CK and proportionally less AST than cardiac muscle, lower CK:AST ratios (< 14 when total CK is 300–1,200 U/L) pointed toward myocardial infarction with 95% sensitivity [7, mechanism_review]. The practical clinical take-away is that **AST must never be interpreted in isolation from ALT and CK**; the trio together narrows the source differential far more reliably than any single enzyme alone.

---

## Macro-AST: A Benign Cause of Persistent Isolated AST Elevation

When AST is persistently elevated in isolation — that is, ALT, CK, and all other liver chemistries are normal, and no organ disease is identified — clinicians should consider **macro-AST** before pursuing invasive workup. Macro-AST arises when the AST enzyme forms high-molecular-weight complexes with immunoglobulins (most commonly IgG or IgA). These complexes are too large for renal filtration, so their plasma half-life is extended and measured AST activity is falsely and persistently elevated [3, cohort].

The **PEG (polyethylene glycol) precipitation test** is the standard confirmatory method: PEG is added to serum, precipitating immunoglobulin-bound AST; a post-precipitation AST recovery of ≤40% (equivalently, a drop of ≥60% from baseline) confirms macro-AST, while recovery >50% makes the diagnosis unlikely [8, mechanism_review]. The exact cutoff is method- and PEG-concentration-specific across laboratories, but the ≤40% recovery threshold is the most widely cited operational criterion. The test is simple, inexpensive, and should be reflexively ordered when other causes of isolated AST elevation have been excluded. Macro-AST is clinically benign and requires no treatment; its principal importance is preventing unnecessary hepatic biopsy or further workup.
