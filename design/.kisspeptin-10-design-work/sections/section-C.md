## Section C — Sexual/limbic effects, sexual-peptide class context & independent-replication assessment

> **Evidence-tier ceiling — read before interpreting any claim in this section.**
> The entire human evidence base linking kisspeptin to sexual/limbic effects and HSDD has five structural constraints that cap the maximum defensible evidence tier for sexual claims at **low / promising-but-unconfirmed**:
>
> 1. **Small-n.** Every human study has 29–37 participants per arm.
> 2. **Single-center.** All five RCTs were conducted exclusively at Imperial College London by the Comninos/Dhillo group. No other research site has run a kisspeptin sexual-brain study.
> 3. **Proof-of-concept design.** Studies are short, crossover fMRI trials and brief HSDD crossover RCTs — not powered for clinical efficacy endpoints.
> 4. **No independent replication.** Zero independently led groups have confirmed the central finding (peripheral kisspeptin enhances human sexual brain processing).
> 5. **Surrogate/mechanistic endpoints only.** Outcomes are brain-activation fMRI maps, penile tumescence, and psychometric questionnaires — not long-term clinical outcomes (sustained desire, relationship satisfaction, quality-of-life at ≥6 months).
>
> The studies are methodologically sound RCTs in tier-1 journals; the ceiling reflects the state of the field, not a flaw in any individual paper. Claims below are accurate within these constraints.

### C.1 The Dhillo/Imperial fMRI program: kisspeptin and the limbic/sexual brain

The clearest human evidence for kisspeptin's effect on sexual and emotional brain circuits comes from a sequenced program of double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover fMRI studies, all from Alexander Comninos and Waljit Dhillo at Imperial College London.

**The foundational healthy-volunteer study (2017).** Comninos et al. enrolled 29 healthy heterosexual men (mean age 25.0 years) in a randomized, double-blinded, 2-way crossover, placebo-controlled design. Intravenous kisspeptin enhanced activation of the anterior and posterior cingulate cortex and left amygdala in response to sexual images, and of the thalamus and globus pallidus in response to couple-bonding images [1, rct]. Crucially, the amygdala enhancement during bonding images correlated with improved positive mood, and enhanced limbic responses correlated with reduced sexual aversion on psychometric scales — the first evidence that a peripheral kisspeptin bolus reshapes limbic-sexual processing in humans. Published in *The Journal of Clinical Investigation*. PMID 28112678.

**Resting-state connectivity (2018).** Using the same n = 29 cohort in a follow-on analysis, Comninos et al. showed kisspeptin modulated default-mode network (DMN) and salience network resting-state connectivity, strengthening amygdala–cingulate and hippocampus–cingulate functional coupling [2, rct]. The degree of DMN modulation was largest in men with lower baseline reward-drive traits (r = −0.489, p = 0.008), and both DMN and salience-network shifts predicted reduced sexual aversion. This mechanistically links a single peripheral kisspeptin exposure to sustained changes in limbic network architecture at rest. Published in *JCI Insight*. PMID 30333302.

**Olfactory and visual attraction in healthy men (2020).** Yang et al. recruited 33 healthy heterosexual men (mean age 24.5 years) in a further randomized, double-blind, 2-way crossover, placebo-controlled fMRI trial [3, rct]. Kisspeptin significantly amplified activity in the amygdala and thalamus during exposure to an established feminine scent, and enhanced medial prefrontal cortex and superior frontal gyrus responses during facial-attractiveness evaluation across all attractiveness categories. Effects were largest in men reporting lower baseline sexual quality of life, suggesting kisspeptin may preferentially engage the system in individuals with sub-optimal baseline desire. Published in *JCI Insight* (2020). DOI 10.1172/jci.insight.133633.

### C.2 HSDD trials: moving from healthy volunteers to clinical populations

**Men with HSDD (2023).** Mills et al. conducted a double-blind, 2-way crossover, placebo-controlled RCT in 32 men (37 randomized) with physician-confirmed hypoactive sexual desire disorder, meeting between January and September 2021 [4, rct]. Kisspeptin significantly modulated brain activity in the sexual-processing network vs. placebo on whole-brain fMRI analysis, enhancing activation in the left middle frontal gyrus and anterior cingulate cortex. Penile tumescence — measured objectively — was 56% greater with kisspeptin than placebo at the 8-minute mark of a sexual video task (p = .02). Participants also reported increased "happiness about sex" (p = .02). All authors were affiliated with Imperial College London and Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust. Published in *JAMA Network Open* (2023). PMID 36735255.

**Women with HSDD (2022).** Thurston et al. ran a parallel double-masked, placebo-controlled, 2-way crossover RCT in 32 premenopausal women (40 randomized; mean age 29.2 years) with HSDD, conducted October 2020 to April 2021 [5, rct]. IV kisspeptin deactivated the left inferior and middle frontal gyri during erotic stimuli — regions hypothesized to be hyperactivated in HSDD — and activated the right postcentral and supramarginal gyrus. Enhanced hippocampal activity correlated with baseline sexual distress (r = 0.469, p = .007); posterior cingulate activation correlated with reduced sexual aversion (r = 0.476, p = .005). Self-reported sexiness increased (p = .04). No adverse effects were reported. All lead authors are affiliated with Imperial College London. Published in *JAMA Network Open* (2022). PMID 36287566.

**Synthesis of the HSDD evidence.** Across both trials (n = 32 men + 32 women with confirmed HSDD), kisspeptin: (a) consistently modulates activity in limbic and prefrontal sexual-processing regions vs. placebo; (b) reduces neural correlates of sexual aversion; (c) produces objective pro-erectile and psychometric improvements in men; and (d) is well-tolerated with IV infusion. Neither trial was powered to establish clinical efficacy; the authors explicitly call for longer-duration and oral/SC formulation studies. Kisspeptin does not have regulatory approval for HSDD or any sexual-health indication as of the current evidence date.

### C.3 Mechanistic position in the sexual-peptide class

Three peptide classes have been investigated for sexual/desire effects in humans. Their mechanisms are distinct and the evidence bases are non-interchangeable.

**Kisspeptin-10 (KISS1R agonist).** KP-10 acts upstream of the HPG axis: it binds KISS1R on GnRH neurons in the arcuate and anteroventral periventricular nuclei, driving pulsatile GnRH release and — indirectly — downstream LH/FSH and gonadal steroid production. The sexual/limbic effects documented in the studies above appear partly GnRH-independent: rodent models confirm that central kisspeptin directly stimulates amygdala circuits and lordosis behavior through nitric oxide signaling in the ventromedial hypothalamus without requiring downstream hormonal mediation [6, mechanism_review]. The human fMRI data are consistent with a dual pathway: HPG priming + direct limbic activation. This makes kisspeptin functionally the most upstream of the three, operating at the hypothalamic desire/drive level rather than the peripheral arousal level.

**PT-141 / bremelanotide (melanocortin MC4R/MC3R agonist; brand: Vyleesi).** Bremelanotide is a synthetic cyclic heptapeptide melanocortin receptor agonist (highest potency MC4R and MC3R) that does not act on KISS1R or GnRH neurons. MC4R activation in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus and medial preoptic area drives dopaminergic arousal via the nucleus accumbens/ventral tegmental area and enhances oxytocin secretion — a distinct central pathway from kisspeptin's GnRH-limbic axis. Its pivotal evidence base is two 24-week, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 trials in 1,247 premenopausal women with acquired, generalized HSDD (Kingsberg et al., 2019), which supported FDA approval in June 2019 as Vyleesi (1.75 mg SC self-injection on demand) [7, regulatory]. Bremelanotide is the ONLY peptide in this class with FDA approval for HSDD. Common adverse effects include nausea (40%), flushing (20%), and injection-site reactions (13%). Kisspeptin's approval status, trial scale, and administration route are all substantially less mature than bremelanotide's.

**Melanotan-II (MT-II) (non-selective melanocortin agonist).** MT-II is a non-selective agonist at MC1R, MC3R, and MC4R. The broader MC1R activation causes skin pigmentation (tanning) as an on-mechanism effect, not an adverse event. It produces sexual arousal effects mechanistically overlapping with PT-141 (MC4R), but with greater off-target pigmentation and cardiovascular risk. MT-II is not approved anywhere in the world; it circulates as an unregulated research chemical. The evidentiary quality is substantially below both kisspeptin (peer-reviewed fMRI RCTs) and bremelanotide (FDA-reviewed phase 3 program). Attribution discipline: the evidence for MT-II's sexual effects comes from small, often uncontrolled human reports and animal data — none of that evidence is attributable to kisspeptin or bremelanotide.

**Mechanistic summary.** KP-10 operates at the hypothalamic command level (HPG axis + direct limbic), acting upstream of desire itself; melanocortins (PT-141/MT-II) operate at a mid-level arousal node (dopaminergic/oxytocinergic); PDE5 inhibitors operate at the peripheral vascular level. The three systems are complementary rather than redundant, and their evidence bases must not be merged.

### C.4 Independent-replication assessment

**The concentration problem is real.** Every published human fMRI study linking kisspeptin to sexual or limbic brain processing, and both HSDD trials, originates from a single research group: Comninos and Dhillo at the Section of Endocrinology and Investigative Medicine, Imperial College London. The 2017 JCI, 2018 JCI Insight, 2020 JCI Insight, 2022 JAMA Network Open, and 2023 JAMA Network Open papers share lead authors across institutional affiliations limited to Imperial College London, Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust, Invicro/Hammersmith (an imaging CRO on the same campus), and Statsconsultancy Ltd. [1–5]. No independently led group has replicated the central claim — that peripherally administered kisspeptin enhances human sexual brain processing.

**What independent groups have contributed.** The Seminara/Crowley group at Harvard/MGH established the foundational discovery that KISS1R mutations cause idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (2003), and Seminara's lab has published on kisspeptin's role in GnRH pulse generation, including work in postmenopausal women (e.g., Jayasena et al., *JCEM*, 2014; Gill et al., *JCEM*, 2012). However, those studies focus on reproductive axis activation and LH pulsatility — not on sexual/limbic brain processing. Japanese groups (Oishi, Uenoyama, and colleagues at Nagoya University) and the KNDy field broadly have contributed to the mechanistic biology of kisspeptin neurons, again without replicating the human sexual-brain fMRI findings [6, mechanism_review]. The Frontiers in Endocrinology 2022 review of kisspeptin in behavior, while authored by Mills, Yang, Abbara, Dhillo, and Comninos (Imperial), acknowledges animal work from multiple international groups, but no independent human sexual-desire replication study is cited.

**Verdict.** The sexual/limbic evidence for kisspeptin in humans is promising but has a single-center provenance that constitutes a meaningful limitation. The two HSDD trials (n = 32 per arm, IV infusion, crossover design) are methodologically sound RCTs published in tier-1 journals, but they are not multicenter, and no independent group has run a confirmatory fMRI or desire-outcomes study. This is a standard early-phase trajectory for an emerging neuropeptide — not fraud or p-hacking — but it means the evidence cannot yet be considered independently replicated. Future credibility will require phase 2/3 multicenter trials and non-Imperial confirmation of the limbic fMRI signature.

**2-sentence summary.** In a series of five double-blind crossover RCTs (n = 29–33 healthy men; n = 32 men and 32 women with HSDD) all conducted at Imperial College London, IV kisspeptin consistently enhanced limbic/sexual-processing brain activity, reduced neural correlates of sexual aversion, increased penile tumescence (56% vs. placebo), and improved self-reported desire metrics, representing the most rigorous human evidence that a peripheral peptide can directly modulate central sexual-desire circuitry — but kisspeptin has no regulatory approval and differs mechanistically from the FDA-approved melanocortin MC4R agonist bremelanotide (Vyleesi), which acts via a dopaminergic/oxytocinergic arousal pathway rather than GnRH/limbic command. The independent-replication landscape is a significant limitation: every published human fMRI and HSDD efficacy finding for kisspeptin comes from a single group (Dhillo/Comninos, Imperial), with no multicenter confirmation and no independent sexual-brain-processing replication, placing this evidence at the promising-single-center-RCT stage rather than the independently-replicated stage.
