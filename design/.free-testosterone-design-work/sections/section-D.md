# Section D: Determinants & Clinical Significance

## D.1 The Core Value: Free T When SHBG Is Abnormal

Total testosterone circulates in three fractions: roughly ~2% unbound (free), ~44% tightly bound to sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), and ~50–54% loosely bound to albumin — the canonical reference-adult-male figures from Vermeulen et al. [6, mechanism_review]; depending on population and method, wider ranges (SHBG 30–44%, albumin 54–68%) appear in the literature. Because only the free and albumin-bound fractions are biologically accessible to target tissues, total testosterone can give a misleading picture whenever SHBG is abnormal. Free testosterone adds clinically meaningful information in precisely those situations — which are common.

**When SHBG is LOW** (obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, nephrotic syndrome, hypothyroidism, glucocorticoids, exogenous androgens): less testosterone is sequestered on the carrier protein, so a larger proportion circulates free. Total testosterone is pulled downward because much of what would have been SHBG-bound is absent, yet the free fraction may be preserved or only modestly reduced. This creates a systematic over-diagnosis risk. In a cohort of 150 obese men, 52% met standard criteria for testosterone deficiency by total T, but that figure fell to 17.6% when calculated free testosterone was used instead — the discrepancy driven by SHBG levels that tracked inversely with BMI [2, cohort]. Both insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and SHBG independently predicted free testosterone after multivariate adjustment, meaning the suppression of SHBG by insulin excess and the metabolic milieu act through partly distinct pathways [2, cohort]. In clinical practice, defaulting to total T alone in an obese or metabolically unwell man risks labelling functional hormone status as deficient and, potentially, triggering unnecessary testosterone therapy.

**When SHBG is HIGH** (aging, hyperthyroidism, estrogen/oral contraceptives, hepatic cirrhosis, HIV infection, anticonvulsants): more testosterone is locked onto the carrier, reducing the bioavailable fraction even as total T can appear normal or even high. In a single-centre retrospective cohort of HIV-infected men (n=94 complete-profile patients of 169 enrolled), roughly 36% had SHBG > 70 nmol/L; using total testosterone alone identified hypogonadism in 10.6% of patients, whereas adding calculated free testosterone increased that rate to 20.2% — an approximately two-fold (20.2% vs 10.6%) diagnostic gain [3, cohort]. SHBG rose with HIV infection duration, and compensatory increases in total T masked the progressive decline in free T until the compensation failed. This pattern generalises to any cause of elevated SHBG: total T looks normal, free T is quietly insufficient, and symptoms of androgen deficiency go unexplained if free T is never measured.

## D.2 Endocrine Society Measurement Guidance

The 2018 Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline on testosterone therapy in men (Bhasin et al.) specifies that when total testosterone is near the lower limit of normal, or when conditions known to alter SHBG are present, clinicians should obtain free testosterone by **equilibrium dialysis** or estimate it using a validated formula [4, regulatory]. The guideline explicitly cautions against the **analog immunoassay** (direct free T): this method provides values systematically discrepant from equilibrium dialysis — typically one-seventh the absolute concentration — and does not reliably reflect true free testosterone [5, mechanism_review]. Equilibrium dialysis remains the reference method; among calculated approaches, the Vermeulen equation (using total T and SHBG measured by immunoassay, with fixed albumin) demonstrates strong correlation with equilibrium dialysis (r ≈ 0.99 for calculated vs. r ≈ 0.97 for radioimmunoassay), though the calculated method consistently overestimates, and different approaches require their own reference ranges [5, mechanism_review; 6, mechanism_review].

## D.3 Women and PCOS

In women, SHBG is typically higher than in men, so the total testosterone/free testosterone discrepancy is proportionally larger and more diagnostically consequential. The **free androgen index** (FAI = total T [nmol/L] × 100 / SHBG [nmol/L]) and calculated free testosterone are the standard biochemical markers of hyperandrogenism in suspected polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), precisely because total testosterone alone is less sensitive for detecting androgen excess when SHBG varies.

A 2025 systematic review and diagnostic meta-analysis (13 studies, n = 2,182 for total T; 6 studies, n = 1,035 for cFT) found that calculated free testosterone achieved pooled sensitivity of **0.89** (95% CI 0.69–0.96) vs. 0.74 (0.63–0.82) for total testosterone in detecting biochemical hyperandrogenism in PCOS, at comparable specificity (0.83 vs. 0.86) [7, meta_analysis]. The AUC for the two methods was similar (0.85 vs. 0.87), but the near-10-point sensitivity advantage means free T catches substantially more cases of androgen excess that total T misses in women with normal or low-normal SHBG. Rotterdam consensus criteria include FAI > 4 as evidence of biochemical hyperandrogenism; the FAI is calculated from the same inputs as free T but reported as a ratio rather than a concentration.

## D.4 Determinants of Free Testosterone

Because free T is the unbound fraction of total T filtered through SHBG, its determinants are the union of total-T drivers and SHBG modulators:

**Raise free T:**
- Exogenous testosterone or anabolic androgens (raise total T, suppress SHBG, doubly increase free fraction)
- hCG stimulation (raises Leydig cell total T output)
- Conditions that suppress SHBG: obesity, insulin resistance, hypothyroidism, glucocorticoids, androgens themselves, growth hormone excess
- Higher LH pulse amplitude and frequency (intact HPG axis in young men)

**Lower free T:**
- Aging: both total T falls (declining Leydig cell mass and LH pulse amplitude) and SHBG rises, compressing the free fraction from both ends
- Opioids, glucocorticoids, chronic illness: suppress HPG axis → lower total T
- Anything raising SHBG (hyperthyroidism, estrogens, oral contraceptives, anticonvulsants, hepatic disease, HIV chronicity) → lower free T without necessarily lowering total T
- Central adiposity and insulin resistance in the context of already-low SHBG: this lowers total T as well, so the net effect on free T depends on the degree of each

## D.5 Clinical Associations: Bone, Function, and Mortality

Several large cohort studies show that free or bioavailable testosterone tracks outcomes more closely than total T when SHBG varies — which is exactly the population in whom it should.

**Bone density and fracture:** In MrOS Sweden (n = 2,908 men, mean age 75.4 years), free testosterone below the median was an independent predictor of prevalent osteoporosis-related fractures (OR 1.56; 95% CI 1.14–2.14; p < 0.01) and was significantly associated with cortical bone mineral density at the hip, arm, and total body — even after adjustment for BMD itself [8, cohort]. A smaller cross-sectional study of 83 community-dwelling men aged > 65 years with low bioavailable testosterone found bioavailable T the strongest single predictor of femoral neck BMD, explaining approximately 21% of variance, exceeding contributions of physical activity and BMI [9, cohort].

**Physical function and frailty:** In the Framingham Offspring Study (n = 1,445 men, mean age 61 years), each standard deviation increase in baseline free testosterone was associated with a 22% lower risk of incident mobility limitation and a 25% lower risk of worsening limitation over follow-up [10, cohort]. Free testosterone outperformed total testosterone as a predictor of physical decline, which the investigators attributed to its reflecting biologically active androgen rather than SHBG-bound hormone.

These associations are observational: they establish that free T tracks clinically relevant outcomes in aging men, but they cannot establish that the low free T is causing the adverse outcome rather than tracking an underlying disease state. Causal inference requires intervention data, and no large RCT has specifically randomised by free T level.

## D.6 Limitations

**Method dependence.** Equilibrium dialysis, calculated free T (Vermeulen), and the analog immunoassay return discordant absolute values and cannot share a single reference range. The analog assay systematically underestimates compared to dialysis; the calculated method systematically overestimates; and different calculated formulas (Vermeulen, Sodergard, linear approximations) diverge in edge-population distributions [5, mechanism_review; 6, mechanism_review].

**No harmonised reference range.** Unlike total testosterone, no single population-anchored reference range for free T has been established across methods, laboratories, or age/sex strata. Endocrine Society and other regulatory bodies note the absence of this infrastructure as a gap that limits clinical application [4, regulatory; 6, mechanism_review].

**Error propagation.** Calculated free T inherits analytical error from both total T and SHBG assays; imprecision in either input compounds in the calculation. At low concentrations — where the clinical question is often most pressing — percentage error is largest [6, mechanism_review].

**Analog immunoassay persistence.** Despite its documented unreliability, the direct analog assay remains widely ordered because it is cheap and rapid. Its results should not be used for diagnostic decision-making in the conditions where free T matters most (low and high SHBG extremes), though some clinical labs have moved toward LC-MS/MS-based equilibrium dialysis as a reference-grade alternative.
