## Section C — Exercise, aging & longevity evidence

### C.1 Exercise-induced endogenous MOTS-c in humans

MOTS-c is measurably responsive to acute physical exercise in healthy humans. Reynolds et al. 2021 (n=10 healthy young men, mean age 24.5 ± 3.7 years) had participants perform an acute bout of stationary cycling and measured MOTS-c in skeletal muscle biopsies and plasma. Skeletal muscle MOTS-c increased approximately 11.9-fold immediately post-exercise; circulating (plasma) MOTS-c rose approximately 1.6-fold (from ~125 pg/mL to ~190 pg/mL). Both elevations were sustained at 4 hours post-exercise (muscle ~18.9-fold, plasma ~1.5-fold), suggesting the peptide lingers beyond the exercise bout [1, cohort].

A 16-week supervised aerobic and resistance exercise intervention (3 sessions/week, 65–80% max HR) in 49 overweight/obese breast cancer survivors (24 non-Hispanic White, 25 Hispanic; Stage I–III) found a significant increase in circulating MOTS-c among non-Hispanic White participants (p<0.01), with post-exercise MOTS-c levels correlating with reductions in fat mass, body weight, HOMA-IR, and CRP. No significant change was observed in Hispanic participants (p>0.05) [2, open_label]. This ethnic divergence is unexplained and has not yet been replicated; it should not be overinterpreted.

Whether chronic endurance training shifts RESTING MOTS-c is unsettled: one cross-sectional study (75 professional athletes vs. 30 sedentary controls) reported lower resting-state levels in high-endurance athletes [3, anecdote_aggregate] — but this is a lower-tier, non-whitelisted source, and other reports describe the opposite (higher circulating MOTS-c with endurance training), so the chronic-adaptation direction cannot be stated with confidence. The acute-exercise elevation (above, [1]) is the better-established finding; the acute-vs-chronic distinction matters for any clinical interpretation.

**Summary of human exercise data:** MOTS-c rises acutely with exercise in skeletal muscle and plasma. Chronic high-volume training is associated with lower basal levels. All human data are observational; no intervention study has administered exogenous MOTS-c to humans in an exercise context.

### C.2 Physical capacity and aging: mouse treatment studies

The most cited exogenous MOTS-c treatment data comes from Reynolds et al. 2021, conducted in C57BL/6N male mice at three ages: young (2 months), middle-aged (12 months), and old (22 months). MOTS-c was administered at 15 mg/kg/day for 2 weeks (daily intraperitoneal injection). Treated mice across all age groups showed significantly improved treadmill running performance. In aged mice (22 months; n not individually specified per group in the abstract but consistent with n=6–12 in the paper's figures), MOTS-c treatment approximately doubled total running work output, and treated old mice outperformed untreated middle-aged mice on running capacity. Grip strength and gait metrics also improved in the old-treated group [1, animal].

Additionally, Reynolds et al. initiated a late-life MOTS-c regimen at 23.5 months (3x/week, intermittent) and reported increased physical capacity and improved healthspan markers in treated aged mice versus vehicle controls. No lifespan extension (survival curve to natural death) was reported in this paper [1, animal].

The original MOTS-c discovery paper (Lee et al. 2015) established the foundational metabolic mechanism in mice (C57BL/6 male, n=6–8 per group): 7-day MOTS-c injection (15 mg/kg/day) prevented diet-induced obesity and improved insulin sensitivity in young (3-month) and middle-aged (12-month) mice, working via AMPK activation in skeletal muscle. No running-capacity or aging outcome was measured in that study [4, animal].

**Population note:** All exogenous MOTS-c treatment data showing physical performance benefits are from rodents (mouse, C57BL/6N). No human exogenous MOTS-c administration study for exercise or physical performance has been published.

### C.3 Aging, mitochondrial decline, and MOTS-c

Endogenous MOTS-c declines with age in both rodents and humans. Reynolds et al. 2021 document that skeletal muscle MOTS-c is reduced in aged (22-month) vs. young (2-month) mice, paralleling the age-dependent reduction in physical capacity — framing MOTS-c loss as a possible contributor to sarcopenia and functional decline [1, animal]. This aligns with the broader literature on mitochondrial-derived peptide (MDP) decline in aging tissues.

Burtscher et al. 2023 (Aging Cell) reviewed mitokine biology across aging, noting that multiple mitochondrial stress signals — including MOTS-c — show age-dependent dysregulation and that the exercise–mitokine axis may represent a mechanistic link between physical activity and healthspan. This is a mechanism review summarizing preclinical evidence; no direct intervention data in humans [5, mechanism_review].

### C.4 Longevity genetics: m.1382A>C and the Fuku 2015 hypothesis

Fuku et al. 2015 (Aging Cell, PMID 26289118) PROPOSED and HYPOTHESIZED that the mitochondrial DNA polymorphism m.1382A>C — which produces a Lys14Gln substitution in MOTS-c and is specific to Northeast Asian populations — may contribute to exceptional longevity (EL) rates in Japanese centenarians. This was a **hypothesis/correspondence paper**, not an original cohort study with new data collection. The authors reviewed existing evidence linking the mtDNA haplogroup D4b2 (which contains m.1382A>C) to EL in a small initial Japanese cohort (original suggestion based on n≈96 centenarians) and proposed MOTS-c as the mechanistic link [6, mechanism_review].

Zempo et al. 2021 (Aging, PMID 33468709) formally tested this hypothesis in a large multi-cohort study (n=27,527; three independent cohorts: J-MICC n=11,852, MEC n=3,387, TMM n=12,288) with a dedicated centenarian sub-group of 736 Japanese individuals. The expanded centenarian analysis found **no significant difference** in m.1382A>C allele frequency between centenarians and controls, directly refuting the Fuku longevity association. Instead, Zempo et al. found that the C-allele (K14Q MOTS-c variant) is associated with significantly elevated type 2 diabetes risk in sedentary males (not females), with mouse experiments confirming the K14Q peptide has reduced insulin-sensitizing potency compared to wild-type MOTS-c [7, cohort]. (MOTS-c's WADA status — prohibited as an AMPK-activator metabolic modulator — is covered authoritatively in §D.)

**Status of the longevity-genetics claim:** The Fuku 2015 hypothesis is not supported by the best available evidence. The Zempo 2021 large-cohort refutation represents the current state of the literature. MOTS-c's genetic contribution to exceptional human longevity is unestablished.

### C.5 The "exercise mimetic" framing: evidence vs. marketing

The label "exercise mimetic" applied to MOTS-c is a consumer/media extrapolation from two distinct bodies of evidence that should not be conflated:

1. **Mouse treatment data:** Exogenous MOTS-c (injected, 15 mg/kg/day) improves running capacity, grip strength, and AMPK-mediated metabolism in aged mice. This is the only controlled intervention evidence that MOTS-c improves physical performance [1, animal].

2. **Human biomarker data:** Endogenous MOTS-c rises in human skeletal muscle and plasma after acute exercise. This makes MOTS-c a marker that tracks exercise — not an agent that substitutes for it [1, cohort; 2, open_label].

The logical gap between these two facts is large: that a peptide rises during exercise does not establish that administering it externally replicates the exercise stimulus. No human RCT or open-label trial has administered exogenous MOTS-c and measured physical performance or healthspan outcomes. The only human pharmaceutical program (CohBar's CB4211, a MOTS-c analog) targeted NASH/obesity and was discontinued before Phase 2. Claims that MOTS-c "acts like exercise" or "gives the benefits of exercise without working out" in humans are not supported by peer-reviewed data and constitute marketing overreach.

**Translational gap (explicit):** As of 2026, no published study has administered exogenous MOTS-c to humans and measured exercise capacity, muscle function, healthspan, or longevity outcomes. The exercise/longevity benefit profile is established only in rodents receiving pharmacological doses (15 mg/kg/day IP injection).
