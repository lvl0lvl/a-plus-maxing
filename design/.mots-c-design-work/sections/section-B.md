## Section B — Metabolic & insulin-sensitivity evidence (human vs animal)

### Key framing

All evidence for **exogenous MOTS-c as a therapeutic** derives from rodent (mouse) studies; no published peer-reviewed RCT of exogenous MOTS-c administration in humans exists as of mid-2026 (one Phase 2a trial is recruiting, NCT07505745). Human evidence is exclusively **observational/cohort**, treating endogenous circulating MOTS-c as a **biomarker** — associations do not establish causation and do not license inference about the effects of exogenous administration.

---

### Rodent metabolic evidence (exogenous MOTS-c as therapeutic)

The foundational rodent characterisation was published by Lee et al. 2015 [1, animal] in Cell Metabolism. The study ran six distinct mouse experiments across two strains:

**Experiment 1 (glucose tolerance).** Male C57BL/6 mice (n=7/group) received intraperitoneal MOTS-c 5 mg/kg/day for 7 days. Glucose clearance was significantly enhanced relative to vehicle-injected controls, interpreted as improved insulin sensitivity.

**Experiment 2 (hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp).** Male C57BL/6 mice on a 60%-fat diet (n=6–8/group) received MOTS-c 5 mg/kg/day IP for 7 days. The exogenous glucose infusion rate required to maintain euglycemia increased ~30%, driven by enhanced insulin-stimulated glucose disposal in skeletal muscle (elevated Akt phosphorylation, increased GLUT4 expression). Hepatic glucose production was not significantly altered, indicating the primary site of action is skeletal muscle, not liver.

**Experiment 3 (obesity prevention).** Male CD-1 mice, 8 weeks old, fed a 60%-calorie-fat diet (n=10/group) received MOTS-c 0.5 mg/kg/day IP for 8 weeks. MOTS-c-treated mice showed significantly lower body weight and fat mass versus controls with identical caloric intake. Hepatic lipid accumulation was "dramatically reduced." AMPK was activated in skeletal muscle; hyperinsulinemia was prevented. Respiratory exchange ratio and heat production were elevated, consistent with increased fat oxidation.

**Experiment 4 (age-related insulin resistance).** Male C57BL/6 mice (young: 3 months, middle-aged: 12 months; n=6/group) received MOTS-c 5 mg/kg/day IP for 7 days. Insulin-stimulated glucose uptake in aged animals was restored to levels comparable to young controls.

**Proposed mechanism:** MOTS-c inhibits the folate cycle and de novo purine biosynthesis in skeletal muscle, driving AMPK activation → GLUT4 translocation → enhanced glucose disposal [1, animal].

A follow-up metabolomics study by Kim et al. 2019 [2, animal] in Physiological Reports extended these findings using male C57BL/6J mice, 17 weeks old, on a high-fat diet (n=7/group for injections; n=6/group for metabolomics). MOTS-c was administered by intraperitoneal injection at 2.5 mg/kg twice daily for three days. MOTS-c treatment reduced three metabolic pathways upregulated in obesity and T2DM models: sphingolipid metabolism, monoacylglycerol metabolism, and dicarboxylate metabolism. Blood glucose was significantly lower after three days of MOTS-c treatment. The study reinforces that MOTS-c improves insulin sensitivity in diet-induced obese mice via lipidomic remodeling rather than direct glucose-transporter regulation alone [2, animal].

**Summary of exogenous-therapeutic evidence:** all statistically significant metabolic effects of MOTS-c administration are from male mice (CD-1 and C57BL/6 strains); the largest experiments used n=10. No female rodent data, no primate data, and no published human therapeutic trial.

### Human circulating-MOTS-c evidence (endogenous peptide as biomarker)

#### Type 2 diabetes

Ramanjaneya et al. 2019 [3, cohort] (Frontiers in Endocrinology; n=225 total: 68 controls, 33 prediabetes, 31 well-controlled T2DM, 93 poorly-controlled T2DM, Qatari adults) found serum MOTS-c significantly lower in T2DM versus healthy controls (p=0.007). Mean MOTS-c was 235 ± 182 pg/mL in controls vs. 158 ± 137 pg/mL in poorly-controlled T2DM (−27%). MOTS-c negatively correlated with HbA1c (r=−0.233, p<0.001) and fasting glucose. Association with insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) did not survive multivariate adjustment independently. The study documents association only; reverse causation (lower MOTS-c being a consequence of metabolic dysfunction) cannot be excluded.

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis by Zhou et al. [4, meta_analysis] (Diabetology & Metabolic Syndrome; 7 studies, 11 comparison groups, 602 participants) quantified MOTS-c differences across metabolic states. In T2DM vs. controls: MOTS-c was significantly reduced (SMD=−0.89; 95% CI −1.12 to −0.65; p<0.05). In obesity: the direction depended on BMI threshold — at BMI >28 kg/m², MOTS-c was significantly elevated (SMD=+0.51; 95% CI 0.21 to 0.81; p<0.05), whereas when overweight participants (BMI 24–28) were included, no significant change emerged. No significant correlation with HOMA-IR or BMI was detected in pooled correlational analysis (contradicting several individual studies). The authors propose a biphasic model: MOTS-c secretion rises early in metabolic imbalance (obesity) but falls as obesity progresses to overt T2DM [4, meta_analysis].

#### Obesity and insulin resistance

Du et al. 2018 [5, cohort] (Pediatric Diabetes; n=97: 40 obese children/adolescents [27 male, 13 female] vs. 57 controls [40 male, 17 female], Hubei Province, China) found circulating MOTS-c significantly lower in obese males (465 ± 25 vs. 584 ± 21 ng/mL; p<0.001) but not in females. In the male cohort, MOTS-c negatively correlated with BMI-SDS, waist circumference, fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, and HbA1c. This represents endogenous MOTS-c as a biomarker of insulin resistance in pediatric obesity; sex-specific findings require replication. Exogenous peptide was not administered.

Yoon et al. 2025 [6, cohort] (Journal of Clinical Translational Endocrinology; n=54: 22 lean vs. 32 obese adults; plus 10 obese patients followed for 6 months post-bariatric surgery) found circulating MOTS-c paradoxically higher in obese vs. lean individuals (273 ± 56 vs. 223 ± 50 pg/mL; p<0.01). BMI and HOMA-IR independently predicted elevated MOTS-c, with a biphasic relationship: MOTS-c rose sharply above HOMA-IR ~6.6. After bariatric surgery with significant BMI reduction, MOTS-c remained unchanged (p=0.913), suggesting circulating levels are not rapidly normalized by weight loss. This finding directly contradicts a simple "biomarker of health" narrative and supports a compensatory secretion model in established obesity [6, cohort].

Yildiz Ozkaya et al. 2025 [7, cohort] (Archives of Endocrinology and Metabolism; n=85: 48 obese, 37 normal-BMI adults) found no significant difference in serum MOTS-c between obese and normal-weight groups (14.33 ± 3.76 vs. 13.67 ± 3.44 pg/mL; p=0.395). MOTS-c positively correlated with HOMA-IR (p<0.05) and decreased with age (~0.078 ng/mL per year). No significant association with high-sensitivity CRP or asymmetric dimethylarginine (endothelial dysfunction marker) was detected; MOTS-c did not distinguish individuals by inflammatory or endothelial status in this cohort [7, cohort].

#### Endothelial function

Qin et al. 2018 [8, cohort] (International Journal of Cardiology; n=40 adults undergoing coronary angiography, divided by coronary blood flow response to intracoronary acetylcholine into normal endothelial function vs. coronary endothelial dysfunction) found plasma MOTS-c lower in patients with endothelial dysfunction (p=0.007). MOTS-c positively correlated with microvascular (p=0.01) and epicardial (p=0.02) coronary endothelial function. This is an observational association (n=40); mechanism and causality unestablished [8, cohort].

#### Liver fat (NAFLD)

Sequeira et al. 2021 [9, cohort] (Biochim Biophys Acta Gen Subj; n=125 Chinese adults [91M, 34F] with subset undergoing MRI/S for organ fat) found plasma MOTS-c positively associated with android fat distribution (p<0.004) and hepatic fat content after controlling for total body fat — the opposite direction from a protective biomarker. The authors interpret this as stress-induced mitochondrial signaling from metabolically challenged hepatocytes. This is cross-sectional; MOTS-c-liver-fat directionality cannot be established [9, cohort].

---

### Human intervention data (exogenous administration)

**None published.** No peer-reviewed RCT or open-label trial of exogenous MOTS-c administration in humans had been published as of mid-2026. A Phase 2a double-blind placebo-controlled trial (NCT07505745; subcutaneous MOTS-c, 12 weeks, adults with prediabetes and overweight/obesity, primary endpoint: Matsuda Index from OGTT) was registered and reportedly recruiting in 2025–2026; no results are publicly available. Effect sizes for exogenous MOTS-c in humans remain unknown [10, regulatory].

### Synthesis: biomarker vs. therapeutic — what is actually established

| Domain | Evidence type | Species / n | Direction | Certainty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exogenous MOTS-c → insulin sensitivity | animal | Mice (C57BL/6, CD-1); n≤10 | Improves glucose disposal; AMPK/GLUT4 in muscle | Preclinical only |
| Exogenous MOTS-c → obesity prevention | animal | Male CD-1 mice; n=10 | Prevents HFD-induced obesity | Preclinical only |
| Endogenous MOTS-c in T2DM | cohort | ~600 humans (pooled meta-analysis) | Lower vs. controls; SMD=−0.89 | Associative, not causal |
| Endogenous MOTS-c in obesity | cohort | ~600 humans (pooled); single studies n=54–125 | Mixed: higher in established obesity (compensatory?); lower in pediatric obesity | Conflicting; biphasic model proposed |
| Endogenous MOTS-c in coronary endothelial dysfunction | cohort | n=40 | Lower in endothelial dysfunction | Small, single study |
| Exogenous MOTS-c in humans | — | None | Unknown | No data |
