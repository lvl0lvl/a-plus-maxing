## Section A — Identity, discovery & molecular mechanism

### A.1 Identity and amino acid sequence

MOTS-c (mitochondrial open reading frame of the 12S rRNA type-c) is a 16-amino-acid mitochondrial-derived peptide (MDP) with the sequence **MRWQEMGYIFYPRKLR** [1, animal]. It is encoded by a 51-base-pair short open reading frame (sORF) embedded within MT-RNR1, the mitochondrial gene encoding the 12S ribosomal RNA subunit [1, animal]. Molecular weight: ~2174.6 Da; formula C₁₀₁H₁₅₂N₂₈O₂₂S₂. The sequence is conserved across mammalian species, consistent with functional constraint.

MOTS-c is distinct from humanin, the best-studied MDP: humanin is encoded by MT-RNR2 (the 16S rRNA gene) and acts primarily through known cell-surface receptors on hypothalamic and islet cells, while MOTS-c directly targets skeletal muscle as its primary site of action and, uniquely among MDPs, can undergo intracellular nuclear translocation under metabolic stress [1, animal].

### A.2 Translation: cytoplasmic, not mitochondrial

MOTS-c presents an unusual translation paradox. Using the standard mitochondrial genetic code, the sORF would produce tandem start/stop codons and be untranslatable within the mitochondrial matrix. Translation therefore occurs obligatorily in the cytoplasm using the standard genetic code, implying that the MOTS-c polyadenylated transcript is exported from the mitochondria before being translated on cytoplasmic ribosomes [1, animal]. The export mechanism remains uncharacterized; selective depletion of mitochondrial RNA causes loss of cytoplasmic MOTS-c protein over time, confirming mitochondrial origin of the transcript [1, animal]. This cytoplasmic-translation-from-a-mitochondrial-transcript model is the current consensus, not yet challenged by an experimentally confirmed alternative.

### A.3 Discovery: Lee et al. 2015

MOTS-c was identified by Lee, Cohen, and colleagues in a landmark 2015 Cell Metabolism paper [1, animal]. The authors performed an in-silico scan of the mitochondrial genome for sORFs with strong Kozak consensus sequences, identified the 51-bp sORF within MT-RNR1, synthesized the peptide, and characterized it in multiple cell lines and in mouse metabolic models. Key in-vitro models: HEK293 (human embryonic kidney), HeLa and HeLa-ρ⁰ cells (mitochondrial-DNA-depleted), L6 rat myoblasts differentiated into myotubes. Key in-vivo models: CD-1 outbred mice (n = 10 per group, high-fat diet); C57BL/6 mice (n = 6–8, glucose clamp studies). The founding paper established MOTS-c as circulating and active in plasma, with primary metabolic action on skeletal muscle [1, animal].

### A.4 Molecular mechanism

#### A.4.1 Folate–methionine cycle inhibition and AICAR accumulation (primary proposed mechanism)

The primary mechanistic axis proposed by Lee et al. 2015 operates as follows [1, animal]:

1. MOTS-c depletes intracellular 5-methyl-tetrahydrofolate (5Me-THF), disrupting the folate one-carbon pool.
2. Depletion of 5Me-THF blocks de-novo purine biosynthesis, causing accumulation of the intermediate AICAR (5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide) — measured at >20-fold increase in MOTS-c-treated cells vs. controls.
3. AICAR is a well-characterized endogenous AMPK activator; the resulting AMPK phosphorylation occurs independently of changes in the canonical AMP/ATP ratio (similar to the pharmacological action of salicylate, leptin, or AICAR itself).

This entire mechanistic chain was established in cell lines (HEK293, L6 myotubes) and mouse skeletal muscle. It has **not** been directly demonstrated in humans. The folate–AICAR pathway claim is therefore categorized as in-vitro / animal evidence.

#### A.4.2 AMPK activation

AMPK activation downstream of AICAR accumulation was confirmed by Western blot for phospho-AMPK in vitro, and by metabolic phenotyping (improved glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity) in mouse models [1, animal]. The AMPK dependence was further confirmed in the 2018 Kim et al. study, where AMPK inhibition (compound C) blocked MOTS-c nuclear translocation [2, in_vitro].

#### A.4.3 Mitochondrial-to-nuclear retrograde signaling

A second mechanistic layer was described by Kim, Son, Benayoun & Lee (2018, Cell Metabolism) [2, in_vitro]. Under metabolic stress stimuli — glucose restriction, serum deprivation, or oxidative stress — MOTS-c translocates from cytoplasm to nucleus within 30 minutes (demonstrated in HEK293 and C2C12 cell lines). Once nuclear, MOTS-c directly regulates antioxidant response element (ARE)-containing genes, acting in concert with NRF2/Nrf2-linked stress-response pathways to modulate oxidative defense gene expression [2, in_vitro]. Nuclear translocation is AMPK-dependent: pharmacological AMPK inhibition prevents nuclear entry [2, in_vitro]. This positions MOTS-c as a retrograde mitochondrial-to-nuclear signaling molecule — a role not described for any prior MDP.

**Evidence classification for nuclear-ARE signaling:** all mechanistic evidence is from cell culture (in vitro). Translocation to the nucleus under in-vivo metabolic stress conditions in animal or human tissue has not been directly imaged.

#### A.4.4 Exercise induction and aging context

Reynolds et al. (2021, Nature Communications) showed that MOTS-c is induced by exercise in human skeletal muscle and circulation, and that exogenous MOTS-c administration improved physical capacity and muscle homeostasis in young (2-month), middle-aged (12-month), and aged (22-month) mice [3, animal]. Human exercise data (circulating MOTS-c levels pre/post exercise) were reported in a cross-sectional cohort, but mechanistic human data remain limited. This paper did not establish the folate/AICAR/AMPK chain in humans — it showed the upstream induction signal (exercise) and downstream functional outputs (physical capacity) in mouse models.

### A.5 The m.1382A>C SNP (Lys14Gln variant)

Fuku et al. (2015, Aging Cell) identified the m.1382A>C polymorphism (rs111033358), which lies within the MOTS-c-coding region of MT-RNR1 [4, mechanism_review]. This variant causes a **Lys14Gln amino acid substitution** (K14Q) in position 14 of the 16-amino-acid peptide (Grantham score 53, indicating physicochemical consequence). The SNP is part of the mtDNA D4b2 haplogroup, which is enriched in Northeast Asian (particularly Japanese) populations. Fuku et al. proposed — citing a reported association in Japanese centenarian data — that the m.1382A>C polymorphism may contribute to exceptional longevity, and that MOTS-c acts as a "mitokine" — an endocrine-like mitochondrial signal — with this variant potentially accounting in part for the unusual longevity phenotype in this population [4, mechanism_review]. The paper is a correspondence/letter-length report; the association is exploratory, not a powered prospective study.

A subsequent larger analysis (Zempo et al. 2021, Aging [Albany NY]; meta-analysis of three Japanese/Japanese-American cohorts, n = 27,527) found a contrasting result: the m.1382A>C C-allele was associated with a **65% higher prevalence of type 2 diabetes in sedentary males** but not females [5, cohort]. Mechanistic follow-up in C2C12 myotubes, L6 myotubes, 3T3-L1 pre-adipocytes, and HEK293 cells showed that the K14Q variant peptide has reduced positive charge and increased hydrophobicity (PROVEAN score –4.0, deleterious), and that K14Q-MOTS-c fails to produce the insulin-sensitizing effects of wild-type MOTS-c in vitro [5, cohort]. CD-1 mice (both sexes) on a high-fat diet confirmed that K14Q-MOTS-c does not reduce weight or improve glucose tolerance at doses (7.5 mg/kg twice daily; 0.5 mg/kg daily) that are effective for WT MOTS-c [5, cohort]. Together, the Fuku (longevity association) and Zempo (diabetes risk) findings are not necessarily contradictory: the longevity association may operate through sex-specific, exercise-interacting, or haplogroup-context-dependent effects not captured by either study alone. Neither finding is yet replicated in an independent large-scale prospective cohort.
