## Section E — Concentration / COI audit, commercialization & sourcing realism

Every major discovery and mechanistic characterisation paper from the peptide's identification through 2021 originates from a single laboratory group: the **Pinchas Cohen / Changhan David Lee group at the USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology**.

The first independent primary research appears only circa 2023 (P5–P7), and all three are animal models following the mechanistic scaffold established by Cohen/Lee. Review articles from Chinese and other institutions (Gao et al. Metabolites 2023 [8, mechanism_review]; Wan et al. **Concentration finding (first-class flag):** The ≥70% single-group threshold is substantially exceeded. The Cohen/Lee group holds the entire foundational primary-discovery literature; independent corroborating efficacy data in any species beyond the downstream animal work of 2023–2025 does not exist. This is among the highest single-group concentrations observed for any peptide entering popular use.

### E.2 Conflict of interest: the USC–CohBar nexus

**CohBar Inc.** is a biotechnology company co-founded by Pinchas Cohen and incorporated around the intellectual property of mitochondrial-derived peptides (MDPs). The foundational MOTS-c intellectual property was developed at UCLA and licensed exclusively to CohBar under an agreement between The Regents of the University of California and CohBar dated August 6, 2013 [10, regulatory].

- **Cohen** is a co-founder, stockholder, and board member of CohBar [10].
- **Lee** is disclosed as a consultant and shareholder of CohBar in both the 2018 Cell Metabolism paper (P3 above) and the 2021 Nature Communications paper (P4 above); these disclosures appear directly in the papers' COI statements [3, 4].
- **U.S. Patent 11,111,271** (granted September 2021) covers CB4211 compositions and methods of treatment for NASH; CohBar is assignee [10].

The result is that the two scientists whose laboratory produced all foundational primary evidence for MOTS-c were simultaneously financial stakeholders in the principal commercial vehicle attempting to monetise it. This COI is disclosed in the relevant publications but must be surfaced as a first-class caveat when interpreting the literature's strength.

### E.3 Native peptide vs engineered clinical analog: CB4211 ≠ consumer MOTS-c

**CB4211** is not the native 16-amino-acid MOTS-c peptide. CohBar describes it as "a novel and improved analog of MOTS-c, a naturally occurring mitochondrial derived peptide (MDP)" (U.S. Patent 11,111,271) [10]. CB4211 is a stabilised, chemically modified analog designed to improve the pharmacokinetics and stability deficiencies of the native sequence — modifications that are proprietary and covered by the patent.

**The clinical trial (NCT03998514):** Phase 1a/1b, double-blind, placebo-controlled evaluation of CB4211 25 mg/day by subcutaneous injection × 4 weeks in 20 obese subjects with NAFLD/NASH. Primary endpoint: safety and tolerability — **met** (no serious adverse events; transient mild-to-moderate injection site reactions in >10% of CB4211 subjects). CohBar reported exploratory secondary signals in a company press release: ALT −21% vs −4% placebo; AST −28% vs −11% placebo; fasting glucose −6% vs 0% placebo; body weight showed a numerical trend without statistical significance; liver fat (MRI-PDFF) declined in both arms [11, open_label]. **These figures are company-reported (press release, no peer-reviewed publication); ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03998514 lists the trial as completed but shows no posted results data.**

These results were announced August 10, 2021, and represent the **only human efficacy signal in the MOTS-c/MDP class**. They are for CB4211 (the analog), not the native peptide sold commercially. The Phase 1b formulation was subsequently deemed unsuitable for further development; the CB4211 program was discontinued in 2023 (see E.4).

**What consumer MOTS-c is:** grey-market vendors sell synthetic replicates of the native 16-amino-acid sequence — the unmodified peptide. The Phase 1 human data for CB4211 does not translate to the native peptide; stability, bioavailability, and receptor engagement may differ. No human trial of native MOTS-c has been conducted or registered.

### E.4 CohBar's fate: dissolution 2023

Following the Phase 1a/1b results, CohBar announced a proposed reverse merger with Morphogenesis Inc. (May 2023) to pivot toward an immuno-oncology pipeline and rename the combined entity TuHURA Biosciences [10]. On October 30–31, 2023, Nasdaq issued oral guidance that the proposed transaction structure would not receive listing approval; CohBar's board determined no viable path forward existed and resolved to commence liquidation and dissolution [10]. The CB4211 program has no active corporate sponsor as of 2023 forward; no Phase 2 trial has been initiated.

The only clinical program for any MOTS-c-class molecule has ended in program dissolution.

### E.5 Sourcing realism: consumer access

Consumer MOTS-c is available only through research-chemical / grey-market channels [12, anecdote_aggregate]. As of 2026 there is no FDA-approved product and no approved 503A bulk-substance compounding pathway — the FDA Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee was scheduled to evaluate MOTS-c for potential 503A inclusion at its July 2026 meeting, with no approval granted as of this writing (the FDA 503A status is regulatory-sourced in §D).

Grey-market vendors sell MOTS-c labelled "for research use only / not for human consumption," exempting them from pharmaceutical-grade manufacturing standards, sterility testing, identity verification, and potency certification. Independent analyses have found research-grade peptides frequently fail identity and purity testing; no specific prevalence figure is supportable from a primary source [12, anecdote_aggregate]. Injectable research-chemical peptides carry documented risks: bacterial endotoxins (fever, systemic inflammation), injection-site infections requiring hospitalisation, unknown impurity profiles, and no established human dosing.

Additionally, the **gene–peptide confusion** is prevalent in consumer discourse: MOTS-c is encoded by the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene, not a nuclear gene — a distinction that vendors frequently obscure. Consumer sourcing via vendor websites does not constitute a clinical-grade product regardless of purity claims.
