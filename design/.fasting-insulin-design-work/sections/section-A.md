# Section A: Physiology & What Fasting Insulin Measures

## Insulin Biosynthesis in the Pancreatic β-Cell

Insulin originates in the pancreatic β-cell as a 110-amino-acid precursor, preproinsulin. Within seconds of ribosomal translation, the N-terminal signal peptide directs co-translational translocation across the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane, where signal peptidase removes the peptide and generates proinsulin — an 86-amino-acid single-chain molecule consisting of the B chain, connecting peptide (C-peptide), and A chain in tandem [1, mechanism_review]. Proinsulin undergoes oxidative folding in the ER lumen, forming three conserved disulfide bonds with assistance from ER chaperones, before trafficking through the Golgi apparatus to the trans-Golgi network, where it is packaged into immature secretory granules [1, mechanism_review].

Within the maturing granule, the endoproteases PC1/3 and PC2 cleave proinsulin at two dibasic sites, excising the C-peptide and yielding mature two-chain insulin in equimolar amounts. At least 99% of intragranular proinsulin undergoes this conversion, storing insulin and C-peptide in a 1:1 molar ratio alongside small quantities of incompletely processed split-proinsulin intermediates [1, mechanism_review]. Under normal physiological conditions, these split products and intact proinsulin represent a minor fraction of total β-cell secretory output, though they rise disproportionately when β-cell secretory capacity is stressed [2, mechanism_review].

## Fasting (Basal) Insulin Secretion and Its Determinants

In the overnight-fasted state, circulating glucose concentrations are low and stable, and insulin secretion is accordingly minimal compared with the postprandial period. Basal insulin secretory tone is set by the intersection of ambient nutrient sensing, autonomic input, and incretin background, sustaining enough portal insulin delivery to suppress hepatic glucose production while avoiding hypoglycaemia [2, mechanism_review].

Critically, basal insulin secretion is not tonically flat. Healthy β-cells exhibit rhythmic, pulsatile secretory bursts with a principal oscillatory period of approximately 10–14 minutes. Lang et al. (1982) measured plasma insulin at one-minute intervals in 28 fasted normal subjects and found synchronous oscillations with a mean period of 10.7 minutes and an amplitude of approximately 1.1 mU/L [3, cohort]. This pulsatility is driven in part by glycolytic oscillations within the β-cell, linking mitochondrial metabolism to rhythmic Ca²⁺ influx and exocytosis. Superimposed on these ~10-minute ultradian cycles are faster, ~5-minute oscillations observed across multiple species. Juhl et al. (2002), in a within-subject study of 7 healthy humans, demonstrated that during prolonged fasting the frequency of insulin secretory bursts is preserved (6.3 ± 0.4 minutes per pulse versus 6.1 ± 0.4 in the fed state; P = NS), while burst amplitude falls significantly (10.1 ± 1.7 vs. 16.0 ± 3.2 pmol/L per pulse, P < 0.05) [4, cohort]. Fasting insulin secretion is therefore modulated primarily through changes in secretory burst amplitude, not frequency — a distinction with clinical relevance, since loss of oscillatory amplitude is an early marker of β-cell dysfunction in type 2 diabetes and obesity.

## Co-Secretion with C-Peptide

Because C-peptide and insulin are cleaved from a single proinsulin precursor and stored together in the same secretory granule, they are released in equimolar amounts into the portal circulation on every secretory event [5, mechanism_review]. This stoichiometric co-secretion is the basis for using C-peptide as a surrogate of β-cell secretory output. However, the two molecules diverge sharply once they enter the hepatic portal vein.

Insulin undergoes substantial first-pass hepatic extraction on its initial transit through the liver. Direct measurements in dogs using paired portal/peripheral infusion protocols found a mean hepatic first-pass extraction fraction of 50.4% (SD ±19.1%; range 22.4–77.2%) (canine study) [6, animal]. This extraction is tightly coupled to hepatic insulin sensitivity: subjects with lower insulin sensitivity extract a smaller fraction on first pass, resulting in higher peripheral insulin concentrations for any given secretion rate (correlation rs = −0.64, P = 0.03) [6, animal]. C-peptide, in contrast, is not extracted by hepatocytes in any appreciable amount and passes through the liver essentially intact, conferring a longer and more stable plasma half-life [7, mechanism_review]. Polonsky and Rubenstein (1984) formalized the practical consequence: peripheral C-peptide concentrations reflect β-cell secretory output far more faithfully than peripheral insulin concentrations do, because insulin levels are confounded by variable hepatic clearance [7, mechanism_review].

## Portal-vs-Peripheral Insulin Concentrations

Because the liver extracts approximately 40–60% of portal insulin on first pass, the concentration of insulin in the portal vein substantially exceeds that measured in peripheral venous or arterial blood. Under normal physiology, portal (hepatic sinusoidal) insulin concentrations are 2- to 3-fold greater than arterial concentrations (canine model) — a gradient that is physiologically critical: the liver is exquisitely sensitive to insulin's suppression of hepatic glucose output at portal concentrations that are well above what peripheral muscle and adipose tissue encounter [8, animal]. This gradient is lost when insulin is delivered subcutaneously (as in treated diabetes), producing relative hepatic insulin deficiency alongside peripheral hyperinsulinaemia.

## What a Peripheral Fasting Insulin Measurement Does and Does Not Index

A fasting peripheral venous insulin value is the net product of two upstream variables: (i) basal β-cell secretory output and (ii) hepatic first-pass extraction efficiency. It is therefore a composite biomarker.

In practical terms, a fasting peripheral insulin that is elevated can reflect either excess secretion (β-cell hypersecretion in response to insulin resistance) or reduced hepatic clearance (lower extraction fraction in insulin-resistant individuals) — and typically reflects both simultaneously, since hepatic extraction varies inversely with insulin resistance [6, animal].

When fasting insulin is combined with fasting glucose in the HOMA-IR formula [(fasting insulin × fasting glucose) / 22.5], the resulting index correlates primarily with hepatic insulin resistance — specifically, with the liver's failure to adequately suppress glucose production in response to prevailing insulin concentrations [9, mechanism_review]. Hoffman (2008) confirmed in adolescents that HOMA-IR and QUICKI correlate significantly with hepatic insulin resistance (HIR) but not with peripheral (muscle) insulin sensitivity (SI*) [10, cohort]. Importantly, HOMA-IR does not directly quantify whole-body or peripheral (skeletal muscle) insulin sensitivity; that domain requires dynamic testing such as the hyperinsulinaemic-euglycaemic clamp or insulin suppression test.

A peripheral fasting insulin therefore encodes:
- The integral of basal β-cell secretory tone over the preceding hours
- The degree of hepatic insulin clearance efficiency
- An inverse signal of hepatic insulin resistance (higher fasting insulin → reduced hepatic suppression of glucose output)

It does **not** directly index skeletal muscle or adipose tissue insulin sensitivity, which require glucose infusion/clamp methods to isolate.

## Reference Values

Fasting insulin in healthy, metabolically normal adults is typically measured in peripheral venous blood and reported in µIU/mL (equivalently µU/mL or mIU/L). A 2024 analysis of a large Brazilian laboratory database proposed population reference intervals of 2.52–13.14 µIU/mL (15.1–78.8 pmol/L; conversion: 1 µIU/mL × 6.0 ≈ pmol/L) [11, cohort]. These values are assay-dependent and not harmonised across platforms; each laboratory's reference interval should be applied to its own results. The basal level cited in studies of insulin secretion kinetics is broadly consistent with this range, with postprandial insulin rising from ~20–30 pmol/L at baseline to 250–300 pmol/L at 30 minutes after oral glucose in healthy subjects [2, mechanism_review].
