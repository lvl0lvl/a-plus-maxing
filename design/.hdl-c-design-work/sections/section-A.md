# Section A — Identity, Physiology & Causal Significance (HDL-C)

## Identity

HDL-C (high-density lipoprotein cholesterol) is the mass of cholesterol carried within high-density lipoprotein particles, reported in mg/dL (US) or mmol/L (SI; 1 mmol/L ≈ 38.67 mg/dL). It is a single scalar — the cholesterol cargo — and is NOT a direct measure of HDL particle number, size, composition, or function. This distinction is the through-line of this section: HDL-C the *concentration* and HDL the *functional particle* are not interchangeable, and most of the apparent contradictions in the HDL literature dissolve once they are kept separate [6, mechanism_review].

## Physiology: reverse cholesterol transport and pleiotropic functions

HDL is biogenated when lipid-poor apolipoprotein A-I (apoA-I) acquires cellular cholesterol and phospholipid. Its best-characterized atheroprotective role is **reverse cholesterol transport (RCT)**: efflux of excess cholesterol from peripheral cells — notably lipid-laden macrophage foam cells in the arterial wall — onto HDL, with transport back to the liver for biliary and fecal excretion [6, mechanism_review]. Macrophage efflux proceeds through several parallel pathways: the ATP-binding cassette transporters **ABCA1** (efflux to lipid-poor apoA-I, the rate-limiting biogenesis step) and **ABCG1**, the scavenger receptor **SR-BI**, and passive aqueous diffusion; hepatic SR-BI then mediates selective uptake of HDL cholesterol into the liver [6, mechanism_review]. Beyond RCT, HDL exerts **anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antiapoptotic, and endothelial-protective** actions — it limits LDL oxidation, supports endothelial nitric-oxide signaling, and dampens proinflammatory signaling in the vessel wall [6, mechanism_review].

Critically, these functions are properties of the *particle*, not of the cholesterol mass it carries. Under inflammatory or oxidative conditions HDL becomes **dysfunctional**: myeloperoxidase-mediated oxidation of apoA-I residues (oxidized tryptophan, chlorinated tyrosine-192, oxidized methionine-148) produces particles that lose efflux capacity and become proinflammatory, and such dysfunctional HDL is associated with increased cardiovascular events even when HDL-C concentration is preserved [6, mechanism_review]. The functional readout bears this out: in the Dallas Heart Study, HDL **cholesterol efflux capacity** in the highest versus lowest quartile carried a hazard ratio of **0.33 (95% CI 0.19–0.55)** for incident cardiovascular events *after* adjustment for traditional risk factors, HDL-C level, AND HDL particle concentration — i.e., HDL function predicts risk independently of, and beyond, HDL-C mass [7, cohort].

## Causal significance: a strong inverse risk MARKER, not a validated treatment TARGET

HDL-C is one of the strongest and most reproducible *epidemiologic* inverse risk markers in cardiovascular medicine. In pooled prospective cohorts, a 1-SD higher usual HDL-C was associated with an MI odds ratio of **0.62 (95% CI 0.58–0.66)** [1, meta_analysis]. This observational strength is why HDL-C is embedded as an input in the Pooled Cohort Equations for 10-year ASCVD risk — low HDL-C raises the estimated risk — in the 2018 AHA/ACC/Multisociety cholesterol guideline [8, mechanism_review]. But the same guideline assigns NO HDL-C-raising pharmacotherapy for prevention: HDL-C is a risk-assessment input, not a treatment target [8, mechanism_review].

Two independent lines of evidence explain why raising HDL-C is not therapeutic.

**(1) Mendelian randomization is null.** Genetic variants that raise HDL-C should lower MI risk if the relationship were causal. They do not. The *LIPG* Asn396Ser variant raises HDL-C by ~0.14 mmol/L (~5.4 mg/dL) with no effect on LDL or triglycerides; the observational association predicted an MI OR of **0.87 (95% CI 0.84–0.91)**, yet the *observed* genetic effect was **0.99 (95% CI 0.88–1.11, p=0.85)** across 116,320 participants [1, meta_analysis]. A 14-SNP HDL genetic score gave an MI OR per 1-SD of **0.93 (95% CI 0.68–1.26, p=0.63)** — null [1, meta_analysis]. As a positive control in the same study, a 13-SNP LDL score *did* track its observational effect (OR 2.13, 95% CI 1.69–2.69 per 1-SD), confirming the method works and isolating HDL-C as the non-causal exception [1, meta_analysis].

**(2) HDL-C-raising interventions failed in RCTs.** Every major attempt to lower events by raising HDL-C failed:
- **Torcetrapib (ILLUMINATE, n=15,067):** raised HDL-C ~72% and lowered LDL-C ~25%, yet INCREASED death (HR 1.58, 95% CI 1.14–2.19, p=0.006) and cardiovascular events (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.09–1.44, p=0.001); terminated early for harm [10, rct].
- **Evacetrapib (ACCELERATE, n=12,092):** raised HDL-C ~132 percentage points and lowered LDL-C ~37 points more than placebo, with NO reduction in the primary composite endpoint; stopped for futility [5, rct].
- **Niacin — AIM-HIGH (n=3,414):** raised HDL-C ~25% on a statin background; no reduction in cardiovascular events, stopped early for futility [4, rct].
- **Niacin/laropiprant — HPS2-THRIVE (n=25,673):** primary major-vascular-event risk ratio **0.96 (95% CI 0.90–1.03, p=0.29)** despite favorable lipid changes, with a net excess of serious adverse events [3, rct].

The dalcetrapib CETP trial (dal-OUTCOMES) was likewise null. Across drug classes and tens of thousands of patients, pharmacologically raising HDL-C mass did not reduce — and in the torcetrapib case worsened — outcomes [3, rct; 4, rct; 5, rct; 10, rct].

## The U-shaped / paradoxical association

The "higher is always better" intuition is wrong even epidemiologically: the HDL-C–mortality relationship is **U-shaped**, with very high HDL-C associated with INCREASED mortality. In two Danish cohorts (52,268 men + 64,240 women, CCHS+CGPS), all-cause mortality was lowest at **1.9 mmol/L (~73 mg/dL) in men** and **2.4 mmol/L (~93 mg/dL) in women**; above that, risk rose — men ≥3.0 mmol/L (~116 mg/dL): HR **2.06 (95% CI 1.44–2.95)**; women ≥3.5 mmol/L (~135 mg/dL): HR **1.68 (95% CI 1.09–2.58)** [2, cohort]. The independent Canadian CANHEART cohort (≈631,000 Ontarians without prior CVD) reproduced the U-shape for both CVD and non-CVD mortality, found high HDL-C conferred no cardioprotection, and noted the excess mortality at high HDL-C was largely non-cardiovascular — leading the authors to question whether HDL-C is even a cardiovascular-specific risk factor [9, cohort].

## Honest framing for downstream use

HDL-C is a useful, well-validated **inverse risk marker** and a legitimate **risk-calculator input** (Pooled Cohort Equations) [1, meta_analysis; 8, mechanism_review]. It is NOT a validated causal target: genetics say raising it does not lower MI [1, meta_analysis], interventions confirm raising it does not (and can harm) [3,4,5,10, rct], and very high HDL-C tracks higher mortality [2, cohort; 9, cohort]. The mechanistic resolution is that HDL *function* (efflux capacity, particle quality) — not HDL-C *mass* — carries whatever protective signal exists [6, mechanism_review; 7, cohort]. Interpret HDL-C as a barometer of metabolic/cardiovascular health, never as a number to be pharmacologically maximized.
