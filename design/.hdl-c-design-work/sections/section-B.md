# Section B — Reference Ranges, Measurement & Determinants (HDL-C)

Goal-agnostic canonical reference. Not personalized. Every quantitative claim is tagged inline `[ref, type-tag]`.

## Thresholds and reference ranges

The NCEP Adult Treatment Panel III (ATP III) defines **low HDL cholesterol as < 40 mg/dL**, treated as a categorical major cardiovascular risk factor, and **high HDL cholesterol as ≥ 60 mg/dL**, historically counted as a "negative" (protective) risk factor that subtracts one factor from the risk-factor tally [8, regulatory]. The sex-specific metabolic-syndrome HDL criterion is **< 40 mg/dL in men and < 50 mg/dL in women** — these are the canonical "low HDL" thresholds used in metabolic-syndrome diagnosis and reproduced across the U-shaped-curve and mortality literature [8, regulatory] [1, mechanism_review]. Low HDL-C (< 40 mg/dL in men, < 50 mg/dL in women) is one of the five NCEP ATP III metabolic-syndrome criteria; three of five confer the diagnosis [8, regulatory].

The ≥ 60 mg/dL "protective" designation is being de-emphasized because population data show a **U-shaped** relationship between HDL-C and mortality rather than a monotonic benefit [1, mechanism_review]. In the Copenhagen City Heart Study + Copenhagen General Population Study (52,268 men, 64,240 women, 745,452 person-years), the **nadir of all-cause mortality risk fell at 1.9 mmol/L (73 mg/dL) in men and 2.4 mmol/L (93 mg/dL) in women** — not at the highest HDL values [2, cohort]. Above the nadir, mortality rose: in men, HDL 2.5–2.99 mmol/L gave HR 1.36 (95% CI 1.09–1.70) and HDL ≥ 3.0 mmol/L (≥ 116 mg/dL) gave HR 2.06 (1.44–2.95); in women, HDL ≥ 3.5 mmol/L (≥ 135 mg/dL) gave HR 1.68 (1.09–2.58) [2, cohort].

The high-HDL mortality inflection is most consistently reported **around ≥ 80–90 mg/dL and is stronger in men** [1, mechanism_review]. In the CANHEART cohort (631,762 individuals, mean HDL-C 55.2 mg/dL, mean follow-up 4.9 years), individuals with higher HDL — **> 70 mg/dL in men, > 90 mg/dL in women** — had increased hazard of non-cardiovascular mortality, while lower HDL-C tracked with higher cardiovascular, cancer, and other mortality; the dose-response was U-shaped against a ~40–50 mg/dL reference [3, cohort]. Supporting figures cited in the review: in a coronary-heart-disease cohort, HDL ≥ 80 mg/dL carried **96% higher all-cause and 71% higher ASCVD mortality** versus a 40–60 mg/dL reference; in a pooled cohort (~43,000), men with HDL ≥ 90 mg/dL had a **2.6-fold higher ASCVD mortality**; UK Biobank men with very-high HDL had ~**80% higher** all-cause and ASCVD mortality, with no significant effect in women [1, mechanism_review].

## Units

HDL-C in mg/dL is converted to mmol/L by **dividing by 38.67** (equivalently multiplying by ~0.0259); the factor derives from cholesterol's molecular weight (~386.65 g/mol) and is identical for total, HDL, and LDL cholesterol [10, regulatory]. Worked anchors: 40 mg/dL ≈ 1.03 mmol/L; 60 mg/dL ≈ 1.55 mmol/L; 1.9 mmol/L ≈ 73 mg/dL [2, cohort] [10, regulatory].

## Measurement

Modern laboratories use **homogeneous direct assays**, which replaced the older **lipoprotein-precipitation** methods. Precipitation methods cannot completely precipitate apoB-containing lipoproteins in hypertriglyceridemic samples, introducing interference; homogeneous assays show better precision (one validation: within-run CV < 1.87%, total CV < 3.08%) [10, mechanism_review]. The CDC reference method for HDL-C is a three-step procedure — ultracentrifugation, heparin-manganese precipitation, then the Abell-Kendall reference method — and methods are harmonized through the **CDC/NHLBI Cholesterol Reference Method Laboratory Network (CRMLN)** by fresh-sample comparison [10, regulatory]. The NCEP analytical goals for HDL-C are **bias ≤ 5.0%, precision CV ≤ 4.0% at concentrations ≥ 42 mg/dL (SD ≤ 1.7 mg/dL below 42 mg/dL), and allowable total error ≤ 13%** for a single measurement [10, regulatory].

Biological variability dominates the interpretive picture: HDL-C has a **within-subject biological variation (CVI) of 7.3%** and a between-subject variation (CVG) of 21.2%, with a desirable analytical imprecision of 3.65%, desirable bias 5.61%, and desirable total error 11.63% [9, mechanism_review]. The 7.3% CVI means a serial change must exceed the reference-change value before it is real rather than noise.

## Determinants

**Genetics.** Extreme HDL-C is strongly heritable. Rare large-effect variants in **CETP, LIPC (hepatic lipase), SCARB1, and LIPG (endothelial lipase)** drive very-high HDL-C, while **ABCA1 (Tangier disease), APOA1, and LCAT** drive very-low HDL-C [5, cohort]. In a clinical extremes cohort, **18.7% of low-HDL-C and 10.9% of high-HDL-C patients carried a rare heterozygous large-effect variant**; adding a polygenic trait score, ~30% of extreme-HDL individuals had either a rare large-effect variant or a bundle of common small-effect variants [5, cohort]. Extreme thresholds used: ≤ 0.8 mmol/L (men) / ≤ 1.0 mmol/L (women) for low, ≥ 1.4 mmol/L (men) / ≥ 1.8 mmol/L (women) for high [5, cohort].

**Raised by aerobic exercise.** A meta-analysis of 35 RCTs (1,404 subjects) found aerobic training raised HDL-C by a mean of **2.53 mg/dL**; the minimum effective dose was ~900 kcal/week or ~120 min/week, and **each additional 10 min per session added ~1.4 mg/dL** [4, meta_analysis].

**Raised by moderate alcohol.** A meta-analysis of interventional studies (33 pooled, 796 participants) found moderate alcohol raised HDL-C by **0.094 mmol/L (95% CI 0.064–0.123; ~3.6 mg/dL)**, with a dose-response: +0.072 mmol/L at 1–2 drinks/day, +0.103 at 2–4, +0.141 at ≥ 5 (P-trend = 0.013) [6, meta_analysis].

**Raised by weight loss and estrogen; lowered by smoking, obesity, insulin resistance/T2D.** Smoking lowers HDL-C: a meta-analysis of 27 studies found HDL-C rose **0.100 mmol/L (95% CI 0.074–0.127; ~3.9 mg/dL) after smoking cessation** while LDL-C, total cholesterol, and triglycerides were unchanged [7, meta_analysis]. Low HDL-C co-clusters with obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes as the metabolic-syndrome phenotype [8, regulatory] [1, mechanism_review].

**Lowered by very-low-fat/high-carbohydrate diets.** In a randomized crossover trial, a low-fat high-carbohydrate diet lowered HDL-C from 1.21 to 1.07 mmol/L (~47 → ~41 mg/dL; P = 0.057) versus a Western diet [11, rct] — directional and from a single small trial, flagged approximate.

**Lowered by anabolic-androgenic steroids.** Oral 17-α-alkylated anabolic steroids profoundly suppress HDL-C, with reported reductions of **~60–80% (case reports > 90%)** alongside an LDL-C rise of ~50%; mechanism is a rise in hepatic triglyceride lipase preceding the HDL2 decline, reversible ~2.5–4 months after discontinuation [11, cohort/open_label] — pooled across heterogeneous sources, flagged approximate.
