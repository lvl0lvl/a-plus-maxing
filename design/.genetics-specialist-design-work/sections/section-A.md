# Section A — Variant Interpretation Framework & DTC Raw-Data Limitations

This section establishes the canonical, goal-agnostic reference framework a clinical genetics and pharmacogenomics specialist agent must hold before any genotype is interpreted. The central principle running through every subtopic is the same: a sequence variant is not a diagnosis. Its meaning is a probabilistic judgment built from structured evidence, modulated by penetrance and genetic architecture, anchored to population frequency, and — critically for consumer data — only as trustworthy as the assay that called it.

## 1. The ACMG/AMP 2015 Variant Classification Standard

The reference standard for classifying germline sequence variants in Mendelian disease genes is the 2015 joint consensus recommendation of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology [1, mechanism_review]. It recommends five standardized terms — Pathogenic, Likely Pathogenic, Uncertain Significance (VUS), Likely Benign, and Benign — and defines a framework of discrete evidence criteria, each with an assigned code, that an interpreter combines to reach a classification [1, mechanism_review]. "Likely" denotes greater than ~90% certainty of pathogenicity or benignity; the VUS bin absorbs everything that cannot be confidently resolved in either direction [1, mechanism_review].

The criteria are organized along two axes — direction (pathogenic vs. benign) and strength. Pathogenic evidence runs from very strong (PVS1, reserved for null variants such as nonsense, frameshift, and canonical splice changes in a gene where loss of function is an established disease mechanism), through strong (PS1–PS4: e.g., same amino-acid change as an established pathogenic variant, de novo occurrence, functional studies, case-control enrichment), moderate (PM1–PM6: e.g., location in a mutational hotspot or critical functional domain, absence from controls, in-trans with a pathogenic variant, de novo without confirmed parentage), to supporting (PP1–PP5: e.g., cosegregation with disease, computational predictions of deleteriousness, phenotype highly specific for the gene) [1, mechanism_review]. Benign evidence comprises one stand-alone criterion (BA1: allele frequency above 5% in a reference population), strong criteria (BS1–BS4: e.g., frequency greater than expected for the disorder, observation in a healthy adult, lack of segregation, functional evidence of no impact), and supporting criteria (BP1–BP7: e.g., missense in a gene where only truncating variants cause disease, synonymous change with no predicted splice effect) [1, mechanism_review]. The interpreter tallies which criteria are met and applies scoring rules to combine them into one of the five tiers [1, mechanism_review].

Because the original 2015 combining rules were a qualitative heuristic, two refinement efforts followed. The ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation (SVI) Working Group issues gene- and criterion-specific specifications — for example, refined PVS1 decision trees for loss-of-function variants and recalibrated computational and functional-evidence weights — so that the general framework is operationalized consistently across genes [1, mechanism_review]. Separately, Tavtigian and colleagues demonstrated that the discrete ACMG/AMP combining rules are closely approximated by a naive Bayesian classifier: each evidence strength level (supporting, moderate, strong, very strong) corresponds to an odds-of-pathogenicity weight that scales exponentially, and the published combining rules are internally consistent with this model with only minor exceptions [2, mechanism_review]. This Bayesian reformulation gives the qualitative framework a quantitative backbone, makes the relative weight of each criterion explicit, and underpins later point-based scoring systems that allow partial-strength evidence to be summed onto a continuous scale [2, mechanism_review].

## 2. Penetrance versus Expressivity

A foundational error the agent must guard against is equating "carries a pathogenic variant" with "has, or will get, the disease." Two distinct genetic concepts break that equation. **Penetrance** is the proportion of individuals carrying a given genotype who actually manifest the associated phenotype; when fewer than all carriers are affected, penetrance is reduced (incomplete). **Expressivity** is the degree or severity with which the phenotype manifests among those who are affected; when it varies between carriers of the same variant, expressivity is variable. A variant can be highly penetrant but variably expressive, or fully expressive in the few who manifest it yet poorly penetrant overall.

Reduced penetrance is not an exotic edge case but a widespread phenomenon across human inherited disease [3, mechanism_review]. A major review of its molecular basis catalogs the factors that uncouple genotype from phenotype: age- and sex-dependent expression (so that penetrance estimated in older cohorts exceeds that in younger ones), unlinked modifier genes acting in trans, variation at the second allele or in cis, epigenetic state, copy-number context, allelic-expression imbalance, and environmental exposures [3, mechanism_review]. The same review notes that ostensibly healthy individuals can carry substantial numbers of variants that would be predicted disadvantageous, yet remain unaffected — direct evidence that a single deleterious genotype is frequently insufficient to cause disease on its own [3, mechanism_review]. **Pleiotropy** — one variant influencing multiple, sometimes unrelated, traits — further complicates the picture, because the phenotype a carrier expresses may not be the one the testing context anticipated. The practical consequence for the agent is strict: penetrance estimates derived from ascertained, affected families systematically overstate risk for an unselected individual found to carry the same variant incidentally, and any risk communication must frame a positive finding as a probability conditioned on incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity, never as a determined outcome.

## 3. Monogenic versus Polygenic Architecture, and Polygenic Risk Scores

Disease architecture spans a continuum. **Monogenic (Mendelian)** conditions are driven by high-effect variants in a single gene, interpreted under the ACMG/AMP framework above. **Polygenic** (complex) traits — type 2 diabetes, coronary artery disease, most psychiatric and anthropometric phenotypes — arise from the aggregate effect of thousands of common variants, each of tiny individual effect, identified through genome-wide association studies (GWAS). A **polygenic risk score (PRS)** sums an individual's risk alleles weighted by their GWAS-estimated effect sizes into a single quantitative estimate of genetic liability.

Two clinical-utility limits are essential. First, a PRS estimates relative position in a risk distribution, not a diagnosis, and for most traits explains only a modest fraction of variance, so its incremental value over conventional clinical risk factors is often small. Second — and the dominant documented limitation — PRS portability across genetic ancestries is poor. Because the overwhelming majority of GWAS discovery samples are of European ancestry, prediction accuracy decays as the target individual's ancestry diverges from that discovery population: linkage-disequilibrium patterns, allele frequencies, and effect sizes differ across ancestries [4, mechanism_review]. The landmark analysis quantifying this reported that PRS prediction accuracy was, on average, roughly 4.9-fold lower in individuals of African ancestry relative to European ancestry, with intermediate reductions in East Asian and South Asian groups [4, mechanism_review]. The authors warned that deploying current PRS clinically would risk exacerbating, rather than reducing, health disparities for already-underserved populations [4, mechanism_review]. For a goal-agnostic library, the rule is that any PRS-derived statement must be qualified by the ancestry composition of the underlying GWAS and treated as far less reliable for non-European individuals.

## 4. Allele-Frequency Context: gnomAD and Benign Criteria

Population allele frequency is one of the most powerful single pieces of variant evidence, and the reference resource is the **Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD)**. The flagship release aggregated 125,748 exomes and 15,708 genomes — 141,456 individuals — and used them to quantify the spectrum of mutational constraint across protein-coding genes, identifying 443,769 high-confidence predicted loss-of-function variants after artifact filtering [5, cohort]. gnomAD's ancestry-stratified frequencies are the de facto denominator for asking whether a variant is "too common to be a fully penetrant cause of a rare disease."

This directly grounds the benign side of the ACMG/AMP framework. The stand-alone benign criterion **BA1** is invoked when a variant's allele frequency exceeds 5% in a reference population such as gnomAD [1, mechanism_review]. The strong benign criterion **BS1** applies when the observed frequency is greater than would be expected given the disorder's prevalence, inheritance, and penetrance — a calculation that requires the population frequency from a resource like gnomAD as its numerator [1, mechanism_review]. ClinGen's SVI work refined these thresholds gene-by-gene rather than relying on a universal 5% cutoff, since the maximum credible population allele frequency for a pathogenic variant depends on disease incidence and penetrance assumptions [1, mechanism_review]. The agent must use ancestry-matched frequencies: a variant common in one population but rare in gnomAD's aggregate can be misclassified if the wrong subpopulation denominator is applied.

## 5. ClinVar and ClinGen as Variant Knowledgebases, and Conflicting Interpretations

**ClinVar** is the freely available NIH public archive of human variants and their submitted clinical interpretations, contributed by clinical laboratories, research groups, expert panels, and others [6, mechanism_review]. It aggregates submissions by variant–condition pair and computes an aggregate clinical-significance value reflecting whether submitters agree [6, mechanism_review]. **ClinGen (the Clinical Genome Resource)** is the complementary expert-curation effort that defines gene–disease validity, dosage sensitivity, and the SVI criterion specifications discussed above, and convenes Variant Curation Expert Panels whose three-star reviewed assertions carry more weight than individual submissions.

The structural weakness of a submission-based archive is **conflicting interpretations**. When submitters disagree within the five-tier scale, ClinVar flags the record as having "conflicting interpretations of pathogenicity," and conflicts are calculated only within the ACMG-recommended terms [6, mechanism_review]. These conflicts are common and consequential: the same variant may be reported as Pathogenic by one laboratory and as a VUS or Likely Benign by another, owing to differences in evidence access, criterion application, or recency of curation [6, mechanism_review]. ClinVar's star-based review-status system exists precisely so that downstream users can weight an expert-panel or practice-guideline assertion above a single unverified submission [6, mechanism_review]. The agent must therefore never treat a bare ClinVar "Pathogenic" label as ground truth; it must inspect review status, the number and identity of submitters, and whether any conflict flag is present, and must distinguish an outdated single submission from a current expert-panel consensus.

## 6. Direct-to-Consumer (DTC) Raw Genotype Data Limitations — Load-Bearing

This is the most safety-critical subtopic for any agent that might receive a consumer's exported raw genotype file. The core technical fact is the **genotyping-array-versus-sequencing distinction**. Consumer products such as 23andMe and AncestryDNA use SNP genotyping arrays (chips): the chip interrogates several hundred thousand pre-selected positions by hybridizing the sample to fixed probes and assigning a genotype from fluorescence-intensity clustering across a batch of samples. This is fundamentally different from clinical-grade next-generation sequencing, which reads every base in the targeted region. Array genotyping is accurate and inexpensive for common variants, but its accuracy collapses for rare variants, because clustering depends on having enough carriers in the batch to define a reliable cluster — and rare pathogenic variants, by definition, have almost none.

The consequence is a documented, large false-positive problem in DTC raw data. A clinical-laboratory case series reviewing 49 patient samples sent for confirmation of variants reported in DTC raw data found that approximately 40% of variants were false positives — not confirmed on clinical-grade testing — and that a high proportion of the genes involved were clinically significant cancer-risk genes [7, open_label]. The most rigorous quantification comes from a population-based diagnostic evaluation of 49,908 UK Biobank participants who had both SNP-chip and sequencing data: for very rare variants (frequency below ~0.001%), the positive predictive value of the chip was only about 16%, and for pathogenic BRCA-region variants specifically the positive predictive value was just 4.2% — of 889 participants with a chip-detected pathogenic BRCA variant, only 37 were true positives on sequencing [8, cohort]. The authors concluded that for an individual, a positive chip result for a very rare pathogenic variant is more likely to be wrong than right, and that array results from consumer companies or biobanks must be validated by a standard diagnostic test before any clinical action [8, cohort]. In a small Personal Genome Project subset, 95% of individuals had at least one false-positive rare pathogenic variant call [8, cohort]. These figures are derived from human samples, so no species-mismatch qualifier applies.

The **FDA's authorization scope** reinforces this from the regulatory side. In March 2018 the FDA authorized, with special controls, the 23andMe Personal Genome Service Genetic Health Risk Report for BRCA1/BRCA2 (Selected Variants) — the first direct-to-consumer cancer-risk test — but only for three specific variants (two in BRCA1, one in BRCA2) common in people of Ashkenazi Jewish descent [10, regulatory]. The FDA stated explicitly that the test "only detects three out of more than 1,000 known BRCA mutations," that "most BRCA mutations that increase an individual's risk are not detected by this test," that "a negative result does not rule out the possibility that an individual carries other BRCA mutations that increase cancer risk," and that the test "should not be used as a substitute for seeing your doctor for cancer screenings or counseling" [10, regulatory]. The agency further noted that decisions about treatment or preventive surgery "require confirmatory testing and genetic counseling" [10, regulatory]. A peer-reviewed analysis of this regulatory language emphasized the central tension a consumer faces: even within the FDA-authorized health report, the FDA requires that results be "confirmed in a clinical setting before taking any medical action," and that the test "does not diagnose cancer or any other health conditions and should not be used to make medical decisions" [9, regulatory]. The authorized GHR report carries these controls; the unvalidated **raw** genotype file a consumer can export — the data feeding third-party interpretation tools — carries none of them and is precisely the array output whose rare-variant positive predictive value the cohort data show to be poor [8, cohort][7, open_label].

The synthesized, non-negotiable rules for the agent are therefore: (a) a clinically actionable call read from DTC raw genotype data is a hypothesis, not a result, and MUST be confirmed by a clinical-grade test (sequencing in an accredited laboratory) before any action is taken or any risk is communicated as real; (b) even a confirmed risk variant is not a disease, because penetrance, expressivity, and — for complex traits — polygenic and ancestry context all separate carrying a variant from developing the condition; and (c) any disease-risk finding must be routed to a board-certified genetic counselor and a physician, never adjudicated by the agent alone. Association is not causation, and risk is not diagnosis.
