# Section C — HLA Immunogenetic Risk Alleles and Nutrigenomics

This section is canonical, goal-agnostic library reference. It covers two domains a clinical genetics and pharmacogenomics agent must keep clearly separated: (1) HLA class I alleles that predict severe, sometimes fatal immune-mediated drug reactions — where genotype is genuinely actionable and society guidelines mandate pre-prescription testing — and (2) common "wellness" nutrigenomic variants, where associations are real but effect sizes are small, gene–environment interaction dominates, and direct-to-consumer (DTC) reports routinely overinterpret. The contrast between these two domains is the single most important pedagogical point of the section: the same word ("a genetic variant affecting a drug or nutrient") spans risk ratios of 100+ with regulatory contraindications on one end and per-allele effects explaining under 1% of trait variance on the other.

## 1. HLA Pharmacogenomics — Severe Cutaneous and Hypersensitivity Reactions

The human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genes on chromosome 6p21 encode the most polymorphic proteins in the human genome. Specific class I alleles present particular small-molecule drugs (or drug–peptide complexes) to cytotoxic T cells, triggering severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs): Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), and immune-mediated hypersensitivity. Unlike nutrigenomic associations, these are high-effect-size, monogenic-acting predictors with clear clinical action thresholds. The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) issues consensus prescribing guidance for several of these allele–drug pairs.

### HLA-B*57:01 and Abacavir Hypersensitivity

Abacavir, a nucleoside reverse-transcriptase inhibitor used in HIV therapy, causes a systemic hypersensitivity reaction (fever, rash, gastrointestinal and respiratory symptoms) in a subset of patients; rechallenge can be fatal. The PREDICT-1 trial settled the causal role of HLA-B*57:01. This was a double-blind, prospective, randomized study of 1,956 patients across 19 countries, comparing prospective HLA-B*57:01 screening (abacavir withheld from carriers) against standard care [1, rct]. Immunologically confirmed hypersensitivity (positive patch test) occurred in 0% of the screening arm versus 2.7% of the control arm (p<0.001), and clinically suspected hypersensitivity fell from 7.8% to 3.4% (p<0.001) [1, rct]. The allele carried a 100% negative predictive value and a 47.9% positive predictive value, with HLA-B*57:01 present in 5.6% of the (84% white) study population [1, rct]. The association is generalizable across ancestries even though allele frequency varies. This is the textbook example of a pharmacogenomic test that moved directly into routine practice: HLA-B*57:01 testing is now standard of care before abacavir initiation, and FDA labeling reflects this regulatory expectation.

### HLA-B*15:02 and HLA-A*31:01 and Carbamazepine

Carbamazepine (and the related oxcarbazepine) is an anticonvulsant and mood stabilizer associated with SJS/TEN and other hypersensitivity reactions. Two distinct HLA alleles modulate risk, and CPIC's 2017 update integrates both [2, mechanism_review]. HLA-B*15:02 is strongly associated with carbamazepine- and oxcarbazepine-induced SJS/TEN. In Han Chinese populations the test shows ~98% sensitivity, ~97% specificity, a low positive predictive value (~7.7%), and — critically — a 100% negative predictive value [2, mechanism_review]. Allele frequency is markedly ancestry-dependent: ~6.9% in East Asian, ~5.4% in Oceanian, and ~4.6% in South/Central Asian groups, but under 1% in European, African, Middle Eastern, and Hispanic populations, and under 1% in Japanese [2, mechanism_review]. This frequency gradient is why screening recommendations historically targeted Southeast Asian ancestry, although CPIC frames recommendations by genotype rather than self-reported ethnicity. HLA-A*31:01 is associated with a broader spectrum of carbamazepine reactions — maculopapular exanthema, DRESS, and SJS/TEN — with strongest evidence in European and Japanese populations and a combined positive predictive value around 43% in Europeans [2, mechanism_review]. Allele frequency is ~3% in Caucasians, ~6% in Hispanic/South American groups, ~8% in Japanese, and ~5% in Korean populations [2, mechanism_review]. CPIC recommends against carbamazepine in HLA-B*15:02-positive carbamazepine-naïve patients (strong), and advises avoiding carbamazepine in HLA-A*31:01-positive patients when alternatives exist [2, mechanism_review]. Tolerance for more than three months substantially reduces residual risk because most reactions occur within the first 4–28 days of therapy [2, mechanism_review].

### HLA-B*58:01 and Allopurinol

Allopurinol, a xanthine-oxidase inhibitor used for gout and hyperuricemia, is a leading cause of SCARs, and HLA-B*58:01 is the strongest known predictor. The CPIC guideline (2015 update) formalizes the allele–drug association and recommends against allopurinol in HLA-B*58:01-positive patients, favoring alternative urate-lowering therapy [3, mechanism_review]. Reported effect sizes are very large but vary by population and study design: a meta-analysis of allopurinol-induced SJS/TEN reported summary odds ratios of 74.18 (95% CI 26.95–204.14) in Asians and 101.45 (95% CI 44.98–228.82) in non-Asians [12, meta_analysis]. A more recent living systematic review and meta-analysis (24 case–control studies, 13,719 patients) pooled the association at OR 117.6 (95% CI 70.3–196.8; I²=45%) [13, meta_analysis]. An updated diagnostic meta-analysis pooling nine population-control studies (7,534 patients, predominantly Asian) reported ~78% sensitivity (95% CI 0.71–0.85) and ~96% specificity (95% CI 0.96–0.97) for HLA-B*58:01 in allopurinol-induced SCARs, with a negative predictive value near 99% [14, meta_analysis]. As with HLA-B*15:02, the allele is far more common in some East/Southeast Asian populations than in Europeans, which shaped where pre-prescription screening became cost-effective. The imperfect (~78%) sensitivity illustrates an important nuance: even a strong HLA predictor is not a complete predictor — a negative result lowers but does not eliminate risk, so clinical vigilance for early rash remains mandatory regardless of genotype [14, meta_analysis].

### G6PD Deficiency — An Adjacent Enzyme Pharmacogene

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is not an HLA/immune mechanism but is the canonical adjacent enzyme pharmacogene, because the actionable risk is also a severe, drug-triggered adverse event: acute hemolytic anemia under oxidative stress. G6PD is the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway, generating the NADPH that protects erythrocytes from oxidative damage; deficient red cells hemolyze when challenged by oxidative drugs (e.g., rasburicase, dapsone, primaquine), certain infections, or fava beans. The CPIC guideline for rasburicase states that rasburicase is contraindicated in G6PD-deficient patients due to risk of acute hemolytic anemia and possible methemoglobinemia [4, mechanism_review]. G6PD deficiency is X-linked, so it affects hemizygous males more uniformly than heterozygous females (in whom random X-inactivation produces variable, mosaic enzyme levels); it is among the most common human enzymopathies worldwide, with high prevalence in populations historically exposed to malaria (Mediterranean, African, Middle Eastern, and South/Southeast Asian ancestry). Because heterozygous females and intermediate phenotypes complicate genotype-to-phenotype prediction, CPIC notes that a quantitative enzyme activity assay remains the mainstay of screening when preemptive genotyping has not established the diagnosis [4, mechanism_review]. This is a useful contrast to the HLA examples: genotype predicts enzyme deficiency imperfectly, so the functional assay, not the genotype alone, governs the clinical decision.

## 2. Nutrigenomics — Common "Wellness" Variants with Honest Effect-Size Framing

The variants below are real associations from large human cohorts and GWAS. They are physiologically interesting and, in a few cases, actionable. But none functions like an HLA contraindication, and every one of them must be communicated with effect-size honesty.

### MTHFR C677T (rs1801133) and A1298C (rs1801131)

Methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) converts 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate to 5-methyltetrahydrofolate, the methyl donor for remethylation of homocysteine to methionine. The C677T variant (Ala222Val) produces a thermolabile enzyme with reduced activity; TT homozygotes have modestly elevated plasma homocysteine, especially under low folate intake. The biology is genuine. The clinical-utility conclusion, however, is unambiguous and load-bearing: the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) practice guideline states that MTHFR polymorphism testing has minimal clinical utility and should not be ordered as part of a routine thrombophilia evaluation, nor as part of the evaluation for recurrent pregnancy loss [5, mechanism_review]. The guideline rests on meta-analyses that disproved both the association between MTHFR genotype and venous thromboembolism risk and the association between hyperhomocysteinemia and coronary heart disease risk [5, mechanism_review]. This is the cleanest available example of "statistically detectable biochemical effect" not translating into "clinically actionable test." A genetics agent should treat a positive MTHFR result as, at most, a prompt to ensure adequate dietary folate — not as a diagnosis, a thrombophilia marker, or a justification for high-dose methylfolate supplementation. The A1298C variant has even weaker phenotypic consequences and is likewise not a recommended clinical test [5, mechanism_review].

### CYP1A2 *1F (rs762551) and Caffeine Metabolism

The hepatic enzyme CYP1A2 metabolizes ~95% of ingested caffeine. The rs762551 variant defines metabolizer status: the A/A genotype (CYP1A2*1A/*1A) confers "rapid" metabolism, while C-allele carriers (CYP1A2*1F) are "slow" metabolizers. The pivotal gene–environment study was a Costa Rican case-control study of 2,014 incident nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI) cases and 2,014 matched population controls [6, cohort]. CYP1A2 genotype modified the coffee–MI association: increased coffee intake was associated with elevated MI risk only among slow metabolizers (C carriers), and the effect was most pronounced in younger individuals; rapid metabolizers showed no such risk increase, and at lower intakes coffee was not harmful even in slow metabolizers [6, cohort]. This is one of the better-characterized real gene–diet interactions, and it is instructive precisely because the headline ("a gene affects whether coffee is bad for you") is true yet the actionable advice is narrow: heavy intake in slow metabolizers, in a single observational cohort. It demonstrates effect modification rather than a deterministic rule, and it has not been translated into a guideline-endorsed clinical test.

### FTO (rs9939609) and Adiposity

The FTO (fat mass and obesity-associated) locus produced the first robust common-variant association with human adiposity. The original report identified the rs9939609 A allele (frequency ~39–45% in Europeans) as associated with BMI and obesity in ~38,759 participants, with the genome-wide signal originating from a type 2 diabetes scan that proved to be mediated by BMI [7, cohort]. The honest effect size is small: a per-allele increase of approximately 0.36 kg/m² in BMI, corresponding to roughly 3 kg of body weight difference between AA and TT homozygotes, and an obesity odds ratio of ~1.67 for AA versus TT [7, cohort]. Subsequent work places this in stark perspective — rs9939609 explains under 1% of population BMI variance, and even 941 independent BMI variants collectively account for only ~6% of phenotypic variation [11, mechanism_review]. FTO is therefore the archetype of a genuine but minuscule common-variant effect: real at the population level, near-meaningless for individual prediction, and entirely subordinate to energy balance and behavior.

### APOE Genotype, Dietary/Lipid Response, and Cardiovascular Risk

The APOE gene's three common alleles (ε2, ε3, ε4) shape lipoprotein metabolism and are among the few nutrigenomic loci with both a strong disease association (ε4 and Alzheimer's, ε4 and cardiovascular disease) and a plausible diet-response interaction. ε4 carriers (~25% of Caucasians) show the highest fasting total and LDL cholesterol in the ordering ε4 > ε3/ε3 > ε2, and appear more responsive to dietary fat manipulation — larger LDL reductions when saturated fat is lowered and larger increases when it is raised [8, mechanism_review]. The mechanistic account involves genotype-dependent differences in postprandial lipoprotein–LDL-receptor interaction [8, mechanism_review]. Important caveats temper this: responsiveness is variable across studies and populations, the elevated cardiovascular risk in ε4 carriers is not fully explained by the modest cholesterol differences, and the dietary advice that flows from an ε4 result (reduce saturated fat) is the same first-line advice given to the general population [8, mechanism_review]. APOE also raises a distinct ethical issue absent from the other variants: ε4 status carries Alzheimer's risk information, so any "wellness" report disclosing APOE genotype is implicitly returning a neurodegenerative risk marker.

### MCM6 / LCT-13910 C>T (rs4988235) and Lactase Persistence

Lactase persistence — retention of intestinal lactase-phlorizin hydrolase activity into adulthood — is one of the few nutrigenomic traits with near-Mendelian behavior in European-ancestry populations. The causal regulatory variant lies ~14 kb upstream of LCT, in intron 13 of the neighboring MCM6 gene: the −13910 C>T variant (rs4988235), which alters a transcription-factor binding site and was completely associated with the trait in Finnish and three other populations [9, cohort]. The T allele (dominant) confers lactase persistence; C/C homozygotes show the typical post-weaning decline in lactase and present with lactose maldigestion [9, cohort]. This locus is a useful counterexample to the "all nutrigenomics is weak" generalization — it is a genuinely high-penetrance dietary-tolerance trait in Europeans. But two honest caveats apply: (1) rs4988235 is the persistence variant in European-ancestry populations, whereas independent variants drive persistence in African and Middle Eastern populations, so a single-SNP test misclassifies non-Europeans; and (2) genotype predicts enzyme capacity, not symptoms — lactose intolerance symptoms depend on dose, gut microbiome, and gastrointestinal comorbidity, so a hydrogen breath test or dietary trial often outperforms genotyping for the clinical question.

### ALDH2*2 (rs671) and Alcohol Flushing / Esophageal Cancer

Aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) detoxifies the carcinogen acetaldehyde produced during ethanol metabolism. The rs671 G>A variant (Glu487Lys; the *2 allele) inactivates the enzyme and is highly prevalent in East Asian populations — roughly 540 million people, ~8% of the global population, carry it [10, mechanism_review]. Carriers experience the alcohol "flushing" response (facial flushing, tachycardia, nausea) from acetaldehyde accumulation, and that accumulation is genotoxic. In heterozygous *2 carriers who drink, reported odds ratios for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma range from 3.7 to 18.1, with many studies exceeding OR 10 in heavy drinkers [10, mechanism_review]. The clinical translation here is unusually concrete and actionable for a "wellness" variant: a two-question survey about flushing detects ALDH2 deficiency with ~90% sensitivity and ~88% specificity, and the recommendation is for clinicians to ask about flushing and counsel deficient heavy drinkers to reduce alcohol intake [10, mechanism_review]. ALDH2*2 thus sits at the actionable end of the nutrigenomic spectrum — but note the action is "drink less," a recommendation whose value does not actually require the genotype, only the phenotype.

## 3. Overinterpretation Caveats — The Load-Bearing Message

The defining failure mode of consumer-facing nutrigenomics is mistaking a statistically significant population association for actionable personal advice. Four principles must accompany every common-variant result a genetics agent reports.

First, effect sizes are small. The FTO example is definitive: rs9939609 explains under 1% of BMI variance, and the full set of known BMI variants captures only ~6% [11, mechanism_review]. Vitamin-D-related variants similarly leave heritability estimates near 7.5% [11, mechanism_review]. A per-allele effect of 0.36 kg/m² is real in a cohort of tens of thousands and invisible in an individual.

Second, gene–environment interaction dominates. The CYP1A2–coffee, APOE–saturated fat, and ALDH2–alcohol findings are all interactions: the genotype matters only conditional on an exposure, and the exposure (and behavior change) is what carries the clinical weight [6, cohort; 8, mechanism_review; 10, mechanism_review]. For most diet-related disease risk, environmental and lifestyle factors substantially outweigh genetic predisposition [11, mechanism_review].

Third, ancestry affects both allele frequency and effect. HLA-B*15:02 ranges from ~7% in East Asians to under 1% in Europeans [2, mechanism_review]; the lactase-persistence SNP rs4988235 is European-specific [9, cohort]; ALDH2*2 is concentrated in East Asians [10, mechanism_review]. Applying a single-population variant panel to a different ancestry produces systematic misclassification.

Fourth, the gap between association and advice is where DTC reports fail. An overview of DTC nutrigenetic companies found only 35% (16 of 45) clearly disclosed which genes they tested, only 8 gave unambiguous variant identifiers, and roughly half of disclosed variants lacked any genome-wide-significant association with a nutritional trait [11, mechanism_review]. The genetic component of such reports frequently adds little or nothing over generic lifestyle advice [11, mechanism_review]. The MTHFR case is the professional-society capstone: ACMG explicitly recommends against the test for its most common indications [5, mechanism_review], yet it remains among the most frequently overinterpreted results in consumer reports.

The operational rule for a genetics agent: nutrigenomic associations are not diagnoses and are rarely actionable in isolation. A common-variant result should change behavior only when it (a) modifies an exposure the person actually has, (b) points to advice with an independent evidence base, and (c) has been confirmed in the relevant ancestry. Any clinically consequential finding — HLA risk alleles, G6PD status, APOE — requires confirmation through accredited clinical testing and professional interpretation, never a consumer report alone. The HLA/G6PD domain shows what a genuinely actionable pharmacogene looks like (regulatory contraindications, society-mandated pre-prescription testing); the nutrigenomic domain shows how easily that bar is rhetorically borrowed and clinically unmet.
