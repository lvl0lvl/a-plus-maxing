# Section D — Disease-Risk Variants, Genetic-Counseling Governance & Data Ethics

Goal-agnostic canonical reference material for a clinical genetics & pharmacogenomics specialist agent; NOT personalized. Its spine is a set of safety floors: a risk variant is not a disease (penetrance and expressivity govern what happens to a carrier); disease-risk findings route to a certified genetic counselor and a physician, never to agent self-management; direct-to-consumer (DTC) raw genotype calls are not diagnostic and require clinical-grade confirmation; and genetic data is uniquely sensitive and immutable. Every numerical estimate below is a population-level figure from a human cohort or consensus guideline — not a personal prediction.

## 1. High-Penetrance Disease-Risk Variants and Management Standards

### 1.1 BRCA1/BRCA2 Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancer (HBOC)

The single best penetrance benchmark is the International BRCA1/2 Carrier Cohort prospective study by Kuchenbaecker and colleagues, which followed 6,036 BRCA1 and 3,820 BRCA2 female carriers [1, cohort]. Cumulative breast cancer risk to age 80 was 72% (95% CI, 65%–79%) for BRCA1 and 69% (95% CI, 61%–77%) for BRCA2 carriers [1, cohort]. Cumulative ovarian cancer risk to age 80 was markedly gene-specific: 44% (95% CI, 36%–53%) for BRCA1 versus 17% (95% CI, 11%–25%) for BRCA2 [1, cohort]. Contralateral breast cancer risk at 20 years after a first diagnosis was 40% (95% CI, 35%–45%) for BRCA1 and 26% (95% CI, 20%–33%) for BRCA2 [1, cohort]. Critically, this same study showed risk is modulated by family history and by the position of the variant within the gene — i.e., penetrance is not a single fixed number but a distribution shaped by genetic and familial context [1, cohort]. This is the operational meaning of "a risk variant is not a disease": a pathogenic BRCA1 variant confers a high lifetime probability, not certainty, and the realized risk for any individual depends on modifiers a single genotype cannot capture.

Management of confirmed pathogenic BRCA1/BRCA2 variants follows NCCN-aligned standards synthesized in the clinical literature [2, mechanism_review]. Unaffected carriers are offered enhanced surveillance — annual breast MRI plus mammography, with MRI typically beginning around age 25–30 — alongside risk-reducing options [2, mechanism_review]. Risk-reducing mastectomy lowers breast cancer risk by more than 90% [2, mechanism_review]. Risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy reduces ovarian cancer mortality and is recommended at age 35–40 for BRCA1 carriers and 40–45 for BRCA2 carriers once childbearing is complete, reflecting the earlier and higher ovarian risk in BRCA1 [2, mechanism_review]. These decisions are surgical and irreversible and belong to a multidisciplinary team — the agent surfaces the evidence and the referral, it does not direct the surgical choice.

**The DTC false-reassurance hazard.** The 23andMe BRCA report received FDA authorization in 2018 to report only three BRCA founder variants — 185delAG and 5382insC in BRCA1, and 6174delT in BRCA2 [3, regulatory]. These three account for roughly 87% of BRCA1 and 93% of BRCA2 pathogenic variants in people of Ashkenazi Jewish descent, but a small minority of pathogenic BRCA variants in people of other ancestries, out of more than a thousand known pathogenic BRCA variants [3, regulatory]. A "no variants detected" DTC result therefore does NOT rule out HBOC, particularly for non-Ashkenazi individuals — it is a false-reassurance trap. The agent must treat a negative DTC BRCA report as non-informative for clinical exclusion and route anyone with a concerning personal or family history to clinical-grade testing regardless of the DTC result.

### 1.2 Lynch Syndrome (Mismatch-Repair Genes MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM)

Lynch syndrome is caused by pathogenic germline variants in the DNA mismatch-repair (MMR) genes MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2; EPCAM deletions silence the adjacent MSH2 gene and produce the same phenotype [4, mechanism_review]. Cancer risk is strongly gene-specific. MLH1 and MSH2 carriers carry a lifetime colorectal cancer risk approaching 50%, which remains elevated even under regular colonoscopy surveillance, whereas MSH6 and PMS2 carriers have substantially lower colorectal risk — in PMS2 carriers the surveillance-adjusted risk may become very low [4, mechanism_review]. Endometrial and ovarian cancer risks are also elevated for the higher-penetrance genes [4, mechanism_review].

Surveillance is the cornerstone of management and is also gene-stratified [4, mechanism_review]. Colonoscopy generally begins at age 20–25 (or five years younger than the youngest affected relative) and repeats every 1–2 years for MLH1/MSH2 carriers; for the lower-risk MSH6 and PMS2 genes, later initiation (around 35 and 40 years, respectively) and longer 3-year intervals can be considered [4, mechanism_review]. Risk-reducing hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is offered to MLH1, MSH2, and MSH6 carriers after about age 40 [4, mechanism_review]. The teaching point: "Lynch syndrome" is not a single risk — the responsible gene materially changes both the magnitude of risk and the surveillance schedule, and that nuance must survive into any counseling-referral summary.

### 1.3 APOE-ε4 and Alzheimer's Risk — A Risk Modifier, Not a Determinant

APOE-ε4 is the paradigm case of a susceptibility allele that is routinely overinterpreted as deterministic. It raises Alzheimer's disease risk but does not cause the disease: many ε4 carriers never develop dementia and many non-carriers do. The REVEAL study — a prospective randomized controlled trial of 162 asymptomatic adults with an affected parent, randomized to receive or not receive their APOE genotype — anchors the counseling evidence [5, rct]. At 6 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year, there were no significant differences between disclosure and non-disclosure groups in anxiety (4.5 vs 4.4; P=0.84), depression (8.8 vs 8.7; P=0.98), or test-related distress (6.9 vs 7.5; P=0.61) [5, rct]. However, ε4-negative participants had significantly lower test-related distress than ε4-positive participants (P=0.01) — i.e., the result content, not merely the act of testing, carries psychological weight [5, rct]. The governance implication is twofold: APOE risk should be framed as a probabilistic modifier within a polygenic and environmental context (never as a diagnosis or a verdict), and disclosure of a predictive-but-non-actionable result is a counseling event, not a data readout. APOE is notably NOT on the ACMG secondary-findings list precisely because it is not a high-penetrance, medically actionable monogenic finding (see §2).

### 1.4 Hereditary Thrombophilia — Factor V Leiden and Prothrombin G20210A

Factor V Leiden (F5; rs6025) and prothrombin G20210A (F2; rs1799963) are the two most common inherited thrombophilias. Heterozygosity for either confers roughly a two- to three-fold increase in venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk — a modest absolute increase on a low baseline [6, mechanism_review]. A large analysis combining FinnGen and the UK Biobank (N=938,355) found that double heterozygotes (carrying one variant in each gene) had a VTE odds ratio of 5.24 (95% CI, 4.01–6.84), approximating the risk conferred by Factor V Leiden homozygosity and remaining elevated (OR 4.53; 95% CI, 3.42–5.90) after adjustment for other VTE risk factors [7, cohort]. The clinical-governance nuance is that professional bodies are deliberately ambivalent about routine thrombophilia testing: because heterozygous carriage confers only a modest absolute risk and rarely changes anticoagulation duration or management, indiscriminate testing can generate anxiety, insurance concerns, and overtreatment without clear benefit [6, mechanism_review]. A DTC report of Factor V Leiden is therefore a classic "interesting but usually low-actionability" finding that belongs in a clinician conversation, not in agent-initiated management.

### 1.5 Familial Hypercholesterolemia (LDLR/APOB/PCSK9) — High Actionability, Underdiagnosed

Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is the inverse of thrombophilia on the actionability axis: high lifetime cardiovascular risk, highly treatable, and badly underdiagnosed. FH is caused chiefly by pathogenic variants in LDLR (most common), APOB, and PCSK9, and has a population prevalence on the order of 1 in 250–300 [8, cohort]. Penetrance is incomplete and modifiable — in one large biobank cohort, carriers of pathogenic/likely-pathogenic LDLR/APOB variants had about a 1.3-fold higher risk of ischemic heart disease, and only ~10.5% of carriers had a prior FH diagnosis [8, cohort]. The dual message for the agent: FH is one of the most clinically valuable disease-risk findings to surface because early lipid-lowering therapy substantially reduces cardiovascular events, AND its underdiagnosis means a carrier may have lived undetected — but the diagnosis and statin/PCSK9-inhibitor decision belong to a physician, not the agent. FH is exactly why the ACMG secondary-findings program exists.

## 2. ACMG Secondary Findings (SF v3.x) — Reporting Norms for Clinical Sequencing

When a patient undergoes clinical exome or genome sequencing for some unrelated indication, the ACMG recommends that laboratories also examine a defined minimum gene list for pathogenic/likely-pathogenic variants and report those "secondary findings" (SF), because they are highly penetrant and medically actionable [9, mechanism_review]. The SF v3.2 policy statement (Miller et al., Genetics in Medicine, 2023) added CALM1, CALM2, and CALM3 (long-QT/CPVT phenotypes) to the list [9, mechanism_review]. The list is organized into cancer, cardiovascular, inborn-errors-of-metabolism, and miscellaneous phenotype categories and includes the disease-risk genes discussed above — BRCA1/BRCA2, the Lynch MMR genes, and the FH genes LDLR/APOB/PCSK9 — while pointedly excluding low-penetrance susceptibility alleles such as APOE-ε4 [9, mechanism_review]. ACMG explicitly frames SF reporting as "neither a replacement for indication-based diagnostic clinical genetic testing nor a form of population screening," and the program supports patient opt-out of receiving secondary findings [9, mechanism_review]. The list is versioned and updated periodically (e.g., the subsequent SF v3.3 revision), so an agent must treat the specific gene set as a living, version-stamped artifact and defer to the laboratory's current SF version rather than a memorized list. Two governance points follow directly: ACMG secondary findings are reported only from clinical-grade sequencing (not DTC raw data), and every reported SF triggers a return-of-results counseling pathway — it is never delivered as a bare data line.

## 3. Genetic-Counseling Governance — When Referral Is the Standard of Care

For any disease-risk finding, referral to a certified genetic counselor working with a physician is the standard of care, structured around pre-test and post-test counseling. The National Society of Genetic Counselors (NSGC) defines the components of informed consent for genetic testing across ten essential elements: the purpose of the test and whom to test; background on the gene(s); the possible results; technical accuracy and limitations; economic considerations; the possibility of genetic discrimination; psychosocial aspects; confidentiality; how results will be used; and the alternatives to testing [10, mechanism_review]. Pre-test counseling sets expectations and obtains informed consent; post-test counseling interprets the result in the context of personal and family history, addresses penetrance/expressivity uncertainty, and translates the result into a management and cascade-testing plan [10, mechanism_review].

The reason this is a hard referral boundary for a non-clinician agent is the same set of facts that make these findings consequential: the results are predictive, familial (a carrier result implicates blood relatives), often irreversible in their management implications (prophylactic surgery), and psychologically loaded. The agent's correct role is to recognize when a finding falls into the disease-risk class, explain at a literacy-appropriate level why a certified genetic counselor + MD is warranted, and facilitate that referral — not to deliver a risk verdict, not to recommend surveillance schedules as if authorizing them, and not to initiate cascade testing of relatives.

## 4. Data Ethics & Governance (Load-Bearing)

### 4.1 GINA — What It Covers and Its Gaps

The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 (GINA) is the principal U.S. federal protection against genetic discrimination [11, regulatory]. Title I prohibits health insurers from using genetic information to determine eligibility, coverage, underwriting, or premiums, and bars them from requesting or requiring genetic testing [11, regulatory]. Title II prohibits employers from using genetic information in hiring, firing, promotion, pay, or job assignment, and bars them from requesting genetic information [11, regulatory]. The GINA gaps are as load-bearing as its protections: GINA does NOT cover life insurance, disability insurance, or long-term-care insurance — these insurers may, in many states, use genetic information in underwriting [11, regulatory]. GINA also does not apply to employers with fewer than 15 employees, and the U.S. military is exempt [11, regulatory]. A patient-facing summary that says "you are protected by GINA" without naming these gaps is materially misleading; the agent must state both the protection and its boundaries so a person weighing whether to test understands the residual exposure (e.g., that a positive disease-risk finding could affect a future life-insurance application).

### 4.2 Genetic Exceptionalism and DTC Data-Privacy

Genetic exceptionalism is the principle that genetic information warrants unique protection because of distinctive properties: it is predictive of future health states, inherently familial (it reveals information about blood relatives who did not consent), immutable (it cannot be changed and is not chosen), and usable as a unique lifelong identifier [12, mechanism_review]. There is a live scholarly counter-position — "genomic contextualism" — arguing that the implications of any given test should be weighed case-by-case rather than treating all genetic data as uniformly exceptional [12, mechanism_review]. For the agent's purposes the practical posture is conservative: treat genetic data as uniquely sensitive, recognize that DTC genetic data sits outside HIPAA-covered clinical custody and is governed largely by the company's own (changeable) privacy policy and terms of service, and never minimize the privacy stakes of uploading raw DNA data to third-party interpretation services. The reference knowledge here is goal-agnostic; the personalization (whose data, what the operator wants) happens at dispatch, not in this library.

### 4.3 The DTC → Clinical-Grade Confirmation Pathway

Any actionable finding sourced from DTC raw data must be confirmed by a clinical-grade laboratory before it changes medical management. The empirical foundation is Tandy-Connor et al., who found that 40% of variants reported in DTC raw data were false positives upon clinical confirmation, and that several variants flagged as "increased risk" by DTC raw data or third-party interpretation services were classified as benign by clinical laboratories [13, cohort]. The DTC raw-data file is a research-grade genotyping artifact, not a validated clinical result. The mandatory pathway is therefore: DTC raw call → recognize it is unconfirmed → route to clinical-grade confirmatory testing (CLIA/CAP-certified lab) ordered through a clinician → only then does the result inform management. The agent must never present a raw DTC genotype as a diagnosis, and must never let a DTC result — positive OR negative — drive a management decision on its own.

### 4.4 EMERGENCY-Class Finding Routing for a Non-Clinician Agent

A subset of incidental/secondary findings are time-sensitive — for example, a pathogenic variant in an arrhythmia gene (long-QT, CPVT, including the CALM1–3 genes added to ACMG SF v3.2) or a cardiomyopathy gene, where the carrier could be at risk of a catastrophic event before routine follow-up [9, mechanism_review]. The routing rule for a non-clinician agent is layered and absolute:

1. **Never self-manage.** The agent does not diagnose, does not initiate treatment or surveillance, and does not adjust any medication on the basis of a genetic finding.
2. **Confirm provenance.** If the finding is from DTC raw data, it is presumptively unconfirmed and requires clinical-grade confirmation (§4.3) — but provenance uncertainty does not justify ignoring a potentially serious signal.
3. **Standard disease-risk findings → genetic counselor + MD referral.** This is the default for BRCA, Lynch, FH, and the rest of the disease-risk class (§3).
4. **Urgent / EMERGENCY-class findings → escalate.** For findings carrying acute risk (e.g., a high-penetrance arrhythmia or aortopathy variant), the agent escalates promptly to clinical care rather than queuing a routine referral, and clearly communicates the urgency without itself rendering a clinical verdict.

The unifying floor: a genetics specialist agent is a literacy-and-routing layer over an inherently clinician-governed domain. It states penetrance honestly, never converts a risk allele into a diagnosis, confirms DTC provenance, refers disease-risk findings to a genetic counselor and physician, and escalates emergencies — keeping the consequential decision with the people licensed and counseled to make it.
