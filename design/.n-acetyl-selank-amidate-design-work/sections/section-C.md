## Section C — Modification rationale, the Semax sibling & stabilized-analog class context

### C.1 The N-Acetyl + C-Amidate Modification Rationale

**General peptide chemistry basis.** The N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate analog carries two terminal modifications relative to its parent: N-terminal acetylation (an acetyl group replacing the free amine at the N-terminus) and C-terminal amidation (conversion of the terminal carboxyl group to a primary amide). Both are standard medicinal-chemistry tools for reducing exopeptidolytic susceptibility. Aminopeptidases — serine proteases that cleave from the free N-terminal amino group — are blocked when that group is acetylated, because the acetyl moiety sterically and chemically prevents substrate recognition [1, vendor_label]. Carboxypeptidases likewise rely on the free C-terminal carboxylate for binding; amidation removes this recognition element and substantially retards hydrolysis [1, vendor_label].

Quantitative data from the broader peptide-modification literature illustrate the scale of the effect. A PLOS ONE stability study using short antimicrobial peptide analogs in human serum found that N-terminal acetylation extended half-life approximately 2-fold relative to unmodified controls (from <0.5 h to ~1 h for hexamers), while the doubly modified (Ac-peptide-NH2) form achieved ~1.5 h — a larger-than-additive benefit; the same study noted that C-terminal amidation alone did not consistently protect against carboxypeptidase activity in human serum, underscoring that sequence context matters [2, in_vitro]. A separate PMC-indexed study on acetylated supramolecular peptide nanofilaments (ACS Biomaterials Science & Engineering, 2023; PMID 37192486) reported dramatically extended plasma half-lives for anionic acetylated sequences (8.6–20.7 h vs. rapid degradation of non-acetylated forms), with the protective effect appearing charge-dependent rather than universal [3, in_vitro]. These figures are for structurally distinct peptides; they establish the mechanistic plausibility and order of magnitude for the class, not a measured value for Selank's analog.

**Critical gap: no analog-specific PK data exist.** There are no published pharmacokinetic or stability studies specifically measuring the half-life, plasma stability, mucosal permeability, or BBB penetration of N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate as a distinct compound. Commercial and grey-market descriptions of the analog describe the modification benefits in general peptide-chemistry terms without citing — or conducting — any original measurement on the acetylated/amidated Selank sequence itself. The claim that N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate produces longer half-life or enhanced bioavailability relative to Selank is mechanistically reasonable given the general literature above, but it is a class-level expectation extrapolated to this specific molecule, not an empirically demonstrated pharmacokinetic fact [4, vendor_label]. The closest relevant data point for the parent compound is a rat pharmacokinetics study (PMID 9854633) on a related Russian tuftsin-analog heptapeptide — detecting the compound in plasma for 7–10 minutes — conducted by Boyko, Zherdev, Myasoedov et al. at the Institute of Pharmacology, Russian Academy of Medical Sciences [5, animal]. Even for the parent Selank itself, intranasal PK data referenced widely by compounding and vendor sources (claimed ~92% bioavailability; 20–30 min half-life) trace to unpublished or Russian-language data not independently verified in indexed English-language journals.

The modification rationale for N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate is therefore: theoretically sound by general peptide-chemistry principles, consistent with the established design logic used in the broader stabilized-analog class, and entirely unsupported by analog-specific published PK evidence.

### C.2 Parent Selank's Own Stability Design

Before the grey-market modification, the originating researchers at the Institute of Molecular Genetics already built metabolic stabilization into Selank's backbone. The parent compound Thr-Lys-Pro-Arg-Pro-Gly-Pro appends a Pro-Gly-Pro tripeptide to the C-terminus of natural tuftsin (Thr-Lys-Pro-Arg) precisely to resist aminopeptidase attack on the N-terminus and to slow carboxypeptidase access to the C-terminus via the proline-terminated tail. Volkova, Shadrina, Myasoedov et al. (Frontiers in Pharmacology, 2016; PMID 26924987) describe Selank as "elongated at the C terminus via the addition of three natural L-amino acids" to "improve its metabolic stability and yield a relatively longer duration" [6, animal]. All seven listed authors are affiliated with the Institute of Molecular Genetics, Russian Academy of Sciences, Moscow — no Western co-authors. The N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate modification layers on top of this already-stabilized backbone, offering incremental exopeptidase protection at both termini; the net expected half-life gain is real but modest, given that the internal peptide bonds remain the dominant degradation pathway for a compound already equipped with proline-rich termini.

### C.3 The Semax Sibling and the Russian "Regulatory Peptide" Class

**Class origin.** Selank and Semax are the two flagship compounds from the same Soviet-era research program at the Institute of Molecular Genetics (then the Research Institute of Molecular Genetics of the Academy of Sciences of the USSR). The program ran under the direction of academicians Igor Ashmarin and Nikolai Myasoedov, beginning in the mid-1980s [7, mechanism_review]. The intellectual framework was the "regulatory peptide" or "neuropeptide analog" paradigm: identify a short naturally occurring regulatory peptide with established biological activity, then engineer a modified sequence that resists enzymatic clearance while preserving or enhancing receptor interaction. Deygin, Poluektova et al. (Pharmaceutics, 2022; PMID 35456550) document Russia's disproportionate contribution to peptide biopharmaceuticals under this paradigm — approximately 14 of the roughly 70 global registered peptide drugs (~20%) — with Selank (anxiolytic) and Semax (nootropic/neuroprotective) listed among the 14 Russian-registered peptide drugs [7, mechanism_review].

**Semax specifically.** Semax (Met-Glu-His-Phe-Pro-Gly-Pro) is an analog of the ACTH(4-10)/ACTH(4-7) fragment, not of tuftsin. It has a distinct pharmacological profile — primarily nootropic/cognitive stimulating, with BDNF upregulation as a proposed key mechanism — distinguishing it from Selank's anxiolytic/immunomodulatory profile. Like Selank, it ends in the Pro-Gly-Pro stabilizing tail. Semax carries Russian and Ukrainian regulatory approval for indications including ischemic stroke, encephalopathy, and optic nerve atrophy; Selank has completed Phase III clinical trials in Russia for generalised anxiety disorder. Neither is FDA-approved or approved by the EMA [7, mechanism_review]. A published study on N-terminal acetylation of Semax (Journal of Inorganic Biochemistry, 2016; PMID 27586814; Magrì et al.) investigated the structural consequences of acetylating Semax's free N-terminus — finding altered copper(II) coordination geometry and loss of redox reactivity — but contained no pharmacokinetic data and did not test the stabilization hypothesis directly [8, in_vitro]. Semax's evidence record should be attributed to Semax; it does not transfer to the Selank analog.

**The grey-market "N-Acetyl-...-Amidate" naming convention.** The commercial research-chemical market has spawned terminally modified analogs of both flagship compounds — N-Acetyl-Semax-Amidate and N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate — sold under this explicit naming convention to signal the stabilization modifications. Both are positioned as "enhanced stability" variants of the registered Russian medicines, available as research chemicals in the West where neither the parent compound nor the analog holds regulatory approval. No published peer-reviewed literature characterizes N-Acetyl-Semax-Amidate or N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate as distinct chemical entities; they are described exclusively by vendor literature, compounding data sheets, and grey-market commercial sources [4, vendor_label]. The naming convention is a grey-market invention, not a nomenclature adopted by the originating Russian research groups.

### C.4 Independent Replication and Western-Acceptance Assessment

The Selank/Semax evidence base has a defining structural problem: near-total concentration in the originating Russian research group. The gene-expression study (PMID 26924987 / PMC4757669) lists seven authors, all from the Institute of Molecular Genetics, with no Western co-investigators [6, animal]. The pharmacokinetics work on the parent tuftsin-analog heptapeptide (PMID 9854633) originates from the Russian Academy of Medical Sciences [5, animal]. The comprehensive review of Russian peptide biopharmaceuticals (PMID 35456550) is itself authored by Russian researchers and does not discuss external replication [7, mechanism_review]. The pattern is consistent across the body of work.

Western independent replication of the Selank/Semax evidence is essentially absent. Neither compound has undergone a randomized controlled trial conducted under FDA or EMA standards, in a Western patient population, with independent data monitoring. The "regulatory peptide" paradigm as a scientific framework — the notion that short tuftsin or ACTH fragments with Pro-Gly-Pro stabilization produce clinically meaningful anxiolysis or nootropic effects — is not a recognized mainstream-Western pharmacological construct; it has not been adopted into any Western drug development program or clinical guideline [4, vendor_label]. This does not constitute proof of inefficacy; Soviet-era and Russian peptide pharmacology were ahead of Western research priorities in certain domains, and the mechanistic hypotheses (GABAergic modulation, BDNF upregulation, enkephalin regulation) are scientifically plausible. But absence of independent replication is a real epistemic limitation, and holding the Russian clinical evidence with proportionate uncertainty is the correct stance.

For the N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate analog specifically, the evidentiary situation is a level below even the parent: there are no clinical or preclinical published studies on the analog as a distinct entity, no independent PK data, and no regulatory characterization anywhere in the world. All claims about its superiority to the parent compound rest on the general peptide-chemistry expectation that terminal modifications reduce exopeptidolytic clearance — a reasonable but unverified extrapolation in this specific molecular context.
