## Section B — Anxiolytic / nootropic clinical evidence (the Selank corpus)

### B.1 Overview and framing

Selank (Thr-Lys-Pro-Arg-Pro-Gly-Pro / TKPRPGP, a synthetic heptapeptide tuftsin analog) was developed at the Institute of Molecular Genetics of the Russian Academy of Sciences under Nikolai F. Myasoedov and was approved for clinical use in Russia and Ukraine under the trade name Selank (0.15% intranasal solution). The human evidence base for anxiolytic and nootropic effects belongs entirely to the **Selank parent compound**. N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate — the grey-market modification in which the N-terminus is acetylated and the C-terminus amidated to resist enzymatic degradation — has **zero published clinical trials and zero published preclinical studies of its own**. The sole rationale for the analog's use is pharmacokinetic extrapolation from the parent, not demonstrated efficacy. This distinction must be held throughout the section.

---

### B.2 The Selank parent: human clinical evidence

#### B.2.1 Foundational comparative efficacy study (Zozulya et al., 2008)

The most-cited primary efficacy study enrolled **62 adults** (30 receiving Selank, 32 receiving medazepam) with generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) and neurasthenia [1]. This was conducted in Russia, published in Russian (Zhurnal Nevrologii i Psikhiatrii, a specialist Russian-language psychiatry journal), and assessed patient state with the Hamilton, Zung, and CGI scales alongside serum enkephalin activity. Selank was administered intranasally. Both drugs produced comparable anxiolytic effects on rating scales, but Selank additionally demonstrated antiasthenic and mild psychostimulant effects not seen with medazepam — consistent with a nootropic profile. Patients with GAD and neurasthenia showed reduced leu-enkephalin half-life at baseline, which normalized more under Selank than medazepam, suggesting inhibition of enkephalin-degrading enzymes as one mechanistic thread [1].

**Critical caveats:** The study is Russian, published in Russian, relatively small (n=62), and was not described as double-blinded in its indexed form. "Randomized" is claimed but blinding methodology is not independently verifiable from the indexed abstract. It was conducted within the Russian regulatory framework for the compound's eventual approval — a context that carries its own publication incentive structure. [1, rct]

#### B.2.2 Selank vs. phenazepam comparative trial (Medvedev et al., 2014)

A second comparative clinical trial enrolled **60 patients** with phobic-anxiety and somatoform disorders (ICD-10: F40.2–9, F41.1–9, F45.0–1), spanning adolescents through middle age, comparing intranasal Selank to phenazepam (a Russian benzodiazepine with higher potency than medazepam) [2]. Published in the same Zh Nevrol Psikhiatr journal in 2014, authors include Medvedev, Kost, Sokolov, and Myasoedov (the inventor). Key findings: Selank demonstrated pronounced anxiolytic and mild nootropic effects, and the anxiolytic benefit persisted approximately one week after the last dose — notable for a short-half-life peptide and consistent with downstream neuroadaptation rather than receptor occupancy. Positive effect on quality-of-life metrics was reported. [2, open_label]

**Limitations:** n=60; Russian-language publication; Myasoedov (inventor/patent holder) is an author, creating conflict-of-interest exposure; blinding not described as double-blind; the open-label design means expectation bias cannot be excluded for subjective scale endpoints. The one-week post-dose persistence finding comes from this open-label, inventor-co-authored study: it is consistent with neuroadaptation, but "neuroadaptation" here is a **hypothesis**, not a demonstrated mechanism — expectation/non-specific effects and regression to mean in an unblinded study are equally plausible explanations for sustained subjective improvement. [2, open_label]

#### B.2.3 EEG characterization of response subpopulations (Syunyakov, Teleshova, Neznamov et al., 2012)

A pharmaco-EEG study in **20 adults aged 24–52** with DSM-IV GAD characterised two response subtypes during Selank treatment (intranasal, 2700 µg/day) [3]. Forty percent of patients showed rapid response (within 1–3 days), with HARS total score reducing from ~20 to ~7 (p<0.01); 60% showed gradual response by day 14. Rapid responders had greater baseline asthenic and cognitive symptom burden and stronger EEG reactivity to a single dose. This study provides mechanistic phenotyping rather than a placebo-controlled efficacy test. Published as a conference abstract in *European Psychiatry* (the journal of the European Psychiatric Association) — an indexed Western-European-outlet abstract, which is a higher-visibility signal than the Russian-language journals for this corpus. The record is indexed in Crossref (DOI confirmed: title, authors T. Syunyakov, E.S. Teleshova, G.G. Neznamov, V.K. Bochkarev, year 2012 — all match); full text is paywalled via Elsevier and is not retrievable via open routes. [3, open_label]

**Limitations:** n=20; open-label; no placebo arm; conference abstract format limits methodological transparency; full text unavailable via open access (Crossref-indexed but Elsevier-paywalled). [3, open_label]

#### B.2.4 Immunomodulatory and anxiety-asthenic clinical study (Uchakina et al., 2008)

A clinical study in patients with **anxiety-asthenic disorders** examined Selank's immunomodulatory effects alongside anxiolytic properties, documenting modulation of interleukin-6 (IL-6) expression and Th1/Th2 cytokine balance shift [4]. Published in Zh Nevrol Psikhiatr Im S.S. Korsakova, 2008;108(5):71–75, PMID 18577961. This study extends the mechanistic rationale for Selank's bi-directional effects (anxiolytic + immunomodulatory) in the same clinical population that the efficacy trials targeted. Population: Russian adults with anxiety-asthenic diagnoses; **n: unconfirmed** — sample size is not reported in the PubMed abstract and was not retrievable from open-access routes at time of research; secondary sources do not specify it. [4, open_label]

### B.3 Selank parent: preclinical and mechanistic evidence (context for clinical claims)

**Source quality note (refs [5], [6], [7]):** The three preclinical studies in this section are published in Frontiers in Pharmacology or Behavioural Neurology (a Hindawi/Wiley open-access journal). These are indexed and peer-reviewed, but per the source whitelist, `frontiersin.org` and similar megajournals are flagged as LOWER-TRUST relative to specialty journals (high acceptance rates, variable review depth). No claim in this section rests solely on these sources; they serve as mechanistic context for the better-powered Russian clinical corpus, and they are relabeled tier-3 in the bibliography accordingly. Any single-source mechanistic claim sourced only from these studies should be treated with appropriate caution.

#### B.3.1 GABAergic mechanism — in vivo rat study (Volkova et al., 2016)

In 30 male Wistar rats divided into control, Selank, and GABA groups (n=10 each), Selank administration produced significant changes in frontal cortex gene expression at 1 and 3 hours post-dose, including 45-gene expression shift at 1 h [5]. Strong positive correlation (r=0.86) between Selank and GABA effects at 1 h supports GABAergic system modulation rather than direct GABA binding. Published in Frontiers in Pharmacology, 2016 (PMID: 26924987; DOI: 10.3389/fphar.2016.00031). Indexed and open-access; lower-trust open-access outlet (see section note above). [5, animal]

**Note:** this is a rodent study — it informs the mechanistic hypothesis for the human clinical findings but does not establish human efficacy. [5, animal]

#### B.3.2 GABAergic mechanism — in vitro neuroblastoma cells (Filatova et al., 2017)

In IMR-32 human neuroblastoma cells expressing GABAA receptors, Selank alone produced no changes in GABAergic gene mRNA levels, but combined Selank + GABA largely suppressed GABA-driven gene expression changes; combined Selank + olanzapine amplified olanzapine's gene expression effects [6]. Published in Frontiers in Pharmacology, 2017 (PMID: 28293190; DOI: 10.3389/fphar.2017.00089). This is in vitro — provides receptor-level mechanistic context but does not translate directly to clinical dosing or human outcomes; lower-trust open-access outlet (see section note above). [6, in_vitro]

#### B.3.3 Rat diazepam interaction study (Kasian et al., 2017)

In 48 male Wistar rats under unpredictable chronic mild stress, combined Selank + diazepam restored anxiety levels to baseline while individual drugs produced partial effects; Selank alone was most effective at reducing drug-administration anxiety [7]. Published in Behavioural Neurology, 2017 (PMC5322660; DOI: 10.1155/2017/5091027; PMID: 28280289). Open-access Hindawi/Wiley journal; lower-trust open-access outlet (see section note above). [7, animal]

### B.4 Mechanism review (Vyunova et al., 2018)

Vyunova, Andreeva, Shevchenko, and Myasoedov published a mechanism review in *Protein and Peptide Letters* (2018, Vol. 25, No. 10, pp. 914–923; DOI: 10.2174/0929866525666180925144642; PMID: 30255741), titled "Peptide-based Anxiolytics: The Molecular Aspects of Heptapeptide Selank Biological Activity" [8]. This review synthesizes the pharmacokinetics, receptor interactions (GABAergic, serotonergic, enkephalinergic), and the structural basis of Selank's activity. It is the primary English-language mechanism review for this compound family, though Myasoedov is again an author. [8, mechanism_review]

### B.5 The analog gap: N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate has no direct evidence

A systematic search across PubMed (captcha-blocked; cross-referenced via EuropePMC API), Semantic Scholar, Europe PMC, and vendor/grey-literature sources finds **no indexed clinical trials, no preclinical published studies, and no regulatory filings** for N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate as a distinct chemical entity.

The vendor and grey-market literature explicitly acknowledges this gap. Vendor claims for the analog state it "is expected to have similar effects and potentially enhanced benefits compared to Selank" and that "studies on N-Acetyl Selank are lacking, but data from Selank's research may be used as a reference." Pharmacokinetic rationale for the modification is structural: N-terminal acetylation blocks aminopeptidase attack, C-terminal amidation prevents carboxypeptidase degradation, extending plasma half-life from ~20–30 minutes (Selank) to an estimated 200–300 minutes (5–10× increase) with 8–10× increased enzymatic resistance. These are chemical logic claims, not pharmacokinetic data from published studies in the analog itself.

The compounding pharmacy market (e.g., Simon's Compounding) sells N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate as an intranasal preparation. The legal context in the United States is grey: not FDA-approved, not scheduled, sold as a compounded research chemical.

**The honest summary:** any claim that N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate is anxiolytic or nootropic in humans is an extrapolation from the parent compound's literature. The extrapolation may be pharmacologically reasonable (the modification preserves the active core sequence while extending bioavailability), but it is not demonstrated. There is no clinical evidence tier for N-Acetyl-Selank-Amidate — the category is simply "absent."
