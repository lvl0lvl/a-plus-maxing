# Section B — Reference Ranges, Measurement & Determinants (LDL-C)

This section is goal-agnostic reference material on LDL-cholesterol (LDL-C) reference
ranges, measurement methodology, and the genetic, dietary, physiological, and
pharmacologic determinants of the LDL-C level. It is not personalized.

## Risk-stratified treatment goals

Modern lipid guidelines abandon a single population "normal" range for LDL-C and instead
set goals that fall as cardiovascular risk rises.

**2019 ESC/EAS (European Society of Cardiology / European Atherosclerosis Society).**
The adopted LDL-C goals are, by risk category: very-high risk **<55 mg/dL (<1.4 mmol/L)**
together with **≥50% reduction** from baseline [1, mechanism_review]; high risk
**<70 mg/dL (<1.8 mmol/L)** plus ≥50% reduction [1, mechanism_review]; moderate risk
**<100 mg/dL (<2.6 mmol/L)** [1, mechanism_review]; and low risk **<116 mg/dL
(<3.0 mmol/L)** to be considered [1, mechanism_review]. For patients with established
ASCVD who suffer a **second vascular event within 2 years** on maximally tolerated statin
therapy, a goal of **<40 mg/dL (<1.0 mmol/L)** may be considered [1, mechanism_review].
Risk-category definitions are explicit: very-high risk includes documented ASCVD, diabetes
with end-organ damage or three major risk factors, severe CKD (eGFR <30), HeFH with ASCVD
or another major risk factor, or SCORE ≥10%; high risk includes a markedly elevated single
risk factor (total cholesterol >310 mg/dL, LDL-C >190 mg/dL, or BP 180/110 mmHg), diabetes
>10 years, moderate CKD (eGFR 30–59), or SCORE 5–9% [1, mechanism_review].

**2018 AHA/ACC Multisociety blood cholesterol guideline.** Rather than fixed targets, this
guideline uses LDL-C **thresholds to trigger added therapy**. In very-high-risk ASCVD,
ezetimibe is reasonable when LDL-C remains **≥70 mg/dL** on maximally tolerated statin, and
a PCSK9 inhibitor is reasonable if LDL-C stays **≥70 mg/dL** on statin plus ezetimibe
[2, mechanism_review]. Severe primary hypercholesterolemia is defined as **LDL-C
≥190 mg/dL**: 10-year risk calculation is not required, maximally tolerated statin is
recommended, and the aim is to reduce LDL-C **to <100 mg/dL** (a ≥50% reduction)
[2, mechanism_review]. The US National Lipid Association (NLA) likewise treats LDL-C
≥190 mg/dL as a standalone high-risk trigger and endorses a <70 mg/dL goal for established
ASCVD, broadly tracking these thresholds.

## NCEP ATP III descriptive bands

The earlier NCEP Adult Treatment Panel III (ATP III, NHLBI) bands remain the source of the
"optimal / borderline / high" vocabulary still seen on lab reports. ATP III Table 2
classifies LDL-C (mg/dL) as: **<100 Optimal; 100–129 Near optimal/above optimal;
130–159 Borderline high; 160–189 High; ≥190 Very high** [3, regulatory]. The same table
gives total cholesterol as **<200 Desirable, 200–239 Borderline high, ≥240 High**, and HDL
as **<40 Low, ≥60 High** [3, regulatory]. These are descriptive population bands, not the
risk-stratified treatment goals above.

## Units and conversion

LDL-C is reported in **mg/dL** (US) or **mmol/L** (most of the world). The conversion is
**mg/dL ÷ 38.67 = mmol/L** (equivalently × 0.02586) [4, mechanism_review]. Thus the ESC/EAS
goals map as 55 mg/dL ≈ 1.4 mmol/L, 70 ≈ 1.8, 100 ≈ 2.6, 116 ≈ 3.0, and 40 ≈ 1.0
mmol/L [1, mechanism_review][4, mechanism_review].

## Measurement methodology

**Calculated LDL-C — Friedewald.** The default lab estimate is the Friedewald equation,
**LDL-C = TC − HDL-C − TG/5** (mg/dL) [4, mechanism_review]. It is **invalid when
triglycerides exceed 400 mg/dL** and in non-fasting samples (the TG/5 term assumes a fixed
fasting VLDL ratio), and it systematically **underestimates LDL-C at low values (<70 mg/dL)**
[4, mechanism_review].

**Martin-Hopkins.** This replaces the fixed divisor of 5 with an **adjustable factor F**,
selected from a 180-cell table of TG and non-HDL-C strata, with values **ranging 3.1 to 11.9**
[4, mechanism_review]. It is more accurate at low LDL-C and higher TG and was recommended by
the 2018 AHA/ACC guideline for LDL-C **<70 mg/dL** [5, cohort]. In a comparison of 111,939
patients, accuracy versus the reference method was **62.1% (Martin/Hopkins) vs 40.4%
(Sampson) vs 19.3% (Friedewald)** [5, cohort].

**Sampson/NIH equation 2.** A fixed (non-adjustable) formula,
**LDL-C = TC/0.948 − HDL-C/0.971 − [TG/8.56 + (TG × non-HDL-C)/2140 − TG²/16100] − 9.44**,
valid to roughly TG 800 mg/dL [4, mechanism_review]. Even so, neither Martin nor Sampson is
considered accurate enough to report LDL-C at **TG ≥400 mg/dL** [5, cohort].

**Direct / beta-quantification.** Direct enzymatic LDL-C assays and the reference
**beta-quantification** (preparative ultracentrifugation) are used when TG >400 mg/dL or in
non-fasting samples; beta-quantification is the gold standard but is expensive,
time-consuming, and impractical for routine use [4, mechanism_review].

**Fasting vs non-fasting.** Friedewald requires a fasting sample; non-fasting lipid panels
are increasingly accepted for screening, but a non-fasting TG >400 mg/dL still mandates a
direct LDL-C method [4, mechanism_review][5, cohort].

## Determinants of the LDL-C level

**Genetics — familial hypercholesterolemia (FH).** FH arises from pathogenic variants in
three principal genes: **LDLR** (the LDL receptor, ≥85% of cases, >1600 mutations), **APOB**
(defective apolipoprotein B, commonly the position-3500 mutation), and **PCSK9**
(gain-of-function variants) [6, mechanism_review]. **Heterozygous FH (HeFH)** typically
presents with untreated **LDL-C >190 mg/dL** and a prevalence of about **1 in 250**;
**homozygous FH (HoFH)** presents with **LDL-C >450 mg/dL** and a prevalence of about
**1 in 300,000**, reaching up to **1 in 100** in founder populations (French Canadians,
Lebanese, Afrikaners) [6, mechanism_review].

**Diet.** Saturated and trans fats and dietary cholesterol raise LDL-C; soluble fiber lowers
it. A dose-response meta-analysis of **165 RCTs (12,773 participants)** found each
**5 g/day** of soluble fiber lowered LDL-C by **−5.57 mg/dL (95% CI −7.44, −3.69)**, roughly
**1.11 mg/dL per gram**, with an overall effect of **−8.28 mg/dL** and an optimal
**−10.75 mg/dL at 10 g/day** [7, meta_analysis].

**Lifestyle.** Weight loss and exercise lower LDL-C modestly relative to the dietary and
pharmacologic levers above; effect sizes vary widely and overlap with concurrent diet
change (qualitative; no single numeric threshold adopted here).

**Secondary medical conditions.** Reversible (secondary) causes of elevated LDL-C include
**hypothyroidism** (reduced LDL clearance), **nephrotic syndrome**, **cholestasis**,
pregnancy, and certain drugs (cyclosporine, thiazides) — all "excludable by history,
physical examination, and laboratory tests" [6, mechanism_review].

**Pharmacotherapy — approximate % LDL-C lowering.** Statins lower LDL-C by **≥50%
(high-intensity: atorvastatin 40–80 mg, rosuvastatin 20–40 mg)** or **30–49%
(moderate-intensity)**, with low-intensity <30% [2, mechanism_review]. As add-on,
**ezetimibe** adds roughly **15–20%** (≈26–46% less reduction than a PCSK9 inhibitor)
[8, meta_analysis]. **PCSK9 monoclonal antibodies** (evolocumab, alirocumab) lower LDL-C by
about **50–60%** versus placebo (evolocumab 420 mg monthly −54.6%; alirocumab −52.6%; real-
world 12-month −58% to −62%) [8, meta_analysis]. **Bempedoic acid** lowers LDL-C by about
**15–25%** (−17.4% to −18.1% on maximal statin; −21.4% to −28.5% in statin-intolerant
patients; pooled ≈−20.7%) [8, meta_analysis]. **Inclisiran** (siRNA against PCSK9) lowers
LDL-C by about **50%** (−51% from baseline vs placebo at 77 weeks) [8, meta_analysis].
