# Section A — Identity, Physiology & Causal Significance (LDL-C)

## What LDL-C is

LDL-C (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol) is the mass of cholesterol carried within circulating LDL particles, reported in mg/dL (US) or mmol/L (most of the world; the conversion factor for cholesterol is 1 mmol/L ≈ 38.7 mg/dL [3, meta_analysis]). It is not a measure of LDL particle *number* — it quantifies the cholesterol cargo, not the carriers. LDL-C is the primary lipid target of every major cardiovascular prevention guideline, and the quantity against which lipid-lowering therapy is titrated.

## LDL-C is causal for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease

The European Atherosclerosis Society Consensus Panel concluded that "consistent evidence from numerous and multiple different types of clinical and genetic studies unequivocally establishes that LDL causes ASCVD" — i.e., LDL is not merely a risk marker but a causal agent in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [4, mechanism_review]. This conclusion rests on three converging evidence streams: randomized statin trials, Mendelian randomization, and epidemiology, which agree in direction and are roughly dose-consistent.

### Randomized trial evidence — the per-1-mmol/L effect

The Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' (CTT) Collaboration pooled individual-participant data from 26 randomized trials (~170,000 participants) and found that each 1.0 mmol/L (≈38.7 mg/dL) reduction in LDL-C with a statin reduced major vascular events by 22% (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.76–0.80) [1, meta_analysis]. The same per-1-mmol/L analysis showed major coronary events RR 0.87 (95% CI 0.81–0.93), coronary revascularization RR 0.81 (95% CI 0.76–0.85), ischaemic stroke RR 0.86 (95% CI 0.77–0.96), and all-cause mortality RR 0.90 (95% CI 0.87–0.93) [1, meta_analysis]. A subsequent CTT analysis of 27 trials (174,149 participants) extended the finding to people at low baseline risk, reporting a 21% reduction in major vascular events per 1 mmol/L (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.77–0.81), with an absolute reduction of ~11 major vascular events per 1,000 over five years in participants whose five-year risk was below 10% [2, meta_analysis]. The EAS panel summarized this body of work as a "22% proportional reduction in the risk of major cardiovascular events per millimole per litre reduction in LDL-C" over ~5 years of treatment [4, mechanism_review].

### Mendelian randomization — cumulative, lifetime exposure

Ference and colleagues used genetic variants that lower LDL-C from birth as a natural randomization. In a meta-analysis of 312,321 participants, naturally randomized lifelong exposure to lower LDL-C was associated with a 54.5% reduction in coronary heart disease risk per 1 mmol/L (95% CI 48.8%–59.5%) — roughly a 3-fold greater reduction per unit LDL-C than a statin started later in life (p = 8.43 × 10⁻¹⁹) [3, meta_analysis]. The EAS panel concurred that long-term exposure to lower LDL-C is associated with "up to a three-fold greater proportional reduction" in cardiovascular risk per unit LDL-C than short-term statin treatment [4, mechanism_review]. The reconciling principle is that LDL's causal effect "is determined by both the absolute magnitude and the cumulative duration of exposure to LDL-C" [4, mechanism_review].

### The "lower is better / cumulative burden" model

These findings define the prevailing model: ASCVD risk is driven by cumulative LDL exposure ("cholesterol-years"), the integral of LDL-C concentration over time, so both magnitude and duration matter [7, mechanism_review]. Because plaque accrues with cumulative exposure, maintaining low LDL-C over many years delays the age at which mature atherosclerotic plaques develop and substantially reduces lifetime ASCVD risk — a single mid-life LDL-C reading captures magnitude but not the accumulated burden [7, mechanism_review]. The short-term ~22% per-mmol/L statin effect [1, meta_analysis] and the ~54% per-mmol/L lifetime-genetic effect [3, meta_analysis] are not contradictory; they are the same causal relationship measured over ~5 years versus over decades.

## Calculated vs directly-measured LDL-C

LDL-C is most often *calculated*, not directly measured. The Friedewald equation (LDL-C = total cholesterol − HDL-C − triglycerides/5) assumes a fixed triglyceride-to-VLDL-cholesterol ratio of 5:1, which breaks down at low LDL-C and high triglycerides [5, cohort]. The Martin-Hopkins method replaces the fixed factor of 5 with an adjustable factor (~3 to 12) selected from a 180-cell table stratified by triglyceride and non-HDL-C concentrations (median ratio 5.2, IQR 4.5–6.0) [5, cohort]. Validated against 1,350,908 lipid profiles, it raised overall concordance with the reference method to 91.7% vs 85.4% for Friedewald (triglycerides <400 mg/dL); the gain was largest for classifying LDL-C <70 mg/dL at elevated triglycerides — e.g., 84.0% vs 40.3% concordance at triglycerides 200–399 mg/dL [5, cohort]. The Sampson (NIH) equation, derived by beta-quantification, extends validity to triglycerides up to 800 mg/dL and outperformed both Friedewald and Martin-Hopkins at low LDL-C and high triglycerides (validation concordance correlation coefficient 0.992) [6, cohort]. The conceptual point: calculated LDL-C carries equation-dependent error that is largest exactly where clinical decisions are tightest (low LDL-C, high triglycerides), so the calculation method is not interchangeable.

## LDL-C vs ApoB / non-HDL-C discordance (brief)

Because each atherogenic particle (LDL, VLDL, IDL, Lp(a)) carries exactly one apolipoprotein B (ApoB) molecule, ApoB measures the total *number* of atherogenic particles, whereas LDL-C measures cholesterol *mass*; atherosclerosis tracks particle number more closely than summed cholesterol [8, mechanism_review]. Particles vary in cholesterol content (small, dense, cholesterol-depleted LDL), so LDL-C can understate particle number — the two are discordant in a meaningful subset of people [8, mechanism_review]. In 293,876 UK Biobank adults, ApoB and LDL-C correlated strongly (r = 0.96), yet at LDL-C = 130 mg/dL ApoB spanned 85.8–108.8 mg/dL; when discordant, ApoB was the better predictor — 10-year ASCVD rates of 7.3 vs 4.0 per 100 for high vs low ApoB at fixed LDL-C, and residual ApoB remained significant after adjusting for LDL-C (HR 1.06) while residual LDL-C did not when ApoB was included [9, cohort]. LDL-C remains the primary guideline target, but in discordance it can under- or over-state the particle-driven risk that ApoB captures.
