## Section E — Concentration / COI audit, commercialization & sourcing realism

### E.1 Single-group concentration audit (headline finding)

The primary-research literature on epitalon is dominated by a single institutional cluster to a degree that is unusual even within a specialty field. The following enumeration covers all identifiable primary-research groups that have published efficacy or mechanism data on epitalon (Ala-Glu-Asp-Gly) or its parent compound Epithalamin.

**Group 1 — St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology (Khavinson et al.)**
Lead principal investigator: Vladimir Kh. Khavinson (director and founder). Key co-authors cycling across papers: S.V. Rosenfeld, N.S. Lin'kova, S.V. Trofimova, A.V. Semenchenko, N.Yu. Zavarzina. This group is the originating and numerically dominant source across all outcome domains: in vitro telomerase/chromatin work [1, in_vitro], fibroblast replication limits [2, in_vitro], neurogenesis gene expression [3, in_vitro], human clinical geroprotective studies [4, open_label], retinitis pigmentosa [5, open_label], melatonin restoration [6, mechanism_review], antioxidant enzyme work [7, animal], and the broader "peptide theory of ageing" synthesis that frames almost all interpretive claims [8, mechanism_review]. Khavinson holds numerous Russian and international patents and has authored hundreds of scientific publications on Khavinson-class peptide bioregulators originating from his institute [9, anecdote_aggregate].

**Group 2 — N.N. Petrov Research Institute of Oncology (Anisimov et al.)**
Lead PI: Vladimir N. Anisimov (Head, Dept. of Carcinogenesis and Oncogerontology). The Anisimov group collaborated with Khavinson on the most-cited animal lifespan and oncology papers — including the key Biogerontology (2003) SHR mice study listing Anisimov, Khavinson, Popovich, Zabezhinski, Alimova, Rosenfeld, Zavarzina, Semenchenko, and Yashin as co-authors. Rosenfeld, Zavarzina, and Semenchenko appear on both Institute rosters across multiple papers, and the Anisimov–Khavinson papers are explicitly collaborative works between two St. Petersburg institutions less than 30 km apart [10, animal]. This group is best classified as a partial collaborator within the same city and scientific milieu, not an independent replication.

**Group 3 — "G. d'Annunzio" University of Chieti-Pescara (Gatta et al., 2025)**
A 2025 Italian paper on epitalon in a diabetic retinopathy wound-healing in vitro model listed 11 authors. Critically, three of the 11 — Aleksandr Trofimov (dual-affiliated with the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology), Vladimir Khavinson, and Svetlana Trofimova — are from Khavinson's own institute [11, in_vitro]. This paper cannot be classified as independent replication; it is a Khavinson-school collaboration with Italian co-authors.

**Group 4 — Gyeongsang National University, Republic of Korea (Ullah/Haider et al., 2025)**
A 2025 study in Life Sciences examined epitalon-activated telomerase in bovine oocyte maturation and post-thaw embryo development [12, in_vitro]. All authors are at Gyeongsang National University animal science and agriculture divisions; no Khavinson co-authorship; funding from the National Research Foundation of Korea. This constitutes the clearest genuinely independent replication to date — though the endpoint (bovine reproductive biology) is mechanistically adjacent but not equivalent to the longevity, cancer, or circadian claims.

**Group 5 — Brunel University London (Al-Dulaimi et al., 2025)**
A 2025 study in Biogerontology from Brunel University's Centre for Genome Engineering and Maintenance plus Royal Brompton Hospital documented dose-dependent telomere length extension in human cell lines via telomerase upregulation or ALT activity [13, in_vitro]. No Khavinson co-authorship; no Russian funding disclosed. A published erratum (PMID 41240216, November 2025) exists; its scope is unknown from the abstract. This is the second genuinely independent group on the telomerase mechanism — confined to in vitro work only.

**Group 6 — Warsaw / Torun review team (Araj et al., 2025)**
A 2025 comprehensive review from the Medical University of Warsaw and Nicolaus Copernicus University, Torun [14, mechanism_review]. Polish authors, no Khavinson co-authorship, no COI declared. This is a secondary synthesis, not new primary efficacy data.

**Group-share computation:**

| Group | Primary efficacy papers (estimated) | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Khavinson / St. Petersburg IBG | ~30–35 primary papers (in vitro + animal + human) | Originating group |
| Anisimov / N.N. Petrov Oncology (collaborative) | ~8–10 co-authored with Khavinson school | Partial collaborator, same city |
| Gatta et al. (Chieti-Pescara + Khavinson co-authors) | 1 | Khavinson co-authored collaboration |
| Ullah et al. (Gyeongsang National, Korea) | 1 | First genuinely independent group |
| Al-Dulaimi et al. (Brunel, UK) | 1 | Second genuinely independent group |
| Earlier in vitro / animal studies (other Russian groups, Kuznik, Kossoy) | ~3–5, with Khavinson institutional overlap | Khavinson-school or co-authored |

**Verdict:** Of approximately 40–50 identified primary epitalon/epithalamin efficacy papers in the literature, an estimated ≥80% originate from or are co-authored by the Khavinson group and its direct collaborators (St. Petersburg IBG + Petrov Oncology, Anisimov). Genuinely independent primary replication — groups with no Khavinson co-authorship and non-Russian funding — is limited to exactly two papers as of late 2025, both in vitro, both on the telomerase mechanism, and neither testing the longevity, anti-cancer, or circadian outcomes that constitute the headline clinical claims. No independent group has replicated the animal lifespan data or any human clinical outcome.

This is an exceptionally high single-school concentration and represents the central epistemic limitation of the epitalon evidence base.

### E.2 Conflict of interest and commercialization

Khavinson occupies a structurally conflicted position that is standard in Russian bioregulator science but would trigger automatic mandatory disclosure under Western journal policies: he is simultaneously (1) the inventor and primary originating researcher of epitalon and the parent Epithalamin compound, (2) Director of the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology, the institution that conducted the dominant body of primary research, and (3) the developer of multiple Russian Ministry of Health-authorized pharmaceuticals, including Epithalamin itself, plus a portfolio of peptide food supplements introduced into clinical practice [9, anecdote_aggregate]. These commercial products were transferred from what began as a Soviet military medicine program [9, anecdote_aggregate].

Epithalamin is a registered pharmaceutical in Russia (pineal gland extract preparation, Russian Ministry of Health authorization). Epitalon (synthetic AEDG tetrapeptide) is Khavinson's synthetic derivative, not separately a registered pharmaceutical in any Western jurisdiction. Formal COI disclosures in Khavinson-group papers routinely state "no conflict of interest" — a declaration that does not address the structural issue of inventor-researcher-commercializer overlap within one institution.

Khavinson holds numerous patents (Russian and international) across his bioregulator portfolio [9, anecdote_aggregate]. Specific patents on Epithalamin and short peptide bioregulators are documented via the public rexresearch patent repository. The St. Petersburg Institute monetizes this portfolio through pharmaceutical licensing and the "Khavinson Peptides" branded supplement line available via multiple Russian and international distributors (e.g., e-peptide.com) [9, anecdote_aggregate].

No formal regulatory-body conflict finding or research misconduct finding has been identified in available sources. The COI concern is structural, not proven fraudulent.

### E.3 Sourcing realism: consumer and grey-market epitalon

**Regulatory status (as of mid-2026):** Epitalon has no FDA-approved indication. It was previously on the FDA 503A Category 2 list (significant safety concerns for compounding) but was removed from that list effective April 15, 2026, with a Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee review scheduled for July 24, 2026 for both "Epitalon acetate" and "Epitalon free base" forms [16, regulatory]. It is not scheduled as a controlled substance in the US, EU, or UK as of this writing. It circulates primarily as a grey-market "research chemical" sold for "research use only."

**Purity and identity verification:** Consumer-accessible epitalon is available from dozens of research-chemical vendors claiming ≥98–99% HPLC purity, typically at $30–65 per 10 mg vial or ~$90–130 per 50 mg vial [15, anecdote_aggregate]. Claimed third-party certificates of analysis exist from some vendors but are unverifiable as to chain of custody and batch provenance. No independent systematic survey of grey-market epitalon identity/purity has been published; general peptide-market surveys document mislabeling, underfilling, and contamination risks across unregulated research-peptide suppliers.

**Epitalon vs. Epithalamin confusion:** Consumer products compound two distinct compounds. Epithalamin is a crude bovine pineal gland polypeptide extract with heterogeneous composition — Khavinson's original clinical preparation, authorized in Russia — whereas epitalon (AEDG) is the synthetic defined tetrapeptide. Their biological profiles partially overlap but are not equivalent; human clinical data from Epithalamin studies (longer-running Russian trials) cannot be directly attributed to synthetic epitalon [14, mechanism_review]. Consumer vendors and even grey-market communities routinely conflate the names (Epithalon = Epitalon = AEDG) while the parent compound Epithalamin is a pharmacologically distinct entity. A 2017 mass-spectrometry study confirmed epitalon's presence in physiological pineal extract for the first time, but product-level confusion in commerce predates and persists independent of that finding [14, mechanism_review].

**Cost of a typical protocol:** The most common community-cited protocol (10 mg/day × 10–20 days, 2–3 cycles per year) implies 200–600 mg per year. At grey-market pricing, this runs approximately $600–$1,800/year before any compounding pharmacy premium. Licensed compounding pharmacies (where available post-PCAC review) would command substantially higher per-unit costs with sterility and identity guarantees.
