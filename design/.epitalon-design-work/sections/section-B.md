## Section B — Telomerase, lifespan & longevity evidence

### B.1 The telomerase / telomere-elongation claim

The compound's central selling point is telomerase activation. The foundational paper is a 2003 in-vitro study by Khavinson, Bondarev, and Butyugov at the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology: they introduced epitalon into cultures of telomerase-negative human fetal fibroblasts and reported expression of the hTERT catalytic subunit, increased telomerase enzymatic activity, and telomere elongation [1, in_vitro]. The cell type is key: fetal fibroblasts are not somatic cells from an aged organism, and telomerase-negative lines can be unusually responsive to telomerase-activating stimuli. The study was small, the methods were not described at the scale needed for full replication, and critically it was a single-group experiment published in the Bulletin of Experimental Biology and Medicine — a Russian-language journal with limited Western peer review. No independent laboratory replicated this finding for over two decades.

The first detailed independent Western replication was published in Biogerontology in September 2025. Al-Dulaimi, Thomas, Matta, and Roberts at Brunel University London (Centre for Genome Engineering and Maintenance) treated two breast cancer cell lines (21NT, BT474) and normal epithelial cells and fibroblasts (HMEC, IBR.3) with epitalon at 0.1–1 µg/ml for 4 days to 3 weeks [2, in_vitro]. qPCR and immunofluorescence showed dose-dependent telomere length extension across all cell types. Mechanism diverged by cell type: in normal cells, hTERT upregulation drove the effect; in cancer cell lines, ALT (Alternative Lengthening of Telomeres) pathway activation was detected via PML body formation, while telomerase appeared downregulated. A correction to the paper was issued in November 2025 (PMID 41240216) clarifying figure elements but not altering conclusions [2, in_vitro]. This 2025 study is the first peer-reviewed independent replication of telomere-lengthening effects, and it confirms the in-vitro biology is real — but in cell culture, under pharmacological concentrations, using cancer and normal lines that do not recapitulate the complex cellular environment of living tissue. No completed human clinical trial has measured blood or tissue telomere length before and after epitalon administration under controlled conditions. The translation from cell-culture telomerase activation to meaningful telomere extension in a living human body is unestablished.

**Evidence tier for the telomerase claim:** two in-vitro studies (one Khavinson-group, one independent), both tier 2–3 by design. The effect is biologically plausible and now reproduced in an independent Western laboratory, but has not been demonstrated in animals or humans under controlled conditions.

### B.2 Animal lifespan studies

The most-cited rodent lifespan study on the synthetic epitalon tetrapeptide (AEDG) is Anisimov, Khavinson, Popovich, and colleagues (2003), published in Biogerontology — a solid specialty gerontology journal (tier 2) [3, animal]. The species was female Swiss-derived SHR mice; n = 54 per group (control and epitalon-treated). Mice received monthly subcutaneous injections of 1.0 µg/mouse from age 3 months until natural death. The result was nuanced: mean lifespan was NOT significantly extended. The statistically significant effects were confined to maximum lifespan (+12.3%) and survival of the last 10% of the cohort (+13.3%). Epitalon inhibited leukemia development approximately 6-fold but did not reduce total tumor incidence. A strain-specific caveat applies: SHR (spontaneously hypertensive) mice carry elevated baseline cancer susceptibility, so the leukemia/tumor-inhibition finding may reflect epitalon's interaction with this strain's particular oncogenic background rather than a generalizable anti-tumor effect — the result should not be extrapolated to other rodent strains or to humans without independent confirmation in non-SHR animals. The Khavinson co-authorship is a COI signal; Anisimov as lead author adds some independence (he is a separate researcher at a different institution, the N. N. Petrov National Medical Research Center of Oncology). No non-Khavinson-affiliated group has replicated this specific lifespan experiment.

The broader epithalamin literature includes animal data in rats and Drosophila. A frequently cited statistic — epithalamin extended mean rat lifespan by approximately 25% with reduced spontaneous tumor incidence — is attributed to Anisimov et al. (PMID 12946225); however, PMID 12946225 does not resolve to an epithalamin rat lifespan paper on PubMed (it appears to map to an unrelated pathology paper, so this specific PMID claim could not be verified and is excluded from the bibliography). The broader pattern of epithalamin-rat data does appear in review-level summaries from the same Khavinson-Anisimov network, including a 2003 Neuro Endocrinol Lett review [4, open_label] and a 2009 Biogerontology review [5, mechanism_review], but these are self-reviews by the same authorship cluster, not independent confirmation. Drosophila lifespan data exists in the same network's publications but is not cited separately here given the translational distance.

**Key methodological concerns for the rodent lifespan data:** (a) virtually all positive lifespan results originate from the Khavinson-Anisimov collaborative network; no Western or Asian independent group has replicated a full lifespan study with epitalon or epithalamin; (b) the SHR mouse result showed maximum-lifespan extension without mean-lifespan extension — a weaker signal than often summarized; (c) the older epithalamin (crude pineal extract) literature should not be conflated with the synthetic AEDG tetrapeptide — these are distinct chemical entities and the human longevity studies all used the crude extract.

### B.3 Human longevity / mortality claims

The most-cited human evidence for longevity benefit is the Khavinson and Morozov (2003) study in Neuro Endocrinol Lett (PMID 14523363) [4, open_label]. This was a non-randomized, non-blinded 6–8-year clinical program at the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology and the Institute of Gerontology of the Ukrainian Academy of Medical Sciences. The population was 266 elderly individuals aged over 60. The controls were not drawn from a concurrent randomized arm but from background population mortality data of the same demographic. There was no blinding: patients knew they were receiving peptides; clinicians knew they were administering them. The administration route is not specified in the available abstract; based on the consistent preparation protocol used in companion studies from the same institute (e.g., Korkushko 2011 [6]: intramuscular courses of 10 mg injections), the route is likely intramuscular epithalamin courses, but this cannot be confirmed from the available source. The claimed mortality reduction was 1.6–1.8-fold for epithalamin alone and 2.5-fold for combined thymalin + epithalamin treatment; a subgroup receiving 6 annual courses showed a 4.1-fold mortality reduction compared to controls. These are extraordinary effect sizes. The honest classification of this study is open-label, non-randomized, with historical/population controls — not a randomized controlled trial. Selection bias is a legitimate concern: patients attending a specialized gerontology institute for regular injections also receive more frequent medical contact, which independently reduces mortality risk. No independent group has reproduced these mortality findings.

The second important human study is Korkushko, Khavinson, Shatilo, and Antonyk-Sheglova (2011), published in the Bulletin of Experimental Biology and Medicine (PMID 22451889) [6, rct]. This study is described in its abstract as a "randomized comparative study" of a pituitary peptide geroprotector in elderly coronary patients. The cohort was enrolled at the Institute of Gerontology, National Academy of Medical Sciences of Ukraine, Kyiv, Ukraine, with follow-up spanning approximately the 1990s–2000s — a period when Ukrainian cardiac standard-of-care differed meaningfully from current Western practice, which limits direct generalizability to contemporary populations managed under modern cardiology guidelines. N = 79 (39 treatment, 40 control). The treatment group received 6 courses of epithalamin (5 intramuscular injections of 10 mg each, with 3-day intervals) over 3 years alongside standard cardiac therapy; controls received standard therapy only. Over a 15-year follow-up, 66.7% of the treated group (26/39) were alive versus 40% of controls (16/40). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank testing was performed. This is the most methodologically rigorous of the Khavinson-group human studies — it has a concurrent control group and a defined randomization claim — but: the n is small (79 total), the journal (Bulletin of Experimental Biology and Medicine) is a Russian-translated tier-3 venue, all authors are from or affiliated with Khavinson's institute, and no independent replication exists. The term "randomized" in the abstract has not been independently audited for allocation concealment or blinding procedures.

**The critical honest distinction:** the human mortality and longevity claims rest almost entirely on Khavinson-institute-authored, single-site studies published in Russian-translated journals. The 2003 mortality study [4] is not an RCT by Western standards and has serious selection-bias vulnerability. The 2011 study [6] claims randomization but n = 79, is single-site, and is unreplicated. Zero independent Western or Asian clinical trials of epitalon or epithalamin have measured mortality or longevity outcomes. Furthermore, the human studies used epithalamin (the crude pineal polypeptide extract), not the synthetic AEDG tetrapeptide that is commercially sold as "epitalon" today — the two are distinct preparations. As of mid-2026, no Phase 2 or Phase 3 controlled efficacy trial of synthetic epitalon (AEDG) has been completed or published in a PubMed-indexed journal.
