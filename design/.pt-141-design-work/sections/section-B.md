## Section B — HSDD clinical-trial evidence

### B.1 The RECONNECT Phase 3 Program — FDA approval basis

The strongest clinical evidence for bremelanotide rests on two identically designed phase 3 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter trials (Study 301 and Study 302), together constituting the RECONNECT program. The combined safety population was 1,247 premenopausal women with acquired, generalized hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD); the efficacy-evaluable population was 1,202. Participants were predominantly US-based (96.6%), white (85.6%), and had a mean age of 39 years. Both trials ran 24 weeks, with bremelanotide 1.75 mg self-administered subcutaneously on an as-needed basis (not more than once in 24 hours) before anticipated sexual activity.

**Co-primary endpoints and effect sizes.** The co-primary endpoints were (1) the change from baseline in the Female Sexual Function Index desire domain (FSFI-D; 0–6 scale) and (2) the change from baseline in item 13 of the Female Sexual Distress Scale — Desire/Arousal/Orgasm (FSDS-DAO; 0–4 scale), capturing distress specifically related to low desire. Both studies met both co-primary endpoints [1, rct].

The placebo-adjusted treatment effects, while statistically robust, were modest in absolute magnitude:

| Outcome | Study 301 | Study 302 | Integrated |
|---|---|---|---|
| FSFI-D change (vs. placebo) | +0.30 (P<.001) | +0.42 (P<.001) | +0.35 (P<.001) |
| FSDS-DAO item 13 change (vs. placebo) | −0.37 (P<.001) | −0.29 (P=.005) | −0.33 (P<.001) |

Cohen's d effect sizes for the integrated RECONNECT data were approximately 0.39 for the desire domain and 0.27 for the distress item — small-to-medium by convention [2, rct]. To contextualize: in the bremelanotide arm the absolute mean improvements exceeded the pre-specified minimal clinically important difference threshold for the FSFI-D (+0.6) in the 52-week open-label extension data (bremelanotide→bremelanotide: +1.25 to +1.30 on the FSFI-D; −1.4 to −1.7 on the FSDS-DAO item 13), though responders in the core 24-week placebo-controlled phase showed more modest gains [3, open_label].

**Responder analysis.** In the efficacy-evaluable integrated population, a meaningful-response threshold analysis (using MCID definitions developed in the phase 2b program [4, rct]) yielded responder rates of approximately 58% for bremelanotide versus approximately 36% for placebo — a real but substantially placebo-inflated separation. Authors noted the high placebo responder rates and cautioned that trial design effects may have inflated the apparent treatment benefit [3, open_label].

**Safety and tolerability.** Nausea was the dominant adverse effect, occurring in approximately 40% of bremelanotide-treated participants versus ~1% of placebo recipients; 40.4% reported nausea in the 52-week open-label extension (42.7% in Study 301 arm; 37.7% in Study 302 arm) [3, open_label]. Flushing (20.6%) and headache (12.0%) were also common. Nausea severity was mostly mild-to-moderate but was the single most common reason for treatment discontinuation: 23.4% discontinued in one arm and 18.7% in the other during the open-label extension phase [3, open_label]. Approximately 13% of patients required antiemetic medication.

Blood pressure: small transient elevations (mean +3.1/+3.2 mmHg systolic/diastolic at the approved 1.75 mg SC dose, lasting typically <15 minutes) were observed in a dedicated ambulatory monitoring study (n=397 premenopausal women with FSD, normotensive or controlled hypertension) [5, rct]. Bremelanotide is contraindicated in patients with cardiovascular disease; the label carries a blood-pressure warning [6, regulatory].

**FDA approval.** The FDA approved bremelanotide injection (Vyleesi, Palatin Technologies/AMAG Pharmaceuticals) on June 21, 2019 (NDA 210557) for acquired, generalized HSDD in premenopausal women. This is the only approved bremelanotide indication and the only FDA-approved pharmacotherapy targeting central sexual desire pathways in this population [6, regulatory].

**Honest tier assessment.** PT-141/Vyleesi holds the highest evidence tier of any sexual peptide discussed in this wiki — two replicated phase 3 RCTs, published in a top obstetrics/gynecology journal, supporting a genuine regulatory approval. The effect is real and statistically robust. However, the absolute treatment-versus-placebo separation is modest (placebo-adjusted FSFI-D increment ~0.35 on a 6-point scale); nausea-driven discontinuation rates are substantial (~20%+); and the approved population is narrow (premenopausal women with a DSM-defined HSDD diagnosis). Any use in men, or in women without an HSDD diagnosis, is off-label.

### B.2 Earlier male erectile dysfunction / intranasal program

Before the subcutaneous HSDD program, bremelanotide was investigated as an intranasal spray (10 mg) for erectile dysfunction in men who were non-responsive to phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors. The most prominent trial was a randomized double-blind placebo-controlled study (n=342 married men aged 28–59 with sildenafil-refractory ED), which reported a positive clinical response in 33.5% of bremelanotide recipients versus 8.5% of placebo recipients (P=.03), with significantly greater intercourse satisfaction scores in the treatment group (P=.03) [7, rct].

**Critical note — research integrity:** The paper by Safarinejad and Hosseini (J Urol, March 2008; PMID 18206919) is subject to an Expression of Concern issued by The Journal of Urology on January 10, 2023 (PMID 36626345), citing data-integrity concerns identified by independent statisticians [7, rct]. The original paper's findings should be interpreted with caution pending resolution of the journal's investigation.

**Route change rationale — blood pressure.** The intranasal formulation was abandoned, not primarily because of failed efficacy, but because of blood-pressure safety concerns and pharmacokinetic variability. The FDA halted the intranasal development program in 2007. A key issue was wide bioavailability variability with intranasal delivery, which exposed some patients to excessive plasma concentrations with heightened cardiovascular risk. The subcutaneous route provided tighter dose-exposure relationships and the small-but-transient blood pressure elevations at the approved 1.75 mg SC dose were deemed acceptable for the approved indication with appropriate contraindications [5, rct].

No phase 3 RCT of any bremelanotide formulation in men has been completed. Male use — whether for erectile dysfunction or libido augmentation — remains entirely off-label and lacks the phase 3 registration-quality evidence that supports the approved female HSDD indication.
