## Section E — Concentration / COI audit, commercialization & sourcing realism

### E.1 Lineage and platform origin

Bremelanotide (PT-141) was derived from melanocortin research initiated at the University of Arizona in the 1980s, originally aimed at tanning peptides (melanotan-I and melanotan-II). The University of Arizona licensed its melanocortin technology to Competitive Technologies, Inc. (CTT), a university technology-transfer company, which in turn granted Palatin Technologies, Inc. an exclusive license to develop the MT-II lineage [1, vendor_label]. Palatin synthesized bremelanotide — a deamidated, carboxyl-terminated analogue of MT-II — as a compound with reduced non-target receptor activity (minimizing MC1R-mediated tanning) focused on CNS MC3R/MC4R signaling for sexual function. PT-141 is thus not an independent discovery; it is the single downstream commercialization of one licensed university platform, flowing through a single company (Palatin) to a single commercialization partner (AMAG) [2, regulatory].

### E.2 RECONNECT sponsorship and primary-evidence concentration

The pivotal efficacy evidence for bremelanotide rests on two replicate Phase 3 randomized controlled trials — the RECONNECT studies — enrolling a combined 1,267 premenopausal women with HSDD. Both trials were **sponsored jointly by Palatin Technologies, Inc. and AMAG Pharmaceuticals, Inc.**, the developer and exclusive North American licensee respectively [3, rct]. The funding source is stated explicitly as "Palatin Technologies, Inc., and AMAG Pharmaceuticals, Inc." in the primary publication; editorial support was provided by Phase Five Communications, paid by AMAG [3, rct].

**Sponsor-concentration calculation:** The two RECONNECT trials are the sole Phase 3 RCT efficacy evidence cited in the FDA NDA (approved June 2019). No independent (non-Palatin, non-AMAG) Phase 3 replication of HSDD efficacy has been conducted. Subgroup analyses published subsequently [4, rct] carry the same author team (Simon, Kingsberg, Portman, Jordan, Lucas, Krop, Clayton) — all with disclosed financial ties to Palatin and/or AMAG — and derive from the same RECONNECT dataset. A long-term 52-week open-label extension study [5, open_label] likewise lists AMAG employee co-authors (Williams and Krop are disclosed AMAG employees and stockholders; Jordan is disclosed as Palatin VP).

**Verdict:** 100% of the Phase 3 pivotal evidence and 100% of the post-hoc RECONNECT subgroup publications originate from the Palatin/AMAG-sponsored trial program. The single independent publication in this section [6, mechanism_review] is a narrative review that synthesizes the sponsored trials but adds no new clinical data. The sponsor-concentration of the registration-quality efficacy evidence is effectively **1 of 1 independent groups (0% non-sponsor Phase 3 RCT data; 100% Palatin/AMAG registration-program data)**. This is structurally normal for a single-company FDA-approved drug and does not by itself invalidate the efficacy signal, but it means the effect sizes (desire score +0.35, p<0.001; distress score −0.33, p<0.001) carry no external corroboration beyond the sponsor's own program.

Phase 2 male ED data (intranasal bremelanotide for sildenafil non-responders) exists from earlier Palatin-funded studies [3, rct] and is referenced in secondary literature; it has not been replicated independently and no FDA approval for male indication was pursued.

### E.3 Author COI disclosures

The RECONNECT lead author team has extensive, documented financial relationships with the sponsors [3, rct; 5, open_label]:

- **Sheryl A. Kingsberg** (lead, RECONNECT): advisory boards/consulting for both AMAG and Palatin; also consulting 15+ other companies in women's health.
- **James A. Simon** (lead, long-term extension): advisory/consulting for AMAG and Palatin, plus 18+ other companies.
- **David Portman**: advisory roles with AMAG and Palatin; employee and stockholder of Sermonix Pharmaceuticals (an AMAG spin-off entity at the time).
- **Laura A. Williams and Julie Krop**: disclosed as **AMAG employees and stockholders** — these are not arms-length academic authors; they are sponsor employees on the byline [5, open_label].
- **Robert Jordan**: disclosed as **Palatin Technologies Vice President** with a "significant role in bremelanotide development" [5, open_label].
- **Anita H. Clayton**: consulting for Palatin and multiple other companies.

These disclosures confirm that multiple authors in both the primary RECONNECT publication and the long-term extension study have direct employment, equity, or paid consulting relationships with the sponsors — a standard but material COI in this context, particularly because the two employer-level authors (Williams, Krop = AMAG; Jordan = Palatin VP) were embedded in the trial design and reporting chain.

### E.4 Commercialization and the Vyleesi commercial arc

**2017:** Palatin and AMAG execute an exclusive North American commercialization license for bremelanotide.

**June 2019:** FDA approves Vyleesi (bremelanotide injection 1.75 mg/0.3 mL, subcutaneous auto-injector) for acquired, generalized HSDD in premenopausal women [2, regulatory]. Analysts projected peak sales of $810 million–$1 billion. The drug launched in September 2019 with ~125 AMAG sales representatives.

**2019 commercial reality:** Vyleesi did not generate a separate revenue line item in AMAG's quarterly filings (Q3 2019: only 3,000 prescriptions written across 1,300 providers in the first four weeks post-launch; standalone revenue not disclosed). Analysts characterized it as likely finding "niche use" given the 40% nausea rate, the self-injection delivery, and the $800/4-pack list price prior to copay assistance [7, anecdote_aggregate].

**July 2020 — mutual termination:** Palatin and AMAG mutually terminated the January 2017 license agreement within 13 months of Vyleesi's commercial launch [8, vendor_label]. AMAG paid Palatin $12 million at closing plus $4.3 million due March 2021 (total $16.3 million to exit). AMAG's CEO framed the divestiture as enabling a "strategic direction" pivot and implemented a 30% workforce reduction. Palatin regained North American rights and began direct commercialization.

**2021–2022 under Palatin:** Vyleesi gross product sales were $4.7 million (FY2021, ending June 30, 2021) with negative net product revenue (−$283,286 in FY2021). FY2022 gross product sales reached $5.8 million with net product revenue of $1.2 million — a 530% net revenue improvement in percentage terms, but on a very small absolute base that confirmed the drug's persistently modest market traction [8, vendor_label].

**December 2023 — Palatin sells to Cosette Pharmaceuticals:** Palatin completed the sale of Vyleesi to Cosette Pharmaceuticals for a nominal $12 million upfront (identical to what AMAG paid to exit) plus up to $159 million in sales-based milestones contingent on annual net sales reaching thresholds from $15 million to $200 million [9, vendor_label]. The milestone structure indicates that Vyleesi's recent annual sales were well below $15 million at time of sale. Palatin cited a strategic decision to focus on its melanocortin pipeline (dry eye disease PL9643, ulcerative colitis PL8177). As of 2024–2026, Cosette Pharmaceuticals is the listed manufacturer on the Vyleesi package insert.

**Chain of ownership:** University of Arizona → Competitive Technologies (license) → Palatin Technologies (development; licensed to AMAG 2017) → AMAG (commercialization, terminated 2020) → Palatin (direct commercialization 2020–2023) → Cosette Pharmaceuticals (2023–present).

### E.5 Grey-market PT-141: sourcing realism

The gap between the approved Vyleesi product and the grey-market "PT-141" sold online is substantial on every dimension:

**Price and access:** Vyleesi (4-pack auto-injector) carries an approximate retail list price of ~$800 before insurance; copay assistance programs under Palatin/AMAG reduced out-of-pocket costs for insured patients but the drug was never widely covered. The same peptide sequence is available from grey-market "research peptide" vendors at a fraction of the cost (commonly $20–$50 per 10 mg vial), sold under a "not for human consumption" legal fiction.

**Purity and identity unverifiable:** Grey-market PT-141 is sold as a lyophilized powder requiring reconstitution. Vendor-supplied Certificates of Analysis (COAs) reference HPLC purity figures (often 99%+) and mass spectrometry identity confirmation, but these certificates are generated by the vendor or a vendor-selected lab, are not subject to FDA cGMP oversight, and cannot be independently verified by the end user. No batch-level third-party audit exists in the grey-market supply chain [10, anecdote_aggregate].

**MT-II/PT-141/MT-I compound confusion:** Melanotan-I (afamelanotide), Melanotan-II (MT-II), and PT-141 (bremelanotide) are three structurally distinct compounds from the same UA melanocortin platform. MT-II is the parent of PT-141: PT-141 is the deamidated, carboxyl-terminated metabolite of MT-II, lacking the C-terminal amide that drives MT-II's stronger MC1R-mediated tanning effects and broader receptor non-selectivity. Grey-market vendors and bodybuilding communities routinely conflate these three compounds. A vial labelled "PT-141" from an unregulated source may contain MT-II (which carries higher tanning activity and a different blood pressure profile), a structurally adjacent impurity, or a different compound entirely. Health authorities in the UK, Australia, and the US have issued warnings about Melanotan II specifically due to reports of melanoma risk association and adverse events from unregulated sales [10, anecdote_aggregate].

**Formulation differences:** The FDA-approved Vyleesi is a subcutaneous auto-injector (fixed-dose, sterile, cGMP-manufactured). Grey-market PT-141 also circulates as an intranasal spray — a route used in early Phase 1/2 studies (nasal spray, 2001 Phase I) but never approved. The intranasal formulations are compounded or sold by grey-market suppliers with no sterility, bioavailability, or dosing consistency assurance.

**Compounding pharmacy status:** Under 503A, pharmacies may compound bremelanotide for individual patients with a valid prescription if clinical necessity for a customized formulation is documented (e.g., a concentration not available commercially). However, bremelanotide's status as an approved drug means 503A pharmacies face restrictions on compounding a copy without such justification. A 2020 FDA warning letter to Tailor Made Compounding LLC cited bremelanotide among substances compounded without meeting 503A conditions [11, regulatory]. The broader context of 503A enforcement (2017–2022 warning letters) shows adulteration — strength, quality, or purity below label claim — as the leading compounding violation [12, mechanism_review].
