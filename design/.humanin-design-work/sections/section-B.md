## Section B — Neuroprotective + metabolic evidence (human-association vs animal-administration)

### B.1 Neuroprotection / Alzheimer's disease

#### B.1.1 In vitro cell evidence (native humanin, characterization)

The foundational neuroprotection evidence comes from cell culture. Hashimoto et al. (2001) systematically characterized humanin's rescue activity across the full spectrum of familial-AD-relevant insults in human neuronal cell lines: humanin suppressed death induced by four FAD mutant genes (V642I-APP, NL-APP, M146L-PS1, N141I-PS2) and by Aβ1-43 peptide directly. Structure-activity analysis identified Cys8 and Ser14 as essential residues and Pro3–Pro19 as the active domain [1, in_vitro]. This is **in vitro evidence with no human subjects**; it establishes mechanism, not clinical efficacy.

#### B.1.2 Rodent model — HNG (S14G-humanin analog), 3xTg-AD mice

Niikura et al. (2011) administered **S14G-humanin (HNG)** — a synthetic analog with Ser→Gly substitution at position 14, reported to be ~1,000-fold more potent than native humanin in neuroprotection assays — intranasally at **10 nmol per session** (5 days/week for the first 2.5 months, then daily for the final 1 month) to triple-transgenic (3xTg-AD) mice (APPswe, tauP310L, PS-1M146V mutations), n=7–9/group (male and female), starting at 13 months of age, for 3.5 months total [2, animal]. Key outcomes:
- Morris water maze: S14G-HN–treated **male** mice spent significantly longer time near the platform location vs. vehicle (effect in females was directionally consistent but not statistically significant)
- Brain Aβ levels markedly lower in treated vs. vehicle animals; plaque area reduced ~52%, integrated plaque density ~47%
- Hippocampal neprilysin expression increased

**Species: mice (3xTg-AD). Compound: S14G-HNG analog, not native humanin. No human subjects.**

#### B.1.3 Rodent model — native humanin, Aβ1-42–injected rats

Chai et al. (2014) administered native humanin to rats with Aβ1-42–induced pathology [3, animal]. Humanin increased dendritic spine density, upregulated synaptic proteins, enhanced long-term potentiation, and reversed memory deficits. It also attenuated Aβ-induced tau hyperphosphorylation via PP2A disinhibition. **Species: rats. Compound: native humanin (not HNG analog). Sample size not reported in abstract.**

#### B.1.4 Human Alzheimer's disease — exogenous humanin administration

**No human clinical trial of exogenous humanin or HNG for Alzheimer's disease exists as of mid-2026.** No Phase 1, Phase 2, or Phase 3 data on humanin/HNG administration in humans has been published. The neuroprotection evidence base is entirely rodent + cell culture.

### B.2 Metabolic effects: insulin sensitivity, glucose, β-cell function

#### B.2.1 Central and peripheral insulin sensitization — rodent (native humanin + analogs)

Muzumdar et al. (2009) demonstrated that humanin is a novel central regulator of peripheral insulin action in rodents [4, animal]. Study used Sprague-Dawley rats (n=92 three-month-old males) and Zucker Diabetic Fatty (ZDF) rats (n=9). Interventions tested native humanin, F6AHN, HNG, and **HNGF6A** (the combined S14G+F6A analog, most potent variant):
- Intracerebroventricular (ICV) native humanin (**primed-continuous ICV infusion, 20 µg total; infusion rate 0.16 µg/kg/min**) significantly improved whole-body insulin sensitivity via hypothalamic STAT-3 activation
- Peripheral HNGF6A infusion enhanced hepatic insulin sensitivity and skeletal muscle glucose uptake
- HNGF6A lowered blood glucose ~50% in ZDF (diabetic) rats
- The same study measured circulating humanin in humans cross-sectionally (observational age-stratified data only) showing levels decline with age; **no humanin was administered to humans**

**Species: Sprague-Dawley rats, ZDF rats. Compound: multiple analogs including HNGF6A. No human administration.**

#### B.2.2 β-cell insulin secretion — potent analog HNGF6A, isolated islets and β-cell line

Kuliawat et al. (2013) reported that HNGF6A (not native humanin) increased glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) in isolated islets from normal and diabetic mice, and in the βTC3 β-cell line [5, in_vitro]. The mechanism involved enhanced mitochondrial metabolism in the β cell. **Species: mouse islets + cell line. Compound: HNGF6A analog. No rodent in vivo n reported for GSIS experiments; no human data.**

### B.3 GH/IGF-1 axis interaction

Lee et al. (2014) established an inverse relationship between GH/IGF-1 signaling and circulating humanin across multiple mouse models and a human cohort [6, cohort]:
- GH-transgenic (short-lived) mice: plasma humanin **reduced ~70%** vs. controls (n=11/group)
- Long-lived Ames dwarf mice (GH-deficient): plasma humanin **elevated ~40%** (n=10/group)
- Liver IGF-1 deletion mice: humanin **elevated ~45%** (n=7/group)
- IGFBP-3 knockout mice: humanin **reduced ~70%** (n=3/group)
- Human subjects: GH-deficient children (n=11) showed negative correlation between baseline humanin and IGF-1 (r=−0.69, p<0.05); 4 weeks of GH therapy reduced humanin ~20%
- Ecuadorians with GH receptor deficiency/Laron syndrome (n=6/group): humanin elevated ~80% vs. controls

Interpretation: IGF-1 (rather than GH per se) suppresses humanin expression. Long-lived, low-GH/IGF-1 states associate with higher circulating humanin. **The human Laron syndrome data is observational (association, not intervention).**

### B.4 Human circulating-humanin evidence: endogenous biomarker associations

All human data in this sub-section is **observational — endogenous humanin as a biomarker, NOT exogenous administration**.

#### B.4.1 Cognitive aging — human cohort, SNP association

Yen et al. (2018) examined circulating humanin levels and cognitive outcomes in two independent human cohorts [7, cohort]:
- **BVAIT Study** (n=146 matched participants, ~58 years mean age, cardiovascular-disease-free): African Americans had ~20% lower circulating humanin than Caucasian Americans
- **Health and Retirement Study (HRS)** (n=15,620 with genotype data): a mitochondrial SNP (rs2854128) in the humanin-coding region associated with ~14% lower circulating humanin; in African Americans, this SNP associated with ~2 years' accelerated cognitive age
- Journal: *Scientific Reports* (2018), PMID 30242290

Note: the mouse arm of this paper showed that administered humanin improved cognition in aged mice; the human arm is observational only.

#### B.4.2 Cardiovascular / coronary endothelial function — human cross-sectional

Widmer et al. (2013) measured circulating humanin in 40 patients undergoing coronary angiography (n=20 with endothelial dysfunction, n=20 with preserved function) [8, cohort]:
- Patients with coronary endothelial dysfunction had significantly lower humanin (1.3 ± 1.1 vs. 2.2 ± 1.5 ng/mL, p=0.03)
- Positive correlation between humanin level and coronary blood flow response to acetylcholine (p=0.0091)
- Journal: *Am J Physiol Heart Circ Physiol* (2013), PMID 23220334
- **Observational, not interventional. No humanin administered.**

#### B.4.3 Impaired fasting glucose — human cross-sectional biomarker

Voigt & Jelinek (2016) measured plasma humanin in 81 participants at a diabetes screening clinic (58 controls, 23 with impaired fasting glucose, IFG) [9, cohort]:
- Plasma humanin significantly lower in IFG group: 124.3 ± 83.9 pg/mL vs. 204.8 ± 92.9 pg/mL in controls (p=0.0001)
- Authors interpreted this as a mitochondrial stress-response marker
- Journal: *Physiological Reports* (2016), PMID 27173674
- **Observational cross-sectional. No humanin administered to humans.**

#### B.4.4 Hemodialysis / cardiovascular risk — human prospective cohort

Bolignano et al. (2024) followed 94 chronic hemodialysis patients (mean age 68 years, 63.9% male, three centers in Italy/Greece) for 30 months [10, cohort]:
- Non-linear U-shaped relationship between humanin and adverse outcomes: both very low (<450.7 pg/mL) and very high (>759.5 pg/mL) humanin associated with increased hazard for composite endpoint (mortality + CV events)
- Intermediate humanin range (575–759.5 pg/mL) showed lowest event incidence
- 56.4% of participants experienced the primary endpoint over follow-up
- Journal: *Journal of Nephrology* (2024), PMID 39102184
- **Prospective observational biomarker study. No humanin administered.**

#### B.4.5 Mitochondrial disease (MELAS) — humanin in muscle tissue

Kariya et al. (2005) examined humanin expression in skeletal muscle biopsies from MELAS patients vs. controls [11, cohort]:
- Humanin strongly positive in ragged-red fibers and some non-ragged-red fibers
- Localized to mitochondria; expression appeared elevated vs. control muscle
- Authors proposed humanin is induced in response to the energy crisis in MELAS fibers and suggested therapeutic potential
- Journal: *Acta Neuropathologica* (2005), PMID 15759134
- **Tissue biomarker study in human patients; no plasma measurement; no administration of humanin.**

---

### KEY DISTINCTION SUMMARY

| Evidence type | What exists | What does NOT exist |
|---|---|---|
| **Endogenous humanin as BIOMARKER (humans)** | Multiple human cohorts: lower plasma humanin associates with cognitive decline, coronary endothelial dysfunction, impaired fasting glucose, renal/CV risk; elevated in MELAS tissue; suppressed by GH/IGF-1 | — |
| **Exogenous humanin/HNG as THERAPEUTIC (animals)** | Rodent + cell data: HNG (S14G analog) reduces Aβ in 3xTg-AD mice, improves memory; native humanin improves insulin sensitivity (ICV + peripheral) in rats; HNGF6A enhances β-cell GSIS in mouse islets | — |
| **Exogenous humanin/HNG administered to HUMANS** | — | **None. Zero human clinical trials (Phase 1, 2, or 3) of exogenous humanin or any analog as of mid-2026.** All therapeutic evidence is rodent or cell-based. The translational gap is entirely unbridged. |
