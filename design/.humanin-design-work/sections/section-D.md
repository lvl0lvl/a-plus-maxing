## Section D — Safety, dosing, route, PK, regulatory & monitoring

### D.1 Human Dosing — No Established Dose Exists

There is NO approved or established exogenous humanin or HNG dose for humans. No Phase 1, 2, or 3 human administration trials of exogenous humanin or its analogs have been completed and published as of mid-2026 [1, regulatory]. A search of ClinicalTrials.gov yields no registered interventional trial of exogenous humanin or HNG in human subjects [1, regulatory].

Grey-market compounding vendors and peptide protocol sites cite doses in the range of ~100–500 µg/day SC or IM for HNG, typically extrapolated from rodent animal-model dosing [not cited — vendor_label]. These figures are UNVALIDATED and may NOT ground any numerical safety claim. They are listed here solely to document that such claims circulate; they have no basis in human PK, human tolerability, or human dose-finding studies. Citing vendor figures as evidence of efficacy or safety is methodologically impermissible under the sourcing rules for this section.

### D.2 Route of Administration & Pharmacokinetics (Preclinical)

All published PK data for humanin and its analogs are from rodent studies [2, mechanism_review]. Primary routes studied: intraperitoneal (IP) injection and subcutaneous (SC) injection in mice and rats. Intranasal delivery has also been examined in animal models [2, mechanism_review].

**Native humanin (HN):** Plasma half-life in wild-type mice is approximately 30 minutes following IP injection. Rapid proteolytic degradation is the presumed elimination mechanism, which is why stabilized analogs were developed [2, mechanism_review].

**HNG (Gly14-humanin):** A single amino-acid substitution (Ser→Gly at position 14) produces an analog with approximately 1,000-fold greater neuroprotective potency than native HN in vitro and a longer plasma half-life. In adult male Sprague-Dawley rats after IP injection, HNG is detectable in plasma, liver, heart, and brain by ELISA, suggesting CNS penetration, though the half-life remains in the sub-4-hour range in rodents [2, mechanism_review].

**IGFBP-3 binding modulates PK:** HNG analogs with reduced IGFBP-3 binding (e.g., HNGF6A) display extended circulating half-life compared to HNG in wild-type mice, because IGFBP-3 binding accelerates clearance [2, mechanism_review]. This implies species- and binding-partner-dependent PK that cannot be directly extrapolated to humans.

**Tissue half-life vs. plasma half-life:** The tissue half-life in rodent target organs appears substantially longer than plasma half-life; once-daily dosing is used in most animal efficacy studies on this basis [2, mechanism_review]. No human tissue distribution data exist.

**No human PK data:** The pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, tissue distribution, and metabolic fate of exogenous humanin or HNG in humans are entirely uncharacterized [2, mechanism_review; 1, regulatory].

### D.3 Human Safety — Essentially Uncharacterized

**Direct human safety evidence: none.** Because no human administration trials have been conducted, there are no published human adverse event data, no maximum tolerated dose, no NOAEL, and no human dose-response curve for exogenous humanin or HNG [1, regulatory; 2, mechanism_review].

**Animal safety:** Rodent studies at pharmacologically active doses have not reported overt dose-limiting toxicity in short-duration experiments. However, animal safety characterization has been conducted almost exclusively in the context of efficacy (neuroprotection, metabolic benefit), not formal toxicology. Repeat-dose, genotoxicity, and carcinogenicity studies in animals are not represented in the peer-reviewed literature as of this writing.

**Injection-site risks:** Grey-market preparations carry the standard risks of unregulated parenteral administration: contamination, particulates, unknown purity, and non-sterile technique [vendor_label — cited for risk context only; not a clinical AE rate].

### D.4 Counter-Signal: Anti-Apoptotic / Pro-Tumor Concern

This is the single most load-bearing safety signal for humanin and must be surfaced explicitly.

Humanin is a potent anti-apoptotic peptide. Its cytoprotective mechanism — binding pro-apoptotic proteins Bax, tBid, and BimEL, blocking cytochrome c release, and suppressing caspase activation — is the same mechanism that can protect CANCER CELLS from programmed death. Two published preclinical studies document this:

**Triple negative breast cancer (TNBC):** Moreno Ayala et al. (2020, Sci Rep, PMID 32444831) demonstrated in a syngeneic 4T1 murine TNBC model that: (a) humanin expression is elevated in human TNBC biopsy tissue relative to normal mammary gland; (b) systemic exogenous humanin administration significantly accelerated tumor growth and promoted spontaneous lung metastases; (c) exogenous humanin protected TNBC cells from doxorubicin-induced apoptosis and impaired the anti-metastatic effect of chemotherapy; (d) shRNA silencing of endogenous humanin reduced tumor cell viability and enhanced chemosensitivity [3, animal]. The implication is that exogenous humanin supplementation could, in a host with occult or established breast cancer, protect malignant cells from both immune-mediated apoptosis and cytotoxic chemotherapy.

**Glioblastoma (GBM):** Ha et al. (2024, Cell Death Dis, PMID 38942749) showed that humanin directly binds integrin αV on GBM cells, activating the integrin αV–TGFβ signaling axis, which drives Rac1/Cdc42-mediated cytoskeletal remodeling, filopodia formation, enhanced cell migration, invasion, and angiogenesis (elevated VEGF, VEGFR2, CD31). In orthotopic xenograft mice, humanin-treated animals showed significantly shorter survival, larger tumors, and aggressive invasiveness compared to controls. TGFβR1 inhibition reversed these effects [4, animal]. The authors explicitly flag that humanin's historically beneficial cytoprotective properties may prove "detrimental in cancer contexts."

**Clinical implication (theoretical, no human data):** Any individual with a personal or family history of cancer, or with suspected occult malignancy, faces an uncharacterized but mechanistically plausible risk from exogenous humanin or HNG. This is a hard contraindication candidate pending human safety data. The anti-apoptotic mechanism is not a theoretical concern — it is documented in two separate cancer models across two different tumor types and two different signaling pathways.

### D.5 Regulatory Status

**FDA:** Humanin (including HNG and related analogs) has NO approval from any governmental regulatory health authority for human therapeutic use. It is not an FDA-approved drug. It is not listed in the U.S. Pharmacopoeia [1, regulatory].

**503A compounding status:** Humanin does not appear on the 503A Category 1 Bulk Drug Substances list (substances that may be used in compounding). Humanin does not appear on the FDA's Category 2 list (substances presenting significant safety risks subject to enforcement) as of June 2026 [5, regulatory]. In April 2026, FDA announced removal of 12 peptides from Category 2 and scheduled PCAC review meetings for July 2026; the 7 peptides nominated for the July 23–24 PCAC session are BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Emideltide (DSIP), Semax, and Epitalon [6, regulatory]. Humanin does not appear among those 7 nominated peptides. The April 2026 FDA announcement (as reported by [6]) does not identify the 12 removed substances by name, so humanin's absence from that specific removal cohort cannot be independently confirmed from the cited source; what can be confirmed is that humanin does not appear on the Category 1 or Category 2 active lists, nor among the July 2026 PCAC nominees. Humanin's absence from both active lists means it occupies an unresolved regulatory grey zone: it lacks 503A authorization to be compounded, lacks a monograph, and is not a component of any approved drug. Under the 503A framework as interpreted by FDA, compounding humanin for human use is not permitted [5, regulatory; 6, regulatory].

**503B (outsourcing facilities):** No 503B pathway exists for humanin; it is not on the 503B bulks list.

**Grey-market status:** Humanin and HNG are sold via online "research chemical" or "research peptide" vendors. These products are not subject to FDA manufacturing oversight, and their purity, sterility, and concentration cannot be assumed [vendor_label — sourcing quality context only].

**WADA:** Humanin is prohibited in competitive sport under **S0 — Non-Approved Substances**. The 2026 WADA Prohibited List defines S0 as: "Any pharmacological substance which is not addressed by any of the subsequent sections of the List and with no current approval by any governmental regulatory health authority for human therapeutic use (e.g., drugs under pre-clinical or clinical development or discontinued, designer drugs, substances approved only for veterinary use)" [7, regulatory]. ### D.6 Contraindications, Monitoring & Stopping Criteria

Given the absence of human trials, the following framework is precautionary and mechanistically grounded, not evidence-based in the clinical sense:

**Absolute contraindications (precautionary, based on mechanism and animal data):**
- Active malignancy (any type) — anti-apoptotic mechanism demonstrated to protect tumor cells from chemotherapy and to promote metastasis in TNBC [3, animal]
- Personal history of cancer where recurrence risk is active — same mechanistic concern
- Glioblastoma or other aggressive CNS tumors — humanin activates pro-invasive integrin αV–TGFβ signaling [4, animal]
- Competitive athletes subject to WADA code — S0 prohibited substance [7, regulatory]

**Strong relative contraindications / caution signals:**
- First-degree family history of breast cancer or GBM — insufficient data to rule out risk potentiation
- Any situation where apoptosis suppression is clinically undesirable (e.g., active antitumor immunotherapy)

**Monitoring (if used in research/experimental context):**
- Baseline cancer screening (CBC with differential, relevant tumor markers based on personal risk) before initiation
- Periodic screening maintained during any extended use
- Oncology consultation if cancer history present

**Stopping criteria:**
- Any new cancer diagnosis during administration — STOP immediately; alert treating oncologist to anti-apoptotic activity
- Unexplained lymphadenopathy, weight loss, or constitutional symptoms — STOP and evaluate
- Any regulatory change reclassifying humanin to an explicitly prohibited compound for compounding
