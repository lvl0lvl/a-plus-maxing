## Section C — Aging, longevity & broad cytoprotection

### C.1 Plasma humanin declines with age across species

Circulating humanin falls with age in multiple species. In rhesus macaques (n = 86 across three age cohorts), a dramatic decline was observed between 19 and 25 years of age. In mice, plasma humanin dropped approximately 40% over the first 18 months of life. Naked mole rats — a negligible-senescence model with a ~30-year lifespan — showed only a non-significant trend toward decline (p = 0.08, n = 10), contrasting sharply with the pattern in shorter-lived rodents [1, cohort]. These cross-species observations are consistent with a GeroScience review noting the same trajectory: plasma humanin declines in mice, humans, and monkeys but not in naked mole rats [2, mechanism_review]. All data here are observational; no experiment has tested whether reversing the age-related decline extends lifespan in mammals.

### C.2 Centenarian and longevity-cohort associations (human, observational)

The most direct human evidence comes from a cohort study by Yen et al. (2020) in *Aging*: offspring of centenarians (n = 18) displayed significantly higher circulating humanin compared with age-matched controls without exceptional longevity family history (n = 19) [1, cohort]. Because offspring of centenarians have enriched longevity genetics, elevated humanin may be a marker co-inherited with longevity biology rather than a causal driver — the study design cannot distinguish these possibilities.

The same paper also reported that humanin overexpression in *C. elegans* (transgenic lines, n not individually reported) was sufficient to extend lifespan in a daf-16/FOXO-dependent manner, but this is an invertebrate finding and does not establish causation in mammals [1, cohort].

### C.3 The GH/IGF-1–humanin axis in long-lived mouse models

A mechanistic thread connecting humanin to mammalian longevity comes from the GH/IGF-1 axis. Lee et al. (2014, *Aging Cell*) showed that IGF-I suppresses humanin expression and that plasma humanin is inversely proportional to GH/IGF-1 activity across several engineered mouse lines [3, animal]:

| Model | GH/IGF status | Humanin change | Lifespan effect |
|-------|---------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Ames dwarf (df/df) | GH/IGF deficient | +40% | Extended |
| GH-transgenic | GH/IGF elevated | −70% | Shortened (~50%) |
| LID mice | High GH, low IGF-I | +45% | Normal |
| BP3KO mice | Enhanced free IGF-I | −70% | Shortened |

In GH-deficient children (human, n = 11), plasma humanin correlated negatively with IGF-I (r = −0.69, p < 0.05), and four weeks of GH treatment reduced plasma humanin by ~20%. In an Ecuadorian cohort with GH receptor deficiency (n = 6, Laron syndrome), humanin was ~80% higher than matched controls [3, animal]. These human data are observational and confounded by the primary GH deficiency phenotype — they do not demonstrate that raising humanin pharmacologically extends human lifespan.

**Critical framing:** all Ames dwarf / GHRKO lifespan data are genetic-model findings in mice (Mus musculus). The humanin elevation in these long-lived animals is associative; no experiment has shown that restoring humanin to normal in GH-transgenic mice (or reducing it in Ames dwarfs) modifies their lifespan accordingly.

### C.4 The HNG analog: the dominant in-vivo cytoprotection agent

The majority of in-vivo cytoprotection studies use **S14G-humanin (HNG)**, a synthetic analog in which serine at position 14 is replaced with glycine. HNG binds the tripartite FPRL1/GRP75/CNTFR receptor complex with approximately 1000-fold greater potency than native humanin and has a longer effective half-life in tissue, making it the practical tool for efficacy studies. **Results obtained with HNG cannot be mapped 1:1 onto native humanin or onto orally/subcutaneously administered humanin peptide.** This is a recurring analog-conflation confound analogous to the SS-31 / native SS-peptide distinction in that literature.

### C.5 Cardioprotection

**Mouse (I/R model):** In C57BL/6J mice subjected to 45-minute left coronary artery occlusion followed by 24-hour reperfusion, HNG (2 mg/kg i.p. or intracardiac) administered before or at reperfusion reduced infarct size by ~50% and improved left ventricular function. Protection was dose-dependent and route-independent [summarized in 2, mechanism_review; primary mouse study sourced through ATVB; see also porcine study 4].

**Porcine (I/R model):** Sharp et al. (2020, *JACC: Basic to Translational Science*) tested HNG (2 mg/kg i.v., 10 min before reperfusion) in female Yucatan minipigs (n = 14 in the 60-min ischemia group, n = 19 in the 75-min group). At 60 min of ischemia, HNG reduced infarct size relative to area-at-risk by 41% (p = 0.017) and apoptosis by 50% (p = 0.019). When ischemic duration was extended to 75 min, the same HNG dose provided no significant protection (p = 0.541), indicating dose- and ischemia-duration dependence that complicates translation [4, animal].

**Chronic heart failure (mouse):** Zhao et al. (2023, *Heliyon*) administered HNG (4 mg/kg) to male C57BL/6J mice (n = 55 total) in two heart-failure models: isoproterenol-induced cardiomyopathy (28 days) and transverse aortic constriction. HNG preserved ejection fraction, reduced fibrosis (TGF-β–Smad2/3 suppression), and attenuated cardiomyocyte apoptosis in both models [5, animal]. All findings are HNG analog in rodents.

### C.6 Vascular / endothelial protection

Oh et al. (2011, *Atherosclerosis*) used **HNGF6A**, a second-generation humanin analog, in ApoE-deficient mice (n = 12 per group, 4 groups) fed a high-cholesterol diet for 16 weeks. Daily i.p. HNGF6A reduced atherosclerotic plaque area in the proximal aorta by ~89%, restored endothelial nitric oxide synthase expression, reduced plaque apoptosis by ~57%, and preserved acetylcholine-induced vascular relaxation — without altering systemic lipid profiles or cytokine levels [6, animal]. Again: **HNGF6A analog, not native humanin, in a mouse atherosclerosis model**.

In 40 human patients undergoing coronary angiography (observational), Widmer et al. (2013, *Am J Physiol Heart Circ Physiol*) found that endogenous circulating humanin was significantly lower in patients with coronary endothelial dysfunction vs. those with preserved function (1.3 ± 1.1 vs. 2.2 ± 1.5 ng/mL, p = 0.03); plasma humanin correlated positively with coronary blood flow response (p = 0.009) [7, cohort]. This is an observational association of endogenous humanin — it does not test exogenous administration.

### C.7 Renal protection

Shi et al. (2024, *Biochemical and Biophysical Research Communications*) created a lipopolysaccharide-induced septic acute kidney injury model in male C57BL/6 mice (n not individually reported in abstract). HNG (S14G-humanin) treatment improved renal function, reduced tubular injury, attenuated pro-inflammatory cytokines, and reduced oxidative stress markers. Proteomic analysis identified 132 altered proteins linked to fatty acid degradation and PPAR signaling. The authors describe this as "the first study to show HNG's protective effects in S-AKI" [8, animal]. No native humanin renal efficacy studies in humans were identified.

---

### C.8 Translation gap: no exogenous humanin intervention data in humans

No registered or published randomized controlled trial has tested exogenous humanin — native peptide or any analog — for aging, longevity, cardioprotection, or any cytoprotective endpoint in human subjects. The observational human data (centenarian offspring cohort [1]; endogenous humanin and coronary function [7]; GH-deficient cohorts [3]) document associations with endogenous circulating levels; they do not test the therapeutic hypothesis. Native humanin's plasma half-life of approximately 20 minutes further complicates any straightforward administration strategy. The entire cytoprotection efficacy evidence base is: (a) exogenous analogs (HNG, HNGF6A), not native humanin; (b) rodent and porcine animal models; (c) no human RCT. This gap must be stated explicitly in any clinical framing of humanin.
