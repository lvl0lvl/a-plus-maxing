## Section D — Safety, dosing, route, PK, regulatory & monitoring

### D.1 Mechanism of Action (safety-relevant context)

Melanotan-II (MT-II; cyclo[Nle⁴,D-Phe⁷]-α-MSH) is a non-selective agonist at MC1R, MC3R, MC4R, and MC5R [1, mechanism_review] [8, open_label]. The breadth of receptor engagement is the direct mechanistic basis for most adverse events: MC1R drives melanogenesis; MC4R drives sexual arousal, appetite suppression, and sympathomimetic autonomic effects; MC3R/MC4R combined activity likely accounts for the cardiovascular and CNS adverse signals.

---

### D.2 Safety: Adverse-Event Profile

#### D.2.1 Common (MC-mediated, dose-dependent)

**Nausea and vomiting** are the most consistently reported adverse effects, occurring at essentially every dose level tested in the only published human phase I trial [2, open_label]. The Dorr 1996 study noted "mild nausea, not requiring antiemetic treatment, was reported at most MT-II dose levels" across its three subjects receiving 0.01–0.03 mg/kg SC on alternating days [2, open_label]. Post-market case literature and pharmacovigilance data describe nausea as very common in grey-market use; it is MC4R-mediated (the same receptor targeted by appetite-suppressing compounds). Antiemetic pre-treatment is common in grey-market practice but is not studied or validated.

**Facial flushing** occurred concurrently with nausea in the Dorr trial and is reported across all subsequent case series [2, open_label]. Mechanism: MC3R/MC4R-mediated vasodilation.

**Spontaneous penile erections / priapism.** Penile erections lasting 1–5 hours after dosing were documented in the Dorr 1996 phase I trial in male subjects; the effect correlated with dose and co-occurred with a yawning-and-stretching complex [2, open_label]. Prolonged erections (priapism) are a documented risk with supra-therapeutic doses — priapism can cause permanent erectile dysfunction if not treated urgently. This effect is the pharmacological basis for the derivative compound bremelanotide (PT-141, FDA-approved for HSDD).

**Fatigue and somnolence** at higher doses (grade II at 0.03 mg/kg in one of two escalated subjects in the Dorr trial) [2, open_label].

**Loss of appetite** via MC4R-mediated central suppression of food intake — reported in pharmacovigilance data [3, regulatory].

---

#### D.2.2 Dermatologic signals (load-bearing risk tier)

**Darkening of existing nevi and new nevus formation.** Multiple case reports document rapid darkening of existing moles, development of new melanocytic nevi, and histopathologically confirmed dysplastic nevi temporally associated with MT-II use. Evidence tier: case reports (tier 3) but the biological mechanism is coherent and regulator-acknowledged.

*Case 1 (Sivyer 2012):* 16-year-old female with FAMMM syndrome received 0.5 mg SC daily for 2 months (obtained via internet, needles from needle-exchange program). Within 3 weeks: generalised tanning and darkening of multiple moles. A groin nevus enlarged to 14 × 8 mm with dermoscopic 4-colour pattern and blue-white structures; histopathology confirmed dysplastic compound nevus with moderate cytoarchitectural atypia. Three months post-cessation, skin paled and dermoscopic network lightened. The authors cautioned that melanotan use complicates nevus monitoring and may accelerate progression in high-risk patients [4, open_label].

*Case 2 (Hueso-Gabriel et al. 2012):* 25-year-old male with phototype II received subcutaneous MT-II for 4 weeks. Erupted more than 100 new melanocytic nevi, predominantly on his back; the 10 most atypical lesions were excised — 3 showed severe dysplasia on histopathology, and one pigmented basal cell carcinoma was identified on the right shoulder [5, open_label].

**Melanoma association.** Multiple published case reports describe melanoma arising during or shortly after MT-II use, including melanoma in-situ cases (a 2024 case report describing a 66-year-old male, Fitzpatrick type II, who developed melanoma in-situ at the right mandibular angle after 4 weeks of self-administered MT-II could not be verified to a primary source and is excluded from citation; the pattern is corroborated by other indexed literature). The causal inference is confounded by concurrent UV exposure (tanning beds, solar) in virtually every reported case; no controlled epidemiological study exists. The biological mechanism — chronic stimulation of melanocyte proliferation and MC1R-driven melanomagenesis — is plausible and consistent. Regulator position (TGA, MHRA): the dermatologic risk is a stated basis for public warnings [3, regulatory] [6, regulatory]. **Evidence tier for melanoma causation: tier 3 (case reports only; confounded by UV co-exposure). The biologic mechanism is tier 1 (established MC1R carcinogenesis pathway). The regulator-acknowledged risk is tier 1.**

---

#### D.2.3 Serious adverse events (case reports — flag as such)

All entries below are case-report tier (tier 3) for MT-II causation. The pattern is multi-organ and internally consistent with the compound's autonomic/sympathomimetic profile.

**Rhabdomyolysis and systemic toxicity** *(Nelson, Bryant, Aks 2012; PMID 23121206; Clin Toxicol)*: A 39-year-old male injected 6 mg SC (approximately six times the starting dose used in the Dorr trial). Within 2 hours: BP 151/85 mmHg, HR 130–146 bpm, mydriasis, diaphoresis, diffuse muscle tremors. CPK rose from 1,760 IU/L (admission) to 17,773 IU/L at 12 hours (confirming rhabdomyolysis); creatinine 2.25 mg/dL with proteinuria and red cell casts. Treated with benzodiazepines, IV fluids, sodium bicarbonate; 3-day ICU admission. Authors concluded: "Melanotan II use resulted in systemic toxicity including apparent sympathomimetic symptoms, rhabdomyolysis, and renal dysfunction." [7, open_label]

**Renal infarction** *(Peters et al. 2020; PMID 31953620; CEN Case Reports)*: 45-year-old previously healthy male received 27 mg MT-II SC over 6 months (internet purchase). Presented with right-sided abdominal pain and vomiting. CT: right-sided renal infarction affecting approximately 50% of the kidney. BP 165/95 mmHg (previously normotensive); CRP 152 mg/L. Normal renal arteries (no thromboembolic source found). Authors proposed "thrombotic pharmacological influence and possible direct toxic effect on renal parenchyma" as mechanism. This is the first published case of MT-II-associated renal infarction [8, open_label].

**Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES)**: A letter in *Annals of Internal Medicine* (2013; DOI 10.7326/0003-4819-158-9-201305070-00020) documents PRES with seizures and white-matter abnormalities on MRI in a patient using melanotan [9, open_label]. The mechanism is consistent with the acute hypertensive effect of MC4R agonism.

#### D.2.4 Grey-market-specific risk amplifiers

Beyond the pharmacologic hazards, the grey-market supply chain introduces compounding risks not present in any licensed product:

- **Purity unknown:** MT-II is sold as lyophilised powder from unregulated online suppliers with no quality testing, no manufacturing standards, no sterility assurance. Contamination with endotoxin, heavy metals, and mis-labelled peptide sequences is documented in analogous peptide markets.
- **Shared needles / cross-contamination:** Self-injection carries risk of bloodborne pathogen transmission (HIV, hepatitis B/C). The Sivyer case explicitly documented needle procurement from a needle-exchange program [4, open_label].
- **Reconstitution errors:** Bacteriostatic water vs sterile water, concentration errors, and inadequate sterile technique are endemic in self-injection practice.
- **No adverse event reporting pathway:** Users experiencing side effects have no pharmacovigilance channel; serious events are likely heavily under-reported relative to true incidence.
- **MHRA pharmacovigilance data (2013):** The MHRA received 18 Yellow Card reports describing 74 separate suspected adverse reactions, including stomach disorders, heart problems, and blood and eye disorders [6, regulatory]. The MHRA branded melanotan "dangerous" and closed 72 supply websites in a single three-month enforcement window [6, regulatory].
- **TGA adverse event data:** The TGA's public warning specifically cites kidney dysfunction, swelling of the brain, and skin cancer risk [3, regulatory].

### D.3 Dosing and Route

#### D.3.1 Academic trial doses (citeable; open_label tier)

The Dorr 1996 pilot phase I trial — the primary human study — used SC injections on alternating days in three male volunteers [2, open_label]:

- Starting dose: 0.01 mg/kg SC
- Two subjects escalated to 0.03 mg/kg; one to 0.025 mg/kg via 0.005 mg/kg increments
- Duration: 2 consecutive weeks (5 doses over 10 days)
- Tanning effect observed in 2 of 3 subjects after 5 doses, measurable one week post-last dose
- Dose-limiting effects (nausea, somnolence) emerged at 0.03 mg/kg

These are the only peer-reviewed SC dosing data available for human use. There is no established therapeutic dose because no regulatory authority has approved MT-II for any indication.

#### D.3.2 Grey-market dosing conventions (vendor_label / anecdote_aggregate — NO numbers grounded)

Grey-market communities circulate "loading" and "maintenance" protocols, typically involving daily injections for an initial period followed by less frequent dosing. These protocols are derived from anecdote and vendor materials, not clinical trial data. They are not cited here as dose guidance; the Nelson rhabdomyolysis case used a dose approximately six-fold the Dorr starting dose, illustrating the danger of community dose-escalation norms [7, open_label].

The Sivyer case (0.5 mg fixed dose daily × 2 months) and the Peters renal infarction case (27 mg total over 6 months) both reflect grey-market cumulative exposures with no pharmacist or physician oversight [4, open_label] [8, open_label].

---

### D.4 Pharmacokinetics

Published PK data for MT-II in humans are sparse. The Dorr 1996 trial did not report plasma half-life, Cmax, or Tmax. MT-II is an unmodified cyclic heptapeptide; by analogy with structurally similar peptides and limited preclinical data, the plasma half-life is estimated at approximately 30 minutes to 1–2 hours — consistent with enzymatic degradation and renal clearance typical of non-PEGylated, non-lipidated short peptides. No human PK study has been formally published. The approved relative afamelanotide (Melanotan I; Scenesse) uses a subcutaneous biodegradable implant delivering sustained release over approximately 60 days — a formulation specifically developed to avoid the peak-trough cycling of bolus SC injection. MT-II has no such formulation.

**Route:** SC injection (the trial route and grey-market convention). Nasal spray formulations are also sold but lack any human PK or efficacy data. IV use is occasionally reported in pharmacovigilance data and carries infection and embolic risk.

### D.5 Regulatory Status

#### D.5.1 United States (FDA)

MT-II is **not approved by the FDA for any indication.** The FDA issued a warning letter to Melanocorp as early as 2007 over illegal marketing of Melanotan II. The FDA has subsequently stated that tanning injections "are not approved by the FDA and are not safe to use" [10, regulatory].

**503A / 503B compounding status:** MT-II is placed in FDA Category 2 for bulk compounding (meaning compounding pharmacies may NOT compound it under 503A or 503B) [10, regulatory]. [Fetch disclosure: the specific FDA URL cited for the Category-2 placement and a purported PCAC review date returned 404; the Category-2 classification is corroborated by FDA enforcement materials and secondary regulatory reporting, but the specific PCAC review date could not be verified from a live primary source and is omitted here.] Until and unless reclassified, MT-II cannot be legally compounded for human use in the US [10, regulatory].

By contrast: **afamelanotide (Scenesse)** — a selective MC1R agonist — received FDA approval in 2019 for prevention of phototoxicity in adults with erythropoietic protoporphyria (EPP). **Bremelanotide (Vyleesi; PT-141)** received FDA approval in 2019 for premenopausal women with hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD). MT-II itself remains unapproved; neither approval extends to MT-II.

#### D.5.2 United Kingdom (MHRA)

MT-II is **unlicensed and illegal to supply** in the UK. The MHRA issued a public warning in 2013 stating that melanotan has "not been tested for safety, quality or effectiveness" and that its side effects are unknown in terms of severity. By 2013, the MHRA had contacted 18 companies, shut down 72 supply websites, and received 18 Yellow Card reports documenting 74 suspected adverse reactions (stomach, cardiac, blood, and ocular disorders) [6, regulatory]. The MHRA has described the product as "dangerous." Earlier (2008), the MHRA advised consumers not to use melanotan, noting it is sold "illegally" [11, regulatory].

#### D.5.3 Australia (TGA)

MT-II is **prohibited in Australia** under the Therapeutic Goods Act 1989. The TGA explicitly states it is illegal to supply, or advertise, any tanning product containing melanotan without a prescription, and that enforcement extends to social media influencers [3, regulatory]. In a documented enforcement action, the TGA issued 27 infringement notices totalling AUD $101,412 to a NSW-based individual for unlawful supply of Melanotan II [3, regulatory]. The TGA's public warning specifically cites the risks of kidney dysfunction, swelling of the brain, and skin cancer [3, regulatory].

#### D.5.4 WADA (Doping status)

MT-II is **not explicitly named** on the 2026 WADA Prohibited List [12, regulatory]. As a synthetic peptide that mimics α-MSH (a peptide hormone), it **may** fall under S2 (Peptide Hormones, Growth Factors, Related Substances and Mimetics) via the S2 catch-all clause, which covers substances "with similar chemical structure or similar biological effect(s)" to named S2 compounds — but this has not been confirmed by direct retrieval of the S2 subcategory text (the WADA PDF was inaccessible at fetch; no secondary regulatory source reviewed — including USADA — explicitly lists melanotan or melanocortin under S2). **Tested athletes MUST verify MT-II's status directly with their National Anti-Doping Organization (NADO) or the WADA list before any use.** The 2026 Prohibited List was approved by WADA's Executive Committee on 11 September 2025 and is in force as of 1 January 2026 [12, regulatory].

### D.6 Contraindications and High-Risk Populations

No formal contraindication list exists (MT-II is unapproved and unstudied in most populations). Based on the mechanism and adverse-event profile, absolute avoidance is warranted in:

- **Personal or family history of melanoma or dysplastic nevus syndrome / FAMMM** — the Sivyer case directly illustrates accelerated nevus atypia in a genetically predisposed patient [4, open_label]
- **Multiple (>50) atypical nevi** — baseline melanocytic burden amplifies the dermatologic monitoring burden and the absolute risk of malignant transformation
- **Cardiovascular disease, hypertension, or arrhythmia** — the sympathomimetic MC4R profile (acute hypertension, tachycardia) in the Nelson case occurred in an apparently healthy 39-year-old; cardiac risk in patients with pre-existing disease is uncharacterised and likely elevated
- **Renal impairment** — given the renal infarction case and the nephrotoxic signal in the rhabdomyolysis case
- **History of seizures or neurological conditions** — the PRES case with seizures [9, open_label]
- **Male patients with risk factors for priapism** — sickle cell trait, use of PDE5 inhibitors, anticoagulants
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding** — no data; MC4R agonism has potential reproductive-axis effects

---

### D.7 Monitoring and Stopping Criteria

Because MT-II is used only outside licensed medicine, the following represents expert-derived harm-reduction framing, not approved clinical protocol.

**Dermatologic monitoring (highest priority):**
- Full-body skin examination with total-body photography before any use and at intervals during use — baseline documentation is essential because MT-II rapidly changes lesion appearance
- Dermoscopy of all nevi at baseline and after the first weeks of use; the Sivyer case showed dermoscopic changes within 3 weeks [4, open_label]
- Any new, enlarging, darkening, or morphologically changing nevus is a **hard stopping criterion** — referral for dermatologic evaluation and excision biopsy if indicated
- Users with FAMMM syndrome, Clark nevus syndrome, personal or family history of melanoma, Fitzpatrick type I/II, or >50 moles should be advised against use; if they proceed despite advice, dermoscopic surveillance should be intensified

**Cardiovascular monitoring:**
- Baseline BP and HR; discontinue on sustained hypertension (>150/95 mmHg) or tachycardia (>100 bpm at rest) not otherwise explained

**Renal monitoring:**
- Baseline serum creatinine and urinalysis; monitor during prolonged or cumulative use; discontinue on rising creatinine, proteinuria, or flank pain

**Stopping criteria (any one is sufficient):**
1. Any changing, new, or atypical nevus — dermatology referral before resuming
2. Prolonged erection (>4 hours) — emergency evaluation for priapism
3. Significant nausea/vomiting impairing function
4. BP >150/95 mmHg or HR >100 bpm not otherwise explained
5. Muscle pain + dark urine (rhabdomyolysis screen)
6. Neurological symptoms: headache with visual changes, seizure, confusion (PRES screen)
7. Unilateral flank pain (renal infarction screen)
