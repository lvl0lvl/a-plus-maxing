# Section B — Reference Ranges, Units, and the "Serum-Is-a-Poor-Marker" Problem

## Units and Reference Ranges

RBC magnesium (erythrocyte or intracellular magnesium) is reported in **mg/dL of packed red blood cells**; the SI equivalent is mmol/L, converted by **mg/dL ÷ 2.43 ≈ mmol/L** [1, vendor_label]. For reference, **serum magnesium** sits at approximately **1.7–2.4 mg/dL (0.70–1.00 mmol/L)** in adults [3, mechanism_review] — a much lower range, since erythrocytes are selectively enriched 2–3× relative to plasma.

The analytically derived reference interval for adult RBC magnesium comes from a 2024 ICP-MS method-validation study by Bithi et al. using ~25,000 patient samples: **4.2–6.7 mg/dL** [2, cohort]. That study noted that legacy intervals used by many labs are based on older, less precise methods. Commercial laboratory directories illustrate how wide the inter-laboratory spread is: ARUP, for example, reports 3.6–7.5 mg/dL [1, vendor_label] — a range that does not coincide with the analytically derived Bithi interval, and the lower-bound disagreement alone (3.6 vs. 4.2 mg/dL) is large enough to reclassify a patient. No regulatory body has issued a harmonised reference interval for RBC magnesium. Functional medicine contexts propose an "optimal" range of 5.5–6.4 mg/dL, but this has not been validated against clinical outcomes in randomised trials.

The lack of a universal reference interval is a genuine limitation of the test, not a minor footnote. Different lysis protocols, analytical methods (atomic absorption spectrometry vs. dye-binding vs. ICP-MS), and haematocrit-correction approaches produce systematically different results [7, mechanism_review].

## Why Serum Magnesium Is a Poor Marker

The core problem: **less than 1% of total-body magnesium resides in the extracellular fluid**. The remainder is distributed in bone (~60%) and intracellular soft tissues — none of which serum directly samples [3, mechanism_review]. Serum magnesium is tightly buffered by renal reabsorption, intestinal absorption, and bone-exchange mechanisms. That buffer is exactly what makes serum unreliable: the body draws on intracellular and bone reserves to sustain a normal serum concentration, so serum **falls only after those reserves are substantially depleted**.

The clinical consequence is **chronic latent magnesium deficiency (CLMD)**: meaningful negative magnesium balance with serum concentrations that remain within the conventional reference interval (typically 0.75–0.85 mmol/L). One proposed definition of CLMD is "a serum magnesium concentration of between 0.75 and 0.849 mmol/L (within the reference interval), with a positive magnesium load test indicating magnesium deficiency" [5, mechanism_review] — this threshold originates from a narrative review rather than a consensus body and has not been independently validated in prospective cohorts. In this zone — normal by standard serum criteria — a substantial fraction of individuals are measurably depleted by functional testing.

The clinical evidence is direct. In a study of 246 geriatric outpatients, serum magnesium was within normal range in every patient, yet intra-erythrocyte magnesium was low in 57% of them; compartment correlation was negligible (r = 0.098, p = 0.124) [6, cohort]. Similar dissociation appears in metabolic syndrome (intracellular depletion prevalence 36% vs. serum-defined hypomagnesemia 23%) and in ICU patients (low erythrocyte magnesium in 66% of consecutive admissions vs. a much lower serum-positive rate) [5, mechanism_review].

Serum magnesium has poor **sensitivity** for total-body or intracellular depletion. It retains value as a **specific** marker: frank serum hypomagnesemia (< 0.75 mmol/L / < 1.8 mg/dL) is diagnostic and warrants treatment. The problem is the large grey zone at low-normal values, where serum-normal does not mean replete. Two additional confounders: ~30% of serum magnesium is protein-bound, so hypoalbuminaemia or pH shifts can alter the total value without changing body status; and the physiologically active fraction is **ionised (free) magnesium** (~55–70% of total serum), which requires specialised ion-selective electrodes and is not widely available — its relationship to total-body stores also remains incompletely characterised [4, mechanism_review].

## RBC Magnesium as an Intracellular Surrogate — Honest Caveats Required

RBC magnesium is proposed as a more sensitive intracellular index than serum for two reasons: erythrocytes are intracellular compartments, and their ~120-day lifespan means the measurement integrates status over months rather than reflecting the day-of-draw alone — conceptually analogous to HbA1c [7, mechanism_review]. In the geriatric outpatient study, RBC magnesium identified deficiency serum entirely missed [6, cohort]. In diabetic children, erythrocyte magnesium correlated inversely with loading-test retention (more depletion by the gold-standard test = lower RBC Mg), a relationship absent with serum magnesium in the same cohort [7, mechanism_review].

However, several caveats limit the claim that RBC magnesium is a validated diagnostic gold standard:

**Standardisation is absent.** No universally validated analytical method or reference interval exists. Arnaud's comprehensive review documented erythrocyte magnesium concentrations differing significantly between study groups under comparable conditions [7, mechanism_review]; the 2024 ICP-MS validation further confirmed divergent reference intervals across laboratories [2, cohort]. Genetic factors — including MHC-associated genes — influence intra-erythrocyte magnesium independently of body status, adding biological noise [7, mechanism_review]. Specimen handling matters: haemolysis or delayed cell separation artefactually lowers results [1, vendor_label].

**Clinical trial validation is limited.** Most RBC magnesium studies are cross-sectional or short-term. Few have tested whether targeting a specific RBC magnesium value changes outcomes versus targeting serum or loading-test endpoints [4, mechanism_review].

RBC magnesium is best characterised as a **reasonable but imperfect intracellular surrogate** — more informative than serum alone in the right clinical context, but not a gold standard. Results must be interpreted against the reporting lab's own method and interval.

## The Functional Reference: Magnesium Loading Test

The closest available reference standard for diagnosing body magnesium depletion is the **magnesium loading (retention) test**: an IV magnesium dose (commonly 30 mmol over 8 h, or 0.1 mmol/kg over 1 h in shorter outpatient protocols) followed by 24-hour urine collection. Magnesium-replete individuals excrete most of the load (retention ~2–8% in healthy subjects); depleted individuals retain a larger fraction because intracellular compartments remain absorptive [8, cohort; 9, mechanism_review]. Retention > ~20–27% of the administered dose is considered indicative of deficiency [8, cohort; 9, mechanism_review]. Gullestad et al. showed the loading test revealed deficiency across multiple cardiovascular and metabolic disease groups — even when mean serum magnesium was not significantly lower than controls [8, cohort].

The loading test is impractical for routine screening: it requires IV access, intact renal function, and controlled 24-hour urine collection. It is best reserved for cases where deficiency is strongly suspected but serum and RBC results are equivocal, or where clinical response to supplementation is being formally evaluated.

---

## Clinical Interpretation

- **A normal serum magnesium does not exclude deficiency.** In patients with symptoms (cramps, fatigue, arrhythmia, refractory hypokalemia/hypocalcemia) or risk factors (diuretics, PPIs, diabetes, malabsorption, alcohol use, critical illness), serum alone is inadequate [3, mechanism_review].
- **RBC magnesium** is a reasonable second-line intracellular index; interpret results against the laboratory's own reference interval, not a universal cutpoint.
- When deficiency remains strongly suspected despite a low-normal RBC magnesium, the **magnesium loading test** provides the most direct functional evidence.
- **Ionised serum magnesium** is more physiologically direct than total serum but not routinely available, and its reflection of total-body stores is still uncertain.
