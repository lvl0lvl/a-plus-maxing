# Section A: Physiology & What RBC Magnesium Measures

## The Magnesium Ion: Cellular Indispensability

Magnesium (Mg²⁺) is the **second most abundant intracellular cation** in the human body, after potassium [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review]. A healthy adult contains approximately 20–28 grams of total body magnesium, held predominantly within cells and mineralized tissues. Its biological centrality follows from its chemistry: Mg²⁺ carries a high charge density that makes it an ideal cofactor for stabilizing enzyme–substrate complexes, coordinating phosphate group transfers, and neutralizing the negative charge of nucleic acids.

Magnesium functions as **a cofactor for more than 600 enzymatic reactions** [1, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review], including virtually every process involving cellular energy currency. **All biologically active ATP exists as the Mg-ATP complex** — Mg²⁺ chelates the β- and γ-phosphate groups of adenosine triphosphate, rendering the molecule competent for phosphoryl transfer [2, mechanism_review]. Without adequate intracellular Mg²⁺, ATP is biochemically inert. This dependency propagates across glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (which maintains the resting membrane potential), adenylate cyclase, DNA polymerases, RNA polymerases, and protein synthesis machinery [1, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review]. PTH secretion and its downstream signalling through adenylate cyclase are also Mg²⁺-dependent, explaining why severe hypomagnesemia produces a state of functional hypoparathyroidism with secondary hypocalcemia refractory to calcium replacement alone [4, mechanism_review].

## Body Distribution: Why Serum Is a Misleading Window

Total body magnesium is distributed across three broad compartments in proportions that have direct diagnostic consequences:

- **Bone: ~50–60%** — stored principally at the surface of hydroxyapatite crystals as a slowly-exchangeable reservoir [1, mechanism_review; 5, mechanism_review]
- **Intracellular soft tissue (muscle and organs): ~34–40%** — the functionally active pool; most is bound to ATP, ribosomes, and phosphonucleotides; free cytosolic Mg²⁺ is kept at only ~0.5–1.2 mM [2, mechanism_review]
- **Serum and extracellular fluid: ~0.3–1%** of total body magnesium [1, mechanism_review; 3, mechanism_review]

The critical corollary is stark: **serum magnesium reflects a tiny extracellular pool that does not reliably index total-body or intracellular magnesium status** [4, mechanism_review; 5, mechanism_review]. The normal serum reference interval — **0.75–0.95 mmol/L (1.82–2.30 mg/dL)** [6, regulatory] — was established from population surveys conducted in 1971–1974 and has been critiqued as insufficiently sensitive to detect early depletion [4, mechanism_review]. Because the kidney can maintain serum Mg²⁺ within this range for weeks to months by progressively increasing tubular reabsorption, a patient may lose a substantial fraction of intracellular and bone stores before serum levels fall detectably below the lower reference limit [4, mechanism_review; 5, mechanism_review]. A normal serum magnesium concentration therefore does not rule out functional or intracellular magnesium deficiency.

## RBC Magnesium: Rationale and Limitations

**RBC magnesium (RBC-Mg)** measures the magnesium content of erythrocytes and is proposed as a more accessible window into the **intracellular Mg²⁺ compartment** than serum. Several properties underpin this rationale. First, red blood cells reside entirely in the intracellular milieu by definition — their Mg²⁺ content reflects the cellular pool rather than the extracellular pool sampled by serum tests. Second, erythrocytes circulate for approximately **120 days** before senescence [5, mechanism_review], so measured RBC-Mg integrates magnesium exposure and equilibration over that interval rather than capturing only a momentary state. Third, RBC-Mg constitutes roughly 0.5% of total body magnesium — modestly more than the 0.3% in serum — and in well-designed longitudinal studies has shown earlier and more proportionate decline than serum Mg²⁺ in response to dietary depletion protocols [5, mechanism_review].

The **conventional reference range for RBC-Mg** is approximately **4.2–6.7 mg/dL** [5, mechanism_review]. (Unit note: serum Mg is routinely reported in mmol/L [multiply by 2.43 to convert to mg/dL] or mg/dL; RBC-Mg laboratories typically report in mg/dL.)

RBC-Mg is nonetheless an **imperfect surrogate** for several reasons [4, mechanism_review]: (a) erythrocytes lack mitochondria, nuclei, and most of the metabolic machinery that makes intracellular Mg²⁺ functionally significant in other cell types; (b) the dominant intracellular Mg²⁺ binding partners in red cells are hemoglobin, ATP, and 2,3-diphosphoglycerate rather than the ribosomes and nuclear material dominant in nucleated cells; (c) RBC-Mg lags acute Mg²⁺ changes by weeks; (d) few sufficiently powered randomized trials have cross-validated RBC-Mg against whole-body status measures. The **functional reference standard for intracellular magnesium deficiency** remains the **magnesium-loading (retention) test**: intravenous infusion of a standardized Mg²⁺ dose followed by 24-hour urine collection; healthy subjects retain ~2–8% of the load, whereas retention >27% is considered highly indicative of total-body deficiency [5, mechanism_review]. Muscle biopsy Mg²⁺ content is the most direct tissue measure but is clinically impractical. RBC-Mg is best understood as a practical intermediate — superior to serum for detecting chronic subclinical depletion, but subordinate to the loading test as a diagnostic reference method.

## Magnesium Homeostasis: Kidney, Gut, and Bone

The kidney is the **principal regulator** of magnesium balance [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review]. Approximately 95–99% of filtered Mg²⁺ is reabsorbed along the nephron in a segmentally organized fashion:

- **Thick ascending limb (TAL) of the loop of Henle: 65–70% of reabsorption** — passive, **paracellular** transport driven by the lumen-positive transepithelial voltage and mediated by the tight-junction proteins **claudin-16** and **claudin-19** (CLDN16/CLDN19), which form heterodimeric cation channels selectively permeable to Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺. Loss-of-function mutations cause familial hypomagnesemia with hypercalciuria and nephrocalcinosis [2, mechanism_review].

- **Distal convoluted tubule (DCT): ~5–10% of reabsorption** — active, **transcellular** transport via the apical channel **TRPM6** (transient receptor potential melastatin 6) in heterotetramer complexes with **TRPM7**. This segment sets the final urinary excretion rate and is regulated by insulin, epidermal growth factor (EGF), PTH, and estrogen; it is the primary target of drug-induced hypomagnesemia (EGFR inhibitors, calcineurin inhibitors, proton-pump inhibitors, thiazide diuretics) [1, mechanism_review; 2, mechanism_review].

**Intestinal absorption** accounts for ~30–50% of ingested Mg²⁺ under normal dietary conditions (rising toward 80% during deficiency) [1, mechanism_review]. Approximately 90% of intestinal uptake is passive paracellular via claudin-2, -7, and -12 in the small intestine; transcellular absorption mediated by TRPM6 (expressed in the duodenum and ileum) becomes proportionally more important at low dietary Mg²⁺ loads [1, mechanism_review].

**Bone** serves as a slowly-exchangeable Mg²⁺ reservoir: at reduced plasma concentrations, surface-bound Mg²⁺ can be mobilized into the extracellular space; at elevated concentrations, Mg²⁺ is deposited back onto hydroxyapatite [4, mechanism_review]. This buffer function can sustain serum levels during dietary deficiency, further concealing intracellular depletion from serum-only assessment.
