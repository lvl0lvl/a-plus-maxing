# Section A: Physiology & The HPT Axis

## TSH: Molecular Identity

Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), also called thyrotropin, is a heterodimeric glycoprotein of approximately 28 kDa produced by thyrotroph cells of the anterior pituitary [1, mechanism_review]. Like the other pituitary glycoprotein hormones — luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) — TSH is composed of two non-covalently linked polypeptide subunits: a common α-subunit shared across this family and a hormone-specific β-subunit that confers binding selectivity for the TSH receptor (TSH-R) on thyroid follicular cells [1, mechanism_review]. Both subunits carry asparagine-linked oligosaccharide chains: two on the α-subunit (at Asn-52 and Asn-78) and one on the β-subunit (at Asn-23) [2, mechanism_review]. These carbohydrates account for roughly 15% of the molecule's mass and are essential for correct folding, heterodimerization, protection from intracellular degradation, circulatory half-life, and full biological activity. TRH is specifically required for proper glycosylation of TSH; when TRH is deficient (as in central hypothyroidism), circulating TSH measured by immunoassay may appear normal but is biologically subpotent [1, mechanism_review]. Mature TSH is sulfated and sialylated at the termini of its oligosaccharide chains, modifications that regulate both receptor affinity and metabolic clearance rate [2, mechanism_review].

## The Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Thyroid (HPT) Axis

TSH production is governed by a three-tier neuroendocrine circuit: the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis [3, mechanism_review].

**Hypothalamic drive.** Parvocellular neurons in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus synthesize thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), a tripeptide that travels via the hypothalamic-hypophyseal portal system to the anterior pituitary [3, mechanism_review]. TRH binds Gq-coupled receptors on thyrotrophs, activating phospholipase C, generating IP3 and DAG, mobilizing intracellular calcium, and ultimately driving TSH gene transcription, synthesis, and secretion [1, mechanism_review]. TRH is also obligatory for proper TSH glycosylation; without it, bioactive TSH output falls even if immunoreactive levels appear preserved [1, mechanism_review].

**Pituitary output.** Thyrotrophs respond to TRH by releasing TSH into the systemic circulation. TSH then acts on the basolateral membrane of thyroid follicular cells via TSH-R, stimulating all steps of thyroid hormone biosynthesis and secretion: iodide uptake, thyroid peroxidase-mediated organification, thyroglobulin synthesis, and proteolytic release of T4 and T3 [3, mechanism_review]. The thyroid secretes predominantly thyroxine (T4), with triiodothyronine (T3) representing roughly 20% of direct glandular output; the majority of circulating T3 arises from peripheral deiodination of T4 by type 1 and type 2 deiodinases in liver, kidney, and other tissues [3, mechanism_review].

**Negative feedback.** The axis is closed by a well-characterized negative feedback loop. Circulating free T4 (fT4) and free T3 (fT3) inhibit both TRH secretion at the hypothalamus (where tanycytes convert T4 to T3 via type 2 deiodinase, and the resulting T3 suppresses proTRH gene expression via thyroid hormone receptor-β2) and TSH secretion at the pituitary (where intrapituitary T4-to-T3 conversion also occurs) [3, mechanism_review]. T3 is the predominant intracellular effector of TSH suppression; its affinity for the thyroid hormone receptor is approximately 10-fold greater than that of T4 [1, mechanism_review]. Additional modulatory inputs include dopamine and somatostatin (both inhibitory) and leptin signaling (stimulatory), but TRH stimulation and thyroid hormone negative feedback remain the dominant controls under physiological conditions [3, mechanism_review].

## The Log-Linear Inverse Relationship: Why TSH Is the Most Sensitive Thyroid Test

The central quantitative feature of the HPT axis is the **log-linear inverse relationship between TSH and free T4**: across the physiological range, a relatively small proportional change in circulating fT4 produces a disproportionately large, amplified change in TSH in the opposite direction [4, cohort]. Specifically, when log(TSH) is plotted against fT4, the relationship is approximately linear — a 1 pmol/L decrease in fT4 within the reference range is associated with a two- to threefold rise in TSH concentration [4, cohort].

This amplification is the mechanistic basis for TSH being the single most sensitive first-line test of thyroid status. Because the pituitary thyrotroph acts as a biological integrator that continuously monitors circulating fT4, even subtle departures from an individual's homeostatic set point — departures too small to shift fT4 outside the broad population reference interval — produce detectable TSH excursions. In practice, TSH rises or falls well before fT4 becomes overtly abnormal in early thyroid dysfunction [3, mechanism_review].

It is important to note that while the log-TSH/fT4 relationship is approximately linear within an individual, cross-sectional population data reveal a more complex, nonlinear curve shaped by the superposition of many individual set points at different positions within the population range [4, cohort]. The within-person relationship is predominantly log-linear in those with the most longitudinal data points [4, cohort].

## Pulsatile and Circadian Secretion

TSH is not secreted continuously. It is released in a **pulsatile** pattern with a pulse frequency of approximately 10–12 bursts per 24 hours (roughly one pulse every 2 hours) and an amplitude of approximately 0.6 mIU/L per pulse under basal conditions [5, cohort]. Superimposed on this ultradian rhythm is a pronounced **circadian pattern**: TSH concentrations rise progressively through the late afternoon and evening, reach a nocturnal surge peaking between 21:00 and 02:00, and fall to a nadir in the mid-afternoon [5, cohort]. The difference between afternoon nadir and nocturnal peak is typically 1–3 mIU/L in healthy euthyroid adults. Sleep onset normally blunts any further TSH rise; when sleep is deprived, TSH can climb to 5–10 mIU/L [1, mechanism_review]. The nocturnal TSH surge is not fully reflected in peripheral thyroid hormone concentrations because the TSH molecules secreted at night are more heavily sulfated and therefore less bioactive than daytime TSH [1, mechanism_review]. The circadian rhythm of TSH is generated partly by varying dopaminergic tone and pulsatile TRH input, and is maintained in mild thyroid dysfunction but abolished in severe hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism [1, mechanism_review]. For clinical sampling, this circadian variability is a reason to standardize collection timing; most reference intervals are established from morning draws.

## The Individual Set Point: Population Range vs. Personal Range

The commonly cited population TSH reference interval (approximately 0.4–4.0 mIU/L) reflects substantial **between-person** variation arising from genetic, environmental, and age-related differences in HPT axis calibration. Within a single healthy individual, however, TSH varies across a much narrower personal range [6, cohort]. Andersen et al. demonstrated, in a 12-month longitudinal study of 16 healthy men with monthly sampling, that each person's intra-individual 95% confidence interval for TSH was approximately half the width of the population reference interval [6, cohort]. The index of individuality for TSH (the ratio of within-person to between-person variance) was 0.49 — well below 1.4, the threshold at which population reference intervals perform well for individual surveillance [6, cohort]. A single result within the broad population reference interval is therefore insufficient to confirm that an individual is at their personal set point.

What does TSH ultimately index? It is the pituitary's integrated readout of whether thyroid hormone availability is **sufficient at the tissue level for that individual**. The thyrotroph integrates the history of fT4 exposure over hours to days, not merely the instantaneous fT4 level, making TSH a more reliable chronic signal of thyroid sufficiency than a spot fT4 measurement. Because an individual's set point is genetically influenced and reproducible over time [5, cohort], a shift in TSH away from that person's habitual range carries clinical significance even when the absolute value remains within the population reference interval [6, cohort].
