# Section B — Reference Ranges, Units & Thresholds

## Units

TSH is reported in **milli-international units per litre (mIU/L)**, which is numerically equivalent to **µIU/mL** (micro-international units per millilitre). The two notations are interchangeable; older literature and some US laboratory reports use µIU/mL while most current guidelines use mIU/L.

---

## Standard Adult Reference Interval

The most widely applied adult reference interval for TSH is approximately **0.4–4.0 mIU/L** (some assays report an upper limit of 4.5 mIU/L). The precise limits are both assay-dependent and population-dependent; each laboratory is expected to validate its own interval against a locally derived reference cohort [1, meta_analysis].

The foundational US population data come from NHANES III (1988–1994), a nationally representative survey of 17,353 individuals aged ≥12 years [2, cohort]. Excluding those with known thyroid disease, goitre, or thyroid medications yielded a disease-free population of ~16,533 participants; after further restriction to exclude individuals with thyroid antibodies or biochemical thyroid dysfunction, a reference subset of 13,344 remained [2, cohort]. In that reference subset the **geometric mean TSH was 1.40 ± 0.02 mIU/L**, with a notably higher value in white non-Hispanics (1.45 mIU/L) compared with Black Americans (1.18 mIU/L) and Mexican Americans (1.37 mIU/L) [2, cohort]. The use of a geometric mean reflects a defining feature of TSH measurement: the hormone follows a **log-normal (right-skewed) distribution** in healthy populations. Raw TSH values pile up at lower concentrations and have a long right tail; log-transformation normalises the distribution, and the back-transformed geometric mean approximates the true median. This has two practical consequences — the arithmetic mean exceeds the median, and reference intervals derived without log-transformation will have asymmetric tails [2, cohort].

## The Upper-Limit Debate

### The case for a lower upper limit (~2.5 mIU/L)

In 2003, the **National Academy of Clinical Biochemistry (NACB)** published laboratory practice guidelines recommending that the upper reference limit for TSH be revised downward [3, regulatory]. The rationale was that "greater than 95% of rigorously screened normal euthyroid volunteers" had TSH concentrations between 0.4 and 2.5 mU/L, and that the conventional limit of 4.0–4.5 mIU/L reflected contamination of reference cohorts by individuals with undiagnosed occult autoimmune thyroid disease (subclinical Hashimoto thyroiditis) [3, regulatory].

Wartofsky and Dickey elaborated the same argument in a 2005 JCEM commentary, noting that African-American populations — who have substantially lower rates of Hashimoto thyroiditis — cluster at lower TSH values, and that when autoimmune-positive individuals are excluded the upper end of the distribution contracts toward 2.5 mIU/L [4, mechanism_review]. On this view, a TSH persistently between 2.5 and 4.5 mIU/L should be regarded as a soft signal of early thyroid dysfunction, not a clean "normal."

### The case against narrowing the range

The counter-position has two arms: empirical and clinical.

*Empirically*, the upper reference limit is not a fixed biological constant — it drifts upward substantially with age (see below), varies by ethnicity, and shifts with iodine status and assay methodology [5, meta_analysis]. A single narrow range applied uniformly ignores these sources of variation and risks classifying physiologically normal variation in older adults or certain ethnic groups as disease.

*Clinically*, narrowing the upper limit expands the diagnosis of subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) to a much larger fraction of the population without a corresponding evidence base for treatment benefit. The 2012 joint AACE/ATA clinical practice guidelines for hypothyroidism in adults retained a conventional TSH upper limit and explicitly declined to endorse the 2.5 mIU/L cutoff as a treatment threshold, noting that mild TSH elevations (4.5–9.9 mU/L) have uncertain benefit when treated [6, regulatory]. A 2021 clinical debate published in the *International Journal of Endocrinology and Metabolism* illustrates the remaining controversy: the pro-treatment side cites cardiovascular and fertility data; the anti-treatment side cites spontaneous reversion in ~60% of mild SCH cases within five years and lack of symptom improvement in most randomised trials [7, mechanism_review].

The current pragmatic consensus, reflected in most major society guidelines, is that the conventional range (~0.4–4.5 mIU/L) remains the operational standard, but clinicians should factor in age, autoimmune status, and clinical context when interpreting values near the upper boundary.

---

## Age-Specific Drift

TSH reference distributions shift upward progressively with age, even in individuals without thyroid disease or thyroid antibodies. Surks and Hollowell used the NHANES III reference population to quantify this shift: the **97.5th-percentile upper reference limit was 3.56 mIU/L in 20-to-29-year-olds**, but rose to **7.49 mIU/L in adults aged ≥80 years** — a more than two-fold increase across six decades [8, cohort]. The corresponding mean TSH rose from 1.43 mIU/L to 2.44 mIU/L (a 69% increase) over the same span [8, cohort]. A subsequent meta-analysis confirmed this pattern across multiple populations, finding that the female TSH upper limit rose notably after age 40, and the male upper limit after age 60 [5, meta_analysis].

The clinical implication is substantial: applying a fixed population-wide upper limit of 4.5 mIU/L to older adults causes systematic over-diagnosis of subclinical hypothyroidism. Surks and Hollowell estimated that **~70% of older individuals with TSH >4.5 mIU/L fell within their age-appropriate reference range**, meaning the great majority of apparent SCH in the elderly represents age-related set-point adjustment rather than pathology [8, cohort].

## Pregnancy: Trimester-Specific Ranges

TSH physiology changes substantially during pregnancy. Human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG), which peaks in the first trimester, cross-reacts with the TSH receptor and stimulates thyroid hormone production, causing TSH to fall — sometimes below the standard non-pregnant lower limit. This physiological suppression is most marked in the first trimester and attenuates as hCG falls in the second and third trimesters.

The **2011 ATA guidelines** recommended fixed trimester-specific limits:
- First trimester: 0.1–2.5 mIU/L
- Second trimester: 0.2–3.0 mIU/L
- Third trimester: 0.3–3.5 mIU/L

The **2017 ATA guidelines** (Alexander, Pearce, et al.) represented a significant revision [9, regulatory]. Recognising that trimester-specific ranges vary by population, ethnicity, iodine status, and assay, the 2017 guidelines recommend using **population- and assay-specific reference intervals derived from local iodine-replete pregnant women** whenever available. The preferred approach is a locally validated reference derived from TPOAb-negative, iodine-sufficient pregnant women [9, regulatory].

Where local population-specific data are unavailable, the 2017 ATA guidelines propose a pragmatic fallback: the **upper reference limit is the non-pregnant upper limit minus 0.5 mU/L** (yielding an upper limit of approximately 4.0 mU/L in the first trimester), rather than the prior fixed 2.5 mIU/L cutoff [9, regulatory]. This change reflected accumulating evidence that the 2.5 mIU/L first-trimester upper limit was too restrictive for many populations and was leading to over-diagnosis and over-treatment of hypothyroidism in pregnancy.

The core principle remains: pregnancy TSH targets are **lower than non-pregnant targets**, most critically in the first trimester when fetal thyroid function is not yet established and maternal thyroid hormone supply is the sole source.

## Diagnostic Patterns by TSH Result

| Pattern | TSH | Free T4 | Free T3 | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Subclinical hypothyroidism | Above reference range | Normal | Normal | Compensated early thyroid failure; pituitary senses sub-threshold thyroid output |
| Overt hypothyroidism | Elevated (often >10 mIU/L) | Low | (Low) | Frank thyroid failure; TSH typically markedly elevated |
| Subclinical hyperthyroidism | Below reference range (<0.4 mIU/L); Grade 1: 0.1–0.39; Grade 2: <0.1 | Normal | Normal | Excess thyroid activity suppresses TSH without yet raising hormone levels |
| Overt hyperthyroidism | Suppressed (<0.1 mIU/L) | Elevated | Elevated | Frank thyrotoxicosis |
| Central hypothyroidism | Low or low-normal | Low | — | Pituitary/hypothalamic failure; TSH cannot be interpreted alone |

Subclinical hypothyroidism is formally defined by a serum TSH above the statistically defined upper reference limit in combination with a free T4 within the normal range, provided the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis is intact and the measurement is not taken during acute illness or in the immediate post-operative period [6, regulatory]. Subclinical hyperthyroidism is the mirror image: TSH below the reference lower limit with normal free T4 and free T3 [10, regulatory].
