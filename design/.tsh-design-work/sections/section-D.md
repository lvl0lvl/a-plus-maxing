# Section D: Determinants & Clinical Significance

## TSH as First-Line Screening Test

Serum TSH is the recommended initial test for evaluating thyroid function in virtually all outpatient clinical scenarios [1, regulatory]. Because TSH responds exponentially to small changes in circulating thyroid hormone — a 2-fold change in free T4 produces a 100-fold shift in TSH — the pituitary integrates thyroid hormone exposure over weeks and provides a far more sensitive signal than peripheral hormone levels alone [2, mechanism_review]. Clinical practice follows a reflex-testing model: an abnormal TSH triggers automatic add-on measurement of free T4 (fT4), and in select cases free T3 (fT3), to characterize the direction and severity of dysfunction.

**Overt hypothyroidism:** TSH markedly elevated (often > 10 mIU/L) + low fT4.
**Subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH):** TSH mildly elevated (typically 4.5–9.9 mIU/L) + fT4 within the reference range.
**Overt hyperthyroidism:** TSH suppressed (usually < 0.1 mIU/L) + elevated fT4 and/or fT3.
**Subclinical hyperthyroidism:** TSH suppressed + normal fT4 and fT3.

## Elevated TSH: What It Means and Predicts

### Primary Hypothyroidism

Elevated TSH with low fT4 defines overt primary hypothyroidism, most commonly caused by autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto's). The TSH rise precedes fT4 suppression, which is why mild TSH elevation with preserved fT4 defines the subclinical stage.

### Subclinical Hypothyroidism: Cardiovascular Risk

Whether SCH carries clinically meaningful cardiovascular risk depends strongly on the degree of TSH elevation. The Thyroid Studies Collaboration individual-participant data (IPD) meta-analysis — Rodondi et al., 11 prospective cohorts, 55,287 participants, 542,494 person-years — demonstrated a graded TSH-dependent increase in coronary heart disease (CHD) risk [3, meta_analysis] (Rodondi 2010 JAMA):

| TSH level (mIU/L) | CHD events HR (95% CI) | CHD mortality HR (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|
| 4.5–6.9 | 1.00 (0.86–1.18) | 1.09 (0.91–1.30) |
| 7.0–9.9 | 1.17 (0.96–1.43) | 1.42 (1.03–1.95) |
| 10–19.9 | **1.89 (1.28–2.80)** | **1.58 (1.10–2.27)** |

The clearest signal emerged at TSH ≥ 10 mIU/L, where CHD event risk was approximately doubled. No significant increase in all-cause mortality was observed across the SCH range as a whole. These findings underpin the clinical consensus that TSH ≥ 10 mIU/L is a stronger treatment trigger than mild SCH in younger adults.

### The Treatment Controversy: The TRUST Trial

Despite the epidemiological associations, the pivotal TRUST (Thyroid hormone Replacement for Untreated older adults with Subclinical hypothyroidism — a randomised placebo controlled Trial) provides the strongest evidence against routine levothyroxine replacement in older individuals with SCH [4, rct] (Stott 2017 NEJM). In 737 adults aged ≥65 years (mean age 74.4) with persistent SCH (TSH 4.60–19.99 mIU/L), participants were randomized to levothyroxine dose-titration to normalize TSH vs. placebo. After 1 year, the primary outcome — Hypothyroid Symptom score and Tiredness score — showed no difference between groups (between-group difference: −0.2 points, 95% CI −1.5 to 1.0, and 0.2 points, 95% CI −1.0 to 1.5 respectively). There were no significant differences in secondary cardiovascular outcomes, quality of life, or muscle strength. The TRUST trial materially shifted clinical practice: in older patients with mild SCH, routine treatment is not supported by evidence of symptomatic benefit, even when TSH is normalized biochemically.

**Risk stratification summary:** Treatment decisions in SCH weigh TSH level, age, symptom burden, and comorbidities. The TSH ≥ 10 mIU/L threshold — particularly in younger patients — retains the strongest case for treatment. In those > 70 years with TSH 5–10 mIU/L, watchful waiting is the predominant evidence-based approach.

## Suppressed TSH: What It Means and Predicts

### Overt and Subclinical Hyperthyroidism

TSH suppression to < 0.1 mIU/L with elevated fT4 (and often fT3) defines overt hyperthyroidism, most commonly Graves' disease or a toxic nodular goiter. Subclinical hyperthyroidism (sCHyper) — suppressed TSH with normal fT4/fT3 — can be endogenous (Graves', autonomous nodule) or exogenous (over-replacement with levothyroxine).

### Atrial Fibrillation and Mortality Risk

The Thyroid Studies Collaboration IPD analysis by Collet et al. (10 cohorts, 52,674 participants) quantified the sCHyper risk for hard cardiovascular endpoints [5, meta_analysis] (Collet 2012 Arch Intern Med):

- All-cause mortality: **HR 1.24 (95% CI 1.06–1.46)**
- CHD mortality: **HR 1.29 (95% CI 1.02–1.62)**
- Atrial fibrillation: **HR 1.68 (95% CI 1.16–2.43)**

Risk was concentrated in those with TSH < 0.10 mIU/L, consistent with the pattern seen in the Cappola et al. cohort study of 3,233 adults ≥ 65 years, in which suppressed TSH was associated with atrial fibrillation at 67 vs. 31 events per 1,000 person-years (adjusted HR 1.98) [6, cohort] (Cappola 2006 JAMA).

### Fracture Risk

Subclinical hyperthyroidism accelerates bone turnover via direct TSH-receptor-mediated effects on osteoclasts as well as thyroid hormone effects on cortical bone. The Blum et al. IPD meta-analysis (13 cohorts, 70,298 participants) reported subclinical hyperthyroidism (overall) was associated with [7, meta_analysis] (Blum 2015 JAMA):

- Hip fracture: HR 1.36 (95% CI 1.13–1.64)
- Any fracture: HR 1.28 (95% CI 1.06–1.53)
- Spine fracture: HR 1.51 (95% CI 0.93–2.45)

Among participants with TSH < 0.10 mIU/L, hip fracture risk climbed to HR 1.61 (95% CI 1.21–2.15), any-fracture risk to HR 1.98 (95% CI 1.41–2.78), and spine fracture risk to HR 3.57 (95% CI 1.88–6.78). No significant fracture excess was found with subclinical hypothyroidism. Fracture risk from sCHyper is most clinically relevant in postmenopausal women, in whom cortical bone loss may be additive to estrogen-deficiency remodeling.

## Determinants That Raise TSH

- **Autoimmune (Hashimoto's) thyroiditis:** the leading cause of primary hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient regions; progressive lymphocytic infiltration reduces functional thyroid mass, driving compensatory TSH rise.
- **Iodine deficiency or excess:** Iodine deficiency impairs thyroid hormone synthesis; paradoxically, iodine excess can precipitate hypothyroidism (Wolff-Chaikoff effect), particularly in individuals with pre-existing autoimmune thyroid disease.
- **Recovery from non-thyroidal illness (NTI) / sick euthyroid syndrome:** TSH may transiently rebound above the normal range during the recovery phase after acute illness, even when the steady-state is euthyroid.
- **Drugs:** Lithium inhibits thyroid hormone secretion and release; amiodarone (37% iodine by mass) causes hypothyroidism in 5–22% of recipients, particularly in iodine-sufficient regions; immune-checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1/PD-L1, anti-CTLA-4) trigger autoimmune thyroiditis in up to 15–20% of users; interferon-alpha induces thyroid autoimmunity.
- **Thyroid surgery / radioiodine ablation:** permanent reduction of thyroid mass leads to obligate post-treatment hypothyroidism in most patients.
- **Aging:** the population TSH distribution shifts upward with age; TSH levels of 5–8 mIU/L are common in healthy octogenarians without representing pathological SCH.
- **TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma (rare):** TSH inappropriately normal or elevated in the face of high thyroid hormones — a key diagnostic exception.

## Determinants That Lower TSH

- **Graves' disease / toxic nodule:** stimulatory TSH-receptor antibodies (in Graves') or autonomous thyroid hormone secretion suppress the pituitary TSH axis.
- **Central (secondary) hypothyroidism — the critical exception:** Damage to the hypothalamus or anterior pituitary (tumor, surgery, radiation, Sheehan syndrome) impairs TRH/TSH secretion. The result is a paradoxically low or inappropriately normal TSH paired with low fT4 — the opposite of what the TSH alone would suggest. This is the key pattern that TSH measurement alone will miss or misinterpret; it requires paired fT4 measurement for diagnosis.
- **Exogenous thyroid hormone over-replacement:** the most common cause of suppressed TSH in clinical practice; seen in patients on levothyroxine where the dose exceeds physiological replacement.
- **Glucocorticoids and dopamine/dobutamine:** suppress TSH secretion at the pituitary level — a well-documented phenomenon in hospitalized patients on vasopressors or high-dose steroids, where TSH may be low or undetectable without true hyperthyroidism.
- **First-trimester pregnancy:** placental hCG cross-reacts with the TSH receptor (structural homology), transiently suppressing maternal TSH in early pregnancy — typically nadir at 10–12 weeks; gestational reference ranges apply.
- **Severe non-thyroidal illness / sick euthyroid syndrome (acute phase):** TSH is suppressed during the acute phase of critical illness, then may rebound above the upper limit during recovery. Both the suppressed (acute) and elevated (recovery) values may occur in a single hospitalization.

## Key Limitations of TSH Measurement

**1. TSH alone misses central hypothyroidism.** In secondary/tertiary (central) hypothyroidism, the pituitary cannot mount an appropriate TSH response to falling thyroid hormones. A TSH in the "normal" or even low range paired with a low fT4 is the diagnostic footprint. Any evaluation of hypothyroidism that stops at TSH without fT4 will falsely reassure in this clinically important minority — who are often symptomatic and who need pituitary imaging and investigation, not reassurance.

**2. TSH lags acute thyroid changes by 4–6 weeks.** The pituitary thyrotrope population adapts to new steady-state thyroid hormone levels over a period of approximately 4–8 weeks. Remeasuring TSH within 4 weeks of a dose change yields a misleading value that reflects neither the prior nor the new steady state [2, mechanism_review]. The practical implication: after any levothyroxine dose adjustment, the earliest reliable re-check is at 6–8 weeks, and premature rechecks frequently trigger unnecessary further dose changes.

**3. Additional interferences** (cross-referenced to Section C) include heterophile antibodies causing falsely elevated or suppressed TSH on immunoassay; biotin supplementation (common in the wellness context) causing spuriously suppressed TSH on streptavidin-based assays; and within-individual diurnal and seasonal variation of approximately 50% around a personal set point — making single out-of-range values most interpretable in the context of a prior personal baseline.
